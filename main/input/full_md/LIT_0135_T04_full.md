# Sociological perspectives on AI ethics in higher education: a comparative study of student experiences in the UAE and Jordan

Fatima Ahmed Algharbawi <sup>a</sup>, Ayat J. Nashwan <sup>a,b</sup>, Wafa Barhoumi Hamdi <sup>a</sup>, Ahmad F. Klaib <sup>c,d</sup>, Noura Nasir Alkarbi <sup>a</sup> and Nawzat Abu alasal <sup>e</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Sociology, University of Sharjah, Sharjah, UAE; <sup>b</sup>Sociology and Social Work Department, Yarmouk University, Irbid, Jordan; <sup>c</sup>Department of Computer Engineering, Al Yamamah University, Al Khobar, Saudi Arabia; <sup>d</sup>Department of Information Systems, Yarmouk University, Irbid, Jordan; <sup>e</sup>Digital Media Department (Electronic) College of Arts and Humanities, Applied Science Private University, Amman, Jordan

## ABSTRACT

This cross-sectional study aims to examine the ethical values regulating the use of generative artificial intelligence (ChatGPT) among university students in the United Arab Emirates and Jordan. A descriptive–analytical survey of 1,000 students across multiple faculties revealed a high adoption rate of approximately 82% in both contexts, with no statistically significant diferences by university afiliation (odds ratio = 1.009, p = .955) or sex. Students primarily reported using ChatGPT for information retrieval, writing assistance, error detection, and summarization. Despite its frequent use, mean scores on ethical-value items indicate strong disagreement with substituting traditional instruction, uncritically trusting AI-generated content, and prioritizing AI over face-to-face interaction. Regression analysis further demonstrates that ethical values significantly predict responsible usage behaviors, suggesting that AI integration occurs within a framework of moral regulation rather than unrestrained technological dependence. Although usage patterns appear structurally similar across the two national contexts, variations in institutional policy environments highlight difering governance dynamics that may shape long-term regulatory approaches. The findings contribute to ongoing debates on AI in higher education by emphasizing not only the continued role of ethical norms in shaping student engagement but also the importance of culturally grounded governance frameworks that balance innovation with responsibility.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, the world has witnessed a profound technological revolution driven by the rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI). These advancements have enabled the creation of systems capable of simulating human intelligence and performing tasks previously considered uniquely human. From medical diagnostics and autonomous vehicles to intelligent tutoring systems and creative digital assistants, AI applications have become integral to modern societies, transforming the ways in which people live, work, and interact (Russell & Norvig, 2021).

AI has generated significant benefits, including improving eficiency in industrial production, supporting decision-making in healthcare, enhancing accessibility for individuals with disabilities, and assisting in military operations and crisis management (Haenlein & Kaplan, 2019). However, it has also raised complex ethical challenges, including concerns related to bias, privacy, accountability, transparency, and the broader societal implications of increasing reliance on machine intelligence (Jobin et al., 2019).

In the context of higher education, AI tools such as ChatGPT have opened new possibilities for learning, teaching, and research. Students and educators are increasingly adopting these systems for academic tasks, ranging from writing assistance and language learning to brainstorming and information retrieval (Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019). Although such technologies can enhance educational outcomes, they also raise important questions about academic integrity, fairness, dependency, and the role of human critical thinking.

International frameworks emphasize that educational AI should align with principles of human rights, transparency, and accountability, thereby promoting trustworthy innovation and context-sensitive governance (OECD, 2019; UNESCO, 2021, 2023). Recent policy developments in the European Union classify certain uses of AI in education as high-risk, requiring stricter oversight and institutional accountability. Meanwhile, regional initiatives, such as the United Arab Emirates (UAE) Ethical AI Toolkit, provide practical guidance for the responsible deployment and use of AI technologies (European Parliament, 2025; Smart Dubai, 2019).

These developments underscore the need for robust ethical frameworks to guide AI use in educational environments. Ethics in this context represents not only a set of principles guiding behavior but also a safeguard to ensure that technological innovation aligns with human values such as justice, accountability, and respect for knowledge (Floridi & Cowls, 2019). The absence of robust ethical and legal frameworks risks leaving the future development of AI unregulated and vulnerable to misuse.

Research conducted in the UAE and Jordan highlights a growing interest in educational AI, coupled with concerns regarding accuracy, integrity, and institutional readiness (Abdaljaleel et al., 2024; Abu Hammour et al., 2024; Swidan et al., 2025). Findings from these studies emphasize the urgent need for context-specific ethical frameworks and targeted capacity-building initiatives for both faculty and students. Against this backdrop, the present study ofers a comparative analysis of ethical values, usage motivations, and responsible use behaviors among university students in the UAE and Jordan, providing timely insights to inform evidence-based policy and practice.

The study adopts a descriptive–analytical design to: (a) estimate adoption rates and the frequency of ChatGPT use among students in both the UAE and Jordan; (b) analyze variations across diferent university contexts; and (c) examine cognitive, social and psychological, and practical motivations, along with ethical dimensions – such as credibility, integrity, bias awareness, confidentiality, critical thinking, and dependence – associated with responsible AI use in higher education (Floridi & Cowls, 2019; UNESCO, 2023). By aligning institutional policy discourse with students’ reported behaviors and values, the study aims to inform the development of practical guidelines for disclosure, assessment design, and AI literacy initiatives that promote a balanced integration of innovation and accountability within regional higher education systems (UNESCO, 2023; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019).

## 1.1. The dialectical relationship between education and artificial intelligence (AI)

The relationship between education and AI is inherently dialectical, characterized by both opportunities and challenges. On the one hand, AI ofers promising avenues for enhancing the learning process through personalized instruction, adaptive assessment, and real-time feedback. Tools such as ChatGPT enable students to summarize texts, generate ideas, and practice language skills, while also assisting instructors in managing administrative tasks and evaluating performance more eficiently. As Kasneci et al. (2023) note, AI has the potential to foster innovation, creativity, and inclusivity within higher education.

Recent research indicates that the use of ChatGPT is positively associated with enhanced cognitive skills, including thought organization, vocabulary retention, and analytical abilities, while also increasing motivation and engagement in writing tasks (Crompton & Burke, 2023). On the other hand, this integration raises critical concerns regarding ethics, academic integrity, and the risk of overreliance. Students who depend heavily on AI-generated outputs risk undermining their critical thinking, originality, and self-directed learning (Dwivedi et al., 2023). Emerging research raises concerns about cognitive ofloading and reduced deep processing when students rely excessively on generative AI tools (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Rudolph et al., 2023).

The absence of robust ethical frameworks complicates the regulation of AI in education, reinforcing tensions between its perceived benefits and potential harms (Dwivedi et al., 2023).

Resolving this tension requires institutions to establish clear ethical guidelines, pedagogical strategies, and regulatory frameworks to ensure the responsible use of AI. Rather than replacing educators or diminishing student agency, AI should be integrated as a complementary tool that enhances both teaching and learning. Zawacki-Richter et al. (2019) emphasize that the future of AI in education depends largely on universities’ ability to adopt holistic strategies that address both pedagogical opportunities and ethical challenges, allowing AI to serve as both a catalyst for educational transformation and a subject of critical reflection.

## 1.2. Global and policy frameworks

International organizations have provided guiding principles for ethical AI use. UNES-CO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (UNESCO, 2021) emphasizes human rights, transparency, and accountability, while the Organisation for Economic Co-Operation and Development’s AI Principles (OECD, 2019) promote trustworthy innovation aligned with democratic values. The European Union recently introduced the AI Act (European Parliament, 2025), which classifies educational AI systems as ‘high risk,’ requiring strict governance. In the UAE, Smart Dubai issued the Ethical AI Toolkit (Smart Dubai, 2019), ofering practical guidelines for responsible AI deployment in institutions. While the UAE National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence 2031 emphasizes building AI-ready talent and governance frameworks, Jordan’s Artificial Intelligence Strategy and Implementation Plan of 2023–2027 focuses on accelerating social and economic development through AI systems nationally, emphasizing education and capacity building (Ministry of Digital Economy and Entrepreneurship, 2023). The Arab League Educational, Cultural and Scientific Organization recently adopted the first AI ethical code to protect Arab values in higher education, establishing clear principles for responsible and equitable AI use (Floridi & Cowls, 2019; Jobin et al., 2019). These frameworks collectively establish an evolving regulatory landscape, transforming AI ethics from aspirational guidance to enforceable standards with culturally sensitive implementations.

## 1.3 Adoption and uses

Empirical studies indicate widespread student adoption of AI tools, with learners primarily employing ChatGPT for brainstorming, summarization, and writing support (Ravšelj et al., 2025; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019). In the UAE, research validating Technology Acceptance Model Edited (TAME)-ChatGPT factors of perceived usefulness, perceived risks, and social norms demonstrates high uptake, highlighting the necessity of explicit academic integrity policies (Sallam et al., 2024; Swidan et al., 2025). Similarly, in Jordan and broader Arab contexts, enthusiasm for AI coexists with concerns regarding accuracy, bias, and integrity, consistent with Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)-driven adoption patterns observed globally (Abu Hammour et al., 2024; Abdaljaleel et al., 2024).

## 1.4. Challenges of AI detection and assessment

A growing body of research has highlighted the challenges of relying on AI detection tools in academic settings. Liang et al. (2023) demonstrate that many Generative Pretrained Transformer (GPT)-detection systems exhibit bias, particularly against nonnative English writers, raising fairness and equity concerns. Perkins (2024) further reveals that such detectors can be easily bypassed with minor text modifications, undermining their reliability as safeguards for academic integrity. Institutions have been warned against over-reliance on detection software, with recommendations for a shift toward assessment redesign, encouraging approaches such as oral defenses, reflective portfolios, and transparent disclosure of AI use (UNESCO, 2023). False positive rates in AI-detection tools, despite often claimed to be low by companies, can have serious academic consequences for accused students.

Research further reveals that AI detection systems disproportionately flag content produced by non-native English speakers or students with distinctive writing styles, potentially tarnishing reputations based on flawed detection results (Giray, 2024). Recent research highlights significant challenges in enforcing AI disclosure policies, with Gonsalves (2024) finding that 74% of students at King’s Business School failed to declare AI use despite mandatory requirements. Using the Theory of Planned Behavior, the study revealed barriers such as fear of repercussions, unclear guidelines, inconsistent enforcement, and peer influence, underscoring the gap between policy design and actual student compliance.

To address these challenges, scholars and institutions are increasingly advocating for ethical and pedagogical shifts. Floridi and Cowls (2019) provided a conceptual foundation, proposing five universal AI ethics principles – beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice, and explicability – to guide universities in developing ethical assessment practices that prioritize fairness and accountability over punitive measures. In line with this framework, universities are adopting innovative strategies such as portfolio-based evaluations, process-oriented assessments, and AI disclosure forms to promote transparency and ethical AI use (University of Waterloo, 2024). Collectively, these studies emphasize that ensuring academic integrity in the age of AI requires rethinking assessment design, prioritizing fairness, transparency, and student engagement over exclusive dependence on detection tools.

## 1.5. Research gaps and contribution of the current study

Despite the growing body of literature on the integration of AI, specifically ChatGPT, in higher education, several important gaps remain. First, most existing studies have been conducted in Western contexts (Crompton & Burke, 2023; Dwivedi et al., 2023; Kasneci et al., 2023), with limited empirical evidence from Arab countries. Although UNESCO (2021), OECD (2019), and the EU AI Act (2025) outline global ethical standards, few studies investigate how these principles translate into culturally specific higher education settings such as the UAE and Jordan. This creates a gap in understanding how local values, traditions, and institutional policies shape students’ perceptions of ethical AI use.

Second, although AI adoption studies in the region (e.g. Abu Hammour et al., 2024; Sallam et al., 2024; Swidan et al., 2025) have validated factors related to usefulness, risks, and social norms, they rarely examine ethical values directly as a distinct construct. Instead, ethics is often treated implicitly, as a byproduct of academic integrity concerns or technical detection challenges, rather than being studied systematically.

Third, prior research has focused on policy frameworks or the technological reliability of detection tools (Giray, 2024; Liang et al., 2023; Perkins, 2024), with few studies examining large-scale empirical evidence on how students themselves perceive and embody ethical responsibilities in their everyday use of generative AI. This neglects the lived experiences and practical dilemmas of the main stakeholders: university students.

Therefore, the present study seeks to fill these research gaps in several ways: It is one of the first large-scale cross-sectional investigations (n = 1,000) in the Arab region that empirically explores the ethical values governing ChatGPT use in higher education. By focusing on both the UAE and Jordan, it provides a comparative perspective between two countries that share cultural similarities but have diferent AI strategies and regulatory frameworks. Unlike earlier works that emphasize detection or adoption, this study centers explicitly on the ethical dimension, ofering insights into student attitudes, behaviors, and perceived responsibilities.

Finally, by integrating quantitative findings with policy frameworks, this study advances the discourse from descriptive adoption patterns toward a sociologically grounded understanding of ethics in AI use, thereby providing practical implications for universities and policymakers in the Arab world to design culturally sensitive guidelines and pedagogical strategies that balance innovation with responsibility.

## 1.6. Ethical considerations and context-specific insights

Several global and regional studies have addressed the ethical challenges of AI integration in higher education. UNESCO’s (2023) Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research provides a comprehensive framework for policymakers and universities, emphasizing transparency, capacity-building, and ethical alignment with human values.

Zawacki-Richter et al.’s (2019) systematic review concluded that while AI applications in education are expanding rapidly, ethical dimensions remain underexplored. Holmes et al. (2021) reinforced this gap, calling for stronger ethical safeguards in AI-based educational practices. More recently, Kasneci et al. (2023) examined the educational potential of ChatGPT, emphasizing both opportunities for personalized learning and risks of plagiarism, fairness, and over-reliance on automated systems. In a large-scale global study, Ravšelj et al. (2025) surveyed more than 23,000 students across 109 countries, reporting that they primarily use ChatGPT for brainstorming and summarization but also highlighting concerns regarding academic integrity. These findings collectively underscore the global consensus that ethical frameworks are essential to guide AI adoption in higher education.

In the Arab region, recent studies have provided more localized insights. Sallam et al. (2024) validated the TAME-ChatGPT model in UAE universities, demonstrating that adoption is shaped by perceived usefulness, ease of use, and perceived risks. Swidan et al. (2025) reported that nearly 80% of students used AI tools, with persistent concerns regarding academic honesty. In Jordan, Abu Hammour et al. (2024) studied Jordanian medical students and found a balance between enthusiasm for ChatGPT and concerns about accuracy, bias, and integrity. Abdaljaleel et al. (2024) examined 2,240 students across Arab countries including Jordan, reporting that attitudes depend on perceived utility and risks, confirming the cultural relevance of TAM-based models, while Al-Hamlan and Al-Said (2023) highlighted the fact that educators across Arab universities lack suficient training on ethical AI use. Recent investigations reveal that faculty perceptions of academic integrity policies addressing traditional and generative AI plagiarism show significant disparities, as both are rated only as moderately efective (Alsharefeen & Al Sayari, 2025). Faculties demonstrate preferences for educative over punitive approaches while identifying workload constraints, insuficient institutional support, detection challenges, cultural leniency, and systemic limitations as barriers to policy enforcement (Figure 1).

## 1.8. Synthesis and research gap

The literature highlights a nuanced relationship between AI and higher education: While AI enhances learning through personalization, adaptive assessment, and eficiency, it also poses ethical, cognitive, and equity challenges. Policy frameworks (UNESCO, OECD, EU AI Act) emphasize transparency, accountability, and human oversight. Empirical studies demonstrate widespread adoption, including in the UAE and Jordan, driven by perceived usefulness of AI but moderated by concerns over its accuracy, bias, and integrity.

Despite these insights, gaps remain. Most studies address adoption or ethics separately, with limited integration of ethical frameworks into observed behaviors, thereby highlighting the urgent need for context-specific ethical guidelines that balance innovation with accountability in the UAE and Jordan. These studies also emphasize that ensuring academic integrity in the AI era requires rethinking assessment methods focusing on ethical frameworks, transparency, and pedagogical redesigns rather than relying solely on detection technologies. Region-specific evidence on responsible use behaviors, particularly in Middle Eastern educational contexts, is sparse. Furthermore, regulatory and pedagogical guidance often lacks empirical grounding in student practices, resulting in the limited exploration of the interplay between ethical principles, technology acceptance, and actual responsible use behaviors. To address these gaps, the present study links TAM/TAME constructs with ethical dimensions to examine both adoption patterns and measurable responsible use behaviors, providing evidence to inform policy, pedagogy, and AI governance in higher education. The study’s comparison of the UAE with Jordan contributes to understanding how diferent institutional infrastructures and policy environments influence ethical AI use in higher education.

## 1.9. Theoretical framing: universities as moral-regulatory institutions

Building on the identified research gaps, this study adopts a sociological framework that situates AI adoption within broader processes of institutional moral regulation. Although TAM/TAME explain behavioral adoption through perceived usefulness, ease of use, and social influence, they ofer limited insight into how ethical norms are collectively structured and sustained within educational environments. To address this limitation, this study draws on Durkheim’s concept of moral regulation, conceptualizing institutions as sites where shared norms and boundaries shape acceptable conduct.

From a Durkheimian perspective, universities are not only knowledge-producing organizations but also moral-regulatory institutions that define legitimate academic practices through rules, codes of integrity, assessment norms, and shared expectations. The difusion of generative AI tools such as ChatGPT, therefore, represents both a technological innovation and a transformation in the normative structure of academic life. Patterns of responsible uses such as disclosure, verification, and modification – can be interpreted as expressions of moral integration, while overreliance or uncritical acceptance may reflect weakening normative coordination.

Anchoring the study in this framework allows ethical-value dimensions (credibility, integrity, bias awareness, confidentiality, critical thinking, and dependence) to be examined as not only individual attitudes but also indicators of how institutional contexts mediate AI engagement. Comparing the UAE with Jordan further enables the analysis of how variations in infrastructure, policy clarity, and institutional regulation influence the density of moral norms surrounding AI use.

Guided by this integrated framework – linking technology acceptance constructs with Durkheimian moral regulation – this study proceeds to examine adoption patterns, ethical-value dimensions, and responsible use behaviors among university students in the two countries.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Research questions

1. What is the prevalence and frequency of ChatGPT use among university students in the UAE and Jordan?

2. How do adoption rates and usage frequency vary by university (UAE vs. Jordan), sex, and age?

3. To what extent do ethical values predict responsible use behaviors of ChatGPT?

4. How does the duration of ChatGPT use difer across demographic groups and institutional contexts?

## 2.2. Hypotheses

. H1: No significant diference exists in adoption rates between students in the UAE and Jordan.

. H2: No significant diferences exist in adoption rates by sex.

. H3: Age has a limited efect on adoption rates but may influence usage frequency and duration.

H3a: Age has a limited efect on adoption.

H3b: Age has a limited-to-weak efect on frequency and duration.

. H4: Ethical values significantly predict responsible use behaviors of ChatGPT among university students.

## 2.3. Research design

A cross-sectional descriptive–analytical survey design was employed to examine patterns of ChatGPT use and the ethical values regulating AI engagement among university students in the UAE and Jordan. This design allows for both descriptive assessment of usage trends and inferential examination of relationships between ethical orientations and responsible AI behaviors.

## 2.4. Population and sample

The study targeted students enrolled at the University of Sharjah (UAE), Yarmouk University (Jordan), and Princess Sumaya University for Technology (Jordan). A two-stage cluster sampling procedure was implemented. Lecture sections across faculties (humanities, STEM, business, health sciences) were randomly selected, proportional to enrollment. All consenting students within the selected sections were invited to participate. Inclusion criteria included students aged 18 years or above who were currently enrolled. Exclusion criteria were students with audit status and those who failed to provide consent to participate. The final sample included 1,000 students, providing adequate statistical power for multivariate analysis.

## 2.5. Data collection tool

A structured questionnaire was developed based on validated instruments and prior empirical research on AI use in higher education (e.g. Sallam et al., 2024; Swidan et al., 2025). The instrument included items on:

1. Demographics: age, sex, academic level, field of study.

2. Adoption: binary measure (Yes/No).

3. Frequency of Use: ordinal categories (daily, weekly, monthly, several times per year).

4. Duration of Use: continuous measure (typical session length).

5. Ethical Values Index: eight Likert-scale items capturing students’ normative orientations toward AI use, including integrity, credibility, confidentiality, bias awareness, critical engagement, and responsible authorship.

All Likert-scale items were coded as 1 = Strongly Disagree and 5 = Strongly Agree. No reverse-coded items were used in computing composite indices. Higher mean scores reflect stronger agreement with the measured construct. Composite scores were calculated by averaging the eight items.

These ethical dimensions, rather than being considered as separate predictors, were conceptually integrated into a unified Ethical Values Index, reflecting the overall moral-regulatory framework guiding AI engagement. Internal consistency analysis demonstrated strong reliability: Cronbach’s α = .895 Table 2.

The questionnaire items were not adopted from a single pre-validated scale; rather, they were developed based on established ethical and AI governance frameworks. As shown in Table 3, the Responsible Use composite reflects the integration of ethical orientations, behavioral standards, and institutional controls.

This near-identical adoption rate suggests that university afiliation is not a statistically significant determinant in the likelihood of AI usage. Instead, the findings reflect a broader trend: the growing recognition of AI as a valuable tool in higher education.

Recent studies have highlighted similar patterns across universities worldwide, with AI integration in learning and academic practices becoming increasingly normalized. ## 4. Discussion: moral regulation and responsible AI integration in higher education

Adopting a Durkheimian perspective situates the adoption of generative AI not simply as a technological transformation, but as a shift in the moral regulation of academic life. Durkheim conceptualized moral currents as collective normative forces that structure individual conduct through shared values, institutional rules, and patterns of social integration (Durkheim, 1893/2014, 1897/1951). Universities, in this sense, function as moralregulatory institutions that define legitimate academic practices and sustain collective standards of integrity.

Within this framework, the widespread and routinized use of ChatGPT observed among students can be interpreted as the emergence of a new digital moral current within higher education. When institutional frameworks – such as academic integrity codes, clear policy guidelines, and structured pedagogical guidance – are clearly articulated, the integration of AI tools becomes embedded within a regulated moral order. Under such conditions, technological adoption may reinforce rather than undermine collective academic norms.

Conversely, when technological engagement expands more rapidly than regulatory and pedagogical structures, risks of normative instability may arise. Patterns identified in the findings – including ethical ambivalence, uneven institutional guidance, and primarily pragmatic engagement with AI tools – echo what Durkheim described as anomie: a weakening of normative boundaries that guide individual behavior. The challenge for universities, therefore, is not the presence of AI itself, but the capacity of institutions to maintain alignment between technological innovation and moral governance.

The comparative findings between the UAE and Jordan further support this interpretation. Diferences observed in patterns of engagement appear to reflect variations in institutional environments rather than inherent diferences in student values. Factors such as digital infrastructure, policy clarity, and faculty guidance shape the visibility and strength of collective norms surrounding AI use. In this sense, AI governance should be understood as a process of moral integration, whereby universities reinforce shared academic standards within technologically mediated learning environments.

The empirical results also indicate that ethical orientations significantly predict responsible AI use behaviors. This suggests that students’ engagement with generative AI is not driven solely by convenience or technological eficiency but is moderated by normative beliefs about academic responsibility and integrity. These findings challenge deterministic narratives that portray AI adoption as inherently disruptive to educational values. Instead, they reveal a pattern of selective and morally bounded integration within higher education contexts.

From a policy perspective, the findings suggest that responsible AI integration requires a balanced approach combining institutional regulation and ethical awareness. Universities may strengthen responsible AI practices by developing clear guidelines for AI use, integrating digital ethics into curricula, and encouraging transparency in the use of generative AI tools. Such strategies support the development of an academic environment where technological innovation remains aligned with shared educational values.

## 4.1. Policy and educational implications

## Transparent AI-Use Disclosure Practices

Based on the observed patterns of AI use and ethical orientations, universities may consider introducing transparent AI-use disclosure practices in academic assignments. Encouraging students to clearly indicate how generative AI tools were used can promote academic honesty while supporting responsible engagement with emerging technologies.

## Integration of AI Engagement into Assessment Design

Because a substantial proportion of students indicated limited modification of AI-generated responses, assessment rubrics could incorporate criteria that reward evidence of critical engagement. For example, requiring students to annotate AI-generated content or provide reflective commentary on revisions could help reinforce active learning and reduce passive dependence on automated outputs.

## Bias and Source-Critique Training

Given the low mean score for bias awareness (M = 1.45), structured instructional activities focusing on evaluating AI-generated content may be beneficial. Classroom exercises that involve comparing ChatGPT outputs with peer-reviewed sources can enhance students’ ability to identify inaccuracies, omissions, or implicit biases, thereby strengthening their critical literacy skills.

## Critical Thinking Reinforcement

With relatively low mean scores for independent critical engagement (M = 1.52), embedding structured AI-critique activities within coursework may support deeper analytical reasoning. Periodic assignments requiring students to evaluate, challenge, or improve AI-generated responses could foster reflective and autonomous learning practices.

## AI Ethics and Integrity Modules

Given the predictive relationship between ethical values and responsible AI use behaviors identified in the regression analysis, integrating short AI ethics modules within university curricula may reinforce constructive engagement patterns.

## Context-Sensitive Governance Approaches

Although adoption rates were statistically similar across the UAE and Jordan, institutional policy environments may shape how AI governance evolves over time. Universities should therefore develop context-sensitive regulatory frameworks that balance innovation with accountability.

## 4.2. Limitations and future research

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the research relies on self-reported survey data, which may be influenced by social desirability bias or diferences in respondents’ interpretations of AI use practices. Future studies could complement survey methods with qualitative approaches such as interviews or classroom observations to capture deeper insights into students’ actual AI use behaviors.

Second, the study focuses on two higher education contexts – the UAE and Jordan – which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other cultural or institutional environments. Future research could extend the comparative scope by including universities from additional regions or educational systems to explore broader patterns of AI adoption and governance.

Third, the study examines ethical orientations and adoption patterns at a particular moment in time. Given the rapid evolution of generative AI technologies and institutional policies, longitudinal research would be valuable to examine how ethical norms, regulatory frameworks, and patterns of AI use evolve over time.

Overall, future research should continue to investigate the dynamic relationship between technological innovation, institutional governance, and moral regulation within higher education. Such work will be essential for understanding how universities can sustain academic integrity while adapting to the transformative potential of generative AI.

## 5. Conclusion

This study examined patterns of ChatGPT adoption and ethical orientations among university students in the UAE and Jordan. The findings demonstrate that while generative

AI has become widely integrated into academic routines, its use remains moderated by ethical considerations and institutional norms. Interpreted through a Durkheimian perspective, AI adoption reflects a broader transformation in the moral regulation of academic life rather than a simple technological shift. Strengthening institutional guidance, ethical awareness, and critical engagement will be essential for ensuring that generative AI contribute positively to higher education while maintaining academic integrity.
