# ChatGPT and academic integrity: the role of personal best goals, academic competence, and workplace stress

Saeed Awadh Bin-Nashwan, Mouad Sadallah, Abderrahim Benlahcene and Muhammad M. Ma’aji

## Abstract

Purpose – The research aims to unravel the dynamics of academic integrity in the ChatGPT era by analyzing critical predictors such as personal best goals (PBG), academic competence and workplace stress. Furthermore, it examines how ChatGPT adoption acts as a moderating factor, potentially influencing the relationship between these predictors and academic integrity, offering a nuanced understanding ofits impactin academic settings.

Design/methodology/approach – Relying on the social cognitive theory, the authors adopt a quantitative approach through an online survey to explore the impact ofkey variables – PBG, academic competence and workplace stress, as well as ChatGPT adoption on integrity – by analyzing data from responses collectedthrough Academic SocialNetworking Sites.

Findings – The study found that PBG related positively to academic integrity among academic staff. However, workplace stress had a negative impact on academic integrity, while academic competence failed to report any effect. With the integration of ChatGPTadoption as a moderator into the model, the authors foundthe association between PBG andacademic integrityaltered to be negative. The ChatGPT adoption-moderated interactions ofacademic competence and workplace stress on academic integrity weresignificant.

Practical implications – The findings suggest that institutions should provide training on the ethical and effective use of artificial intelligence (AI) tools like ChatGPT to ensure they support rather than hinder academic integrity. Additionally, organizations should focus on stress management initiatives and fostering a balanced approach to personal goal setting to mitigate the potential negative impacts of ChatGPTadoption on ethicalbehavior.

Originality/value – This attempt is both timely and pioneering, addressing a clear gap in the current body ofliterature. As limited studies have examined the role ofChatGPTin academic settings, this work stands out as one ofthe earliest to investigate how educators and researchers incorporate OpenAI tools into theirprofessionalpractices.

Keywords ChatGPT, Personalbestgoals, Academic integrity, Workplace stress, ArtificialIntelligence Paper type Research paper

## 2. Theoretical framework

## 2.1 Generative artificial intelligence

Generative AI represents a significant technological breakthrough that is reshaping both industry and academia. Unlike traditional AI systems, generative AI is capable of producing new content – such as text, images, music and other media – by learning patterns from vast data sets. Tools like OpenAI’s ChatGPT exemplify this category of AI, which is now transforming processes in a variety of industries, including education, healthcare, entertainment and manufacturing. In the context of Industry 4.0 and 5.0, generative AI enhances automation, fosters innovation and promotes human-machine collaboration.

These advancements contribute to more agile and efficient systems, helping businesses to remain competitive in an increasingly digital world. Furthermore, as we move toward Society 5.0, generative AI plays a pivotal role in creating more personalized, intelligent solutions across sectors.

In academia, the adoption of generative AI tools like ChatGPT is revolutionizing how scholars approach knowledge creation, research and teaching. However, this transformation also introduces new challenges, particularly concerning academic integrity. Traditional academic practices, such as originality in research and ethical use of sources, face scrutiny as AI-generated content becomes more prevalent. The core issue lies in how these tools influence academic behaviors, integrity and performance standards. To better understand these dynamics, this study builds on the foundations of generative AI in the industry, while addressing its growing impact on academic institutions. The study “ChatGPT and similar generative artificial intelligence (AI) for smart industry: Function, challenges, and opportunities for Industry 4.0, Industry 5.0, and Society 5.0” by Rane (2023) offers an important reference in exploring the capabilities of generative AI. Rane (2023) detailed how AI technologies, including generative models, are leveraged for innovation in smart industries, improving efficiency, creativity and decision-making. The study also highlighted the ethical challenges associated with generative AI, such as biases in algorithms, transparency and the implications of AI-generated content. These challenges mirror similar issues faced in academia, where the line between human-generated and AI-generated work is becoming increasingly blurred.

Drawing on this and related works, such as Dwivedi et al. (2023) and Cotton et al. (2024), this study situates generative AI within a broader theoretical context, linking its adoption in academia to evolving expectations for ethical behavior, competence and personal performance goals. To further strengthen the theoretical framework of this study, we integrate key concepts from social cognitive theory (SCT), which has been widely used to explore human behaviors in educational settings. SCT emphasizes the interaction between personal factors, behavior and environmental influences. Within the context of academic integrity and AI adoption, generative AI tools like ChatGPT influence these three components. For example, they affect academic self-efficacy, as students and faculty use these tools to augment their research and writing, potentially enhancing their perceived competence. However, this technological shift also poses risks to academic integrity, as the ease of generating content can undermine traditional academic values of originality and authorship.

## 2.2 Socialcognitive theory

SCT, a prominent psychological framework, emphasizes the critical role of the social environment in shaping human behavior, particularly in areas like motivation, learning and self-regulation (Bin-Nashwan and Muneeza, 2023). Widely applied across psychological research, SCT has become increasingly relevant to the study of academic integrity (Schunk and DiBenedetto, 2020). This discussion is grounded in Bandura’s SCT (Bandura, 1986, 1997, 2001), narrowing its focus to explore the connections between this theoretical framework and academic integrity. According to SCT, human motivation and behavior are strongly influenced by learning processes, with forethought – a mechanism of anticipatory control – serving as a cornerstone. This allows individuals to anticipate the consequences of their actions, including those involving ethical decision-making. Several core factors within SCT contribute to understanding behavior (Luszczynska and Schwarzer, 2005).

A key component is perceived self-efficacy, which reflects a person’s confidence in their ability to execute the actions needed to achieve specific outcomes while maintaining ethical standards. Bandura (1986) highlights competence as an essential element of SCT (Bembenutty et al., 2016). The theory also underscores the role of personal beliefs, selfperceptions and goal-setting in influencing ethical behavior, while simultaneously recognizing how environmental factors shape decisions and actions (Bandura, 1989). For instance, individuals may face ethical dilemmas or opportunities for misconduct influenced by their social environment, such as observing peers’ behaviors or engaging with innovative technologies (Ratten and Ratten, 2007).

In today’s academic landscape, emerging technologies like ChatGPT represent a significant environmental factor (Bin-Nashwan et al., 2023a). While such tools hold great potential for fostering ethical practices, they also present risks of misuse, emphasizing the necessity of developing strong self-regulation and ethical foresight among users. SCT suggests that setting PBG can enhance academic integrity, as these goals encourage individuals to pursue higher ethical standards (Burns et al., 2017, 2018). Additionally, intrinsic factors such as perceived stress significantly affect ethical decision-making. Yin et al. (2020) demonstrated that stress can undermine self-regulation, potentially leading to ethical lapses in high-pressure scenarios. Similarly, Resurreccion (2012) explored the interplay between academic integrity and intrinsic motivation, highlighting how internal beliefs influence ethical behavior. Domenech-Betoret et al. (2017) also identified competence as a critical factor that supports self-regulation and ethical conduct within the SCT framework.

## 2.3 Research model and hypotheses

Drawn on SCT, the selection of each variable was strategically made to address key factors influencing academic integrity in the context of ChatGPT adoption. PBG were included as they represent individual motivations for achieving academic excellence, which can impact the ethical use of AI tools. Academic competence was chosen to assess how the perceived skill and expertise of faculty and researchers influence their ability to navigate AI tools ethically, aligning with academic integrity standards. Workplace stress was incorporated as it can affect decision-making and ethical behavior, potentially leading to shortcuts or unethical practices when using AI-driven tools. Finally, ChatGPT adoption serves as the central variable, reflecting the extent to which AI tools are used and their moderating effects on the relationships between personal goals, competence and workplace stress. These variables collectively provide a comprehensive understanding of the dynamics at play in the AI-driven academic environment.

2.3.1 Academic integrity. Academic integrity is described as “the values, behaviour, and conduct of academics in all aspects of their practice” (Macfarlane et al., 2014). Upholding academic and educational integrity requires a strong commitment to truthfulness, fairness, impartiality, respect and responsibility (Holden et al., 2021). It involves conducting research and academic activities in an ethical, consistent and unbiased manner (Guerrero-Dib et al., 2020; Ramdani, 2018). Studies suggest that students often resort to cheating during exams due to the pressure to achieve higher grades. Conversely, academically successful students are generally less inclined to engage in dishonest practices (Lee et al., 2020). Additionally, research shows that students with negative attitudes toward cheating tend to achieve better grades, further highlighting the importance of fostering academic integrity, especially in the context of advanced AI technologies (Vendehey et al., 2007). The relationship between academic integrity and academic performance is widely supported in the literature. Bin-Nashwan et al. (2023a) found that academic integrity negatively impacts the adoption of ChatGPT by academicians, underscoring concerns about ethical AI usage. Furthermore, students with a track record of academic success are more likely to trust their ability to succeed, reducing their reliance on dishonest practices (Lee et al., 2020). Similarly, Elsalem et al. (2021) demonstrated a significant link between academic integrity and the effort and time invested in exam preparation. Academic integrity also plays a critical role in career readiness. Sotiriadou et al. (2020) revealed that promoting academic integrity improves the employability of graduates. However, despite these findings, there remains a gap in understanding the factors shaping academic integrity among academic staff and the key drivers of this essential practice. This study seeks to address this gap by examining the influences on academic integrity in the evolving landscape of the ChatGPT era.

2.3.2 Personal best goals. PBG are defined as challenging, specific, self-improvementfocused and competitively self-referenced objectives that encourage individuals to surpass their previous achievements (Martin and Elliot, 2016). These goals uniquely contribute to explaining variance in academic outcomes, highlighting their critical role in motivation and educational engagement (Benlahcene, 2022; Martin et al., 2022). In a longitudinal crosslagged panel study conducted in Australia, PBG were shown to influence high school students’ implicit beliefs and intelligence traits (Martin, 2015). Furthermore, research comparing PBG with traditional goal types, such as mastery and performance goals, found that PBG significantly enhanced motivation and engagement among Chinese and Australian elementary and secondary school students (Martin and Elliot, 2016). A study involving middle and secondary school students in Australia revealed that students in a PBG-focused setting achieved greater academic growth compared to those in a control group (Martin and Elliot, 2016). Additional research reinforces the positive impact of PBG in higher education contexts. For example, Ramshe et al. (2019) demonstrated a significant positive relationship between PBG and various dimensions of learning engagement – behavioral, emotional and cognitive – among 302 university students. Similarly, Khajavy and Aghaee (2022) found that PBG significantly influenced students’ academic performance. Burns et al. (2018) also provided evidence that PBG significantly predicted both student engagement and academic integrity, supporting the integration of PBG into models like the SCT. Recently, McNulty (2023) emphasized the need for further research to fully understand PBG’s effects on behavioral improvements in academic practices. Based on this evidence, it is reasonable to assume that PBG are strongly linked to academic integrity, particularly in the context of the ChatGPT era:

## H1. Personal best goals relate to academic integrity in the ChatGPT era.

2.3.3 Academic competence. In academia, competence encompasses a blend of knowledge, attitudes and skills that enable individuals to effectively perform tasks and solve problems (Wiek et al., 2011). Instructors play a crucial role in fostering students’ sense of competence by promoting honest communication, providing clear and consistent guidelines and cultivating meaningful interactions between students and faculty (Mun˜oz and Ramirez, 2015). Bandura’s (1986) concept of self-efficacy underscores the importance of perceived competence in influencing behavior (Teoh et al., 2020). Research has highlighted the significant role of competence in shaping academic practices. For example, Liu et al. (2015) conducted a study involving 462 undergraduate students in Hong Kong and demonstrated that competence positively impacts ethical academic activities. Similarly, Panayiotou et al. (2019) found in their experiment with 1,626 students in England that competence significantly affects academic achievement. Manwa et al. (2020) also reported that competence influences the academic practices of female university students in Zimbabwe. However, a systematic review by Luesia et al. (2023) revealed limited evidence regarding the relationship between competence and academic integrity. This gap in the literature highlights the need for further research to explore the connection between competence and ethical academic behaviors, such as integrity, to better understand this critical relationship:

## H2. Academic competence relates to academic integrity in the ChatGPT era.

2.3.4 Workplace stress. Workplace stress, as defined in our research, refers to an individual’s subjective experience of pressure or tension over a specific time frame (Fisher, 2015; Cohen et al., 1983). This stress emerges when individuals face persistent inconveniences, challenges or issues that substantially impact critical aspects of their professional or personal lives. Recent research highlights the adverse effects of stress on mental health, particularly within the context of online learning (Heo and Han, 2018). The abrupt shift to online education during the COVID-19 pandemic has been identified as a significant stressor, exacerbating stress levels among students (Mheidly et al., 2020). Findings in the literature present mixed results regarding the relationship between stress and academic outcomes. For instance, a study involving 214 psychology students in Saudi Arabia found no correlation between perceived stress and academic achievement (Mohsen, 2017), while Tus (2020) reported similar outcomes. Interestingly, perceived stress was also associated with an increase in academic practices – both positive and negative – during the pandemic. However, other studies, such as that of Gustems-Carnicer et al. (2019), identified a significant negative relationship between high levels of stress and academic performance. Building on these insights, this study posits that workplace stress negatively impacts academic integrity by impairing self-regulation and ethical decisionmaking. Elevated stress levels may lead individuals to prioritize short-term relief or outcomes over long-term values, thereby increasing the likelihood of unethical behavior. Stress can further weaken cognitive functioning, reducing the capacity to evaluate ethica considerations effectively. Moreover, it may create a sense of urgency or desperation to meet demands, fostering an environment where academic misconduct becomes more appealing:

H3. Workplace stress relates to academic integrity in the ChatGPT era.

2.3.5 ChatGPTadoption. In education, AI adoption has emerged as a consequential tool in individuals’ achievement and academic practices, garnering substantial attention within the academic community (Hisan and Amri, 2023; Lo, 2023). This technology demonstrates the capacity to swiftly create text that closely resembles human language in a conversational approach using machine learning techniques. Its remarkable versatility extends to handling diverse language prompts, including translating text, answering questions, summarising information and generating new textual content (Cotton et al., 2024). However, using ChatGPT in academia also poses substantial challenges, including the risk of plagiarism, academic dishonesty and AI-assisted fraud (Shiri, 2023; Cotton et al., 2024; Lo, 2023; Alser and Waisberg, 2023). Also, it questions the extent to which educators develop necessary skills and gain from academic challenges that should ameliorate their readiness for future endeavors. As a result, numerous universities globally have opted to restrict access to ChatGPT on their campuses (Tlili et al., 2023), while others remain cautious due to concerns about disseminating unreliable, unethical or plagiarised content.

The growing intellectual discourse surrounding ChatGPT’s impact on academia and higher education highlights its contentious role in shaping academic practices, including ethics and integrity (Lo, 2023; Cotton et al., 2024; Alser and Waisberg, 2023; Farrokhnia et al., 2023; Lund and Wang, 2023; Rahman et al., 2023; Dowling and Lucey, 2023; Dwivedi et al., 2023; Jimenez, 2023). While some researchers, such as Malinka et al. (2023), have argued that ChatGPT can significantly enhance academic practices, they have warned that its current use – absent adequate and responsible user training – may undermine academic quality. This divergence in findings underscores the need for further investigation into the impact of ChatGPT adoption on ethical academic practices. Moreover, there is a notable gap in the literature concerning ChatGPT’s moderating role in the relationship between academic integrity and its predictors. To address this gap and advance understanding in this area, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

H4. ChatGPT adoption relates to academic integrity in the ChatGPT era.

H4a. ChatGPT adoption moderates the relationship between personal best goals and academic integrity in the ChatGPT era.

H4b. ChatGPT adoption moderates the relationship between competence and academic integrity in the ChatGPT era.

H4c. ChatGPT adoption moderates the relationship between perceived stress and academic integrity in the ChatGPT era.

## 3. Research methods

In this attempt, the objective was to provide a comprehensive understanding of trends and relationships by manipulating a limited number of variables to explore theory-driven research questions and hypotheses (Bin-Nashwan et al., 2023b). A cross-sectional research design was used to validate the academic integrity model through ChatGPT adoption, with a web-based questionnaire distributed to the global academic community. The online survey was chosen for its efficiency, cost-effectiveness and accessibility to the target population (Wright, 2005), which aligns with the technological nature of this study. The respondents were academic scholars worldwide, particularly those active on Academia.edu and ResearchGate, with at least two identifiable research publications on their profiles. These platforms are among the most widely used academic social networking sites (ASNSs) worldwide (Bin-Nashwan et al., 2023a, 2023b), with ResearchGate boasting 20 million users and Academia.edu reaching 170 million users in 2021 (ResearchGate, 2021; Academia.edu, 2021). For data collection, survey invitations containing URL links were randomly sent to respondents on RG and Academia.

Prior to distributing the survey, we sought input from three academics and conducted a pilot test with a group of 20 researchers. Based on their feedback, several refinements were made to enhance the validity and reliability of our proposed model (Abdul-Jabbar and Bin-Nashwan, 2022). To minimize potential social desirability bias, we implemented a series of careful measures, including ensuring the confidentiality of responses, conducting an extensive pilot study and adopting a web-based survey design (Dodou and de Winter, 2014). The sample revealed a notable gender disparity among the academic staff and researchers, with 68.2% identifying as male and 31.8% as female. Regarding age, 39.6% of the respondents were between the ages of 31 and 40, while 30.6% were aged between 41 and 50. When it came to academic roles, 26.6% were Senior Lecturers/Assistant Professors, 24.4% were Instructors/Lecturers, 20% were Postdoctoral Researchers/Fellows/Scholars, 17% were Associate Professors and 12% held the title of Professor. Furthermore, participants’ academic experience varied, with 32.6% reporting 1–5 years of experience, and 28.1% having 11–15 years of experience in academia. These insights provide a comprehensive understanding of the academic backgrounds and demographic distribution of the study sample, helping to contextualize the results.

To ensure the validity of the study model, it was essential to assess the potential impact of common method bias (CMB, Bin-Nashwan et al., 2022). Therefore, Harman’s single-factor analysis was conducted to determine whether a single construct could explain a substantia portion of the variance in the proposed model. The results showed that the largest single factor explained only 27.50% of the variance, which is well below the recommended threshold of 50%, as suggested by Podsakoff et al. (2003). This indicates that CMB was not a significant issue, confirming the validity of the study’s findings. Additionally, a thorough collinearity test was conducted to examine potential collinearity problems within the model. The variance inflation factor values were all below the critical threshold of 3.3 (Bin-Nashwan, 2022), which further supports the model’s validity and the reliability of the results.

We relied upon an online survey for assessing five variables: PBG, academic competence, workplace stress, ChatGPT adoption and academic integrity in the ChatGPT era. Each measurement item was derived from well-established instruments used in prior research. These items have been carefully modified to match the specific context of academic performance in the ChatGPT era. PBG were measured using three questions adapted from Martin (2006), while competence was evaluated using five items adapted from Chen et al. (2015). Six items drawn from Cohen et al. (1983) were used to measure stress, and five items derived from Katz et al. (1973) and Papacharissi and Rubin (2000) were used to evaluate ChatGPT adoption in academia. Academic integrity was measured via 17 items derived from Ramdani (2018). A five-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 “strongly disagree” to 5 “strongly agree”, was used to evaluate the perceptions of academicians who participated in this study. As for data analysis software, we used partial least squaresstructural-equation-modeling – a robust analytical tool widely used to explore hypothesized relationships among variables (Sadallah et al., 2023) – to analyze the data collected. As a variance-based modeling tool, this software has gained considerable interest from researchers from various fields, including social sciences and management. It has the potential to deal with issues, e.g. non-normal data distributions, limited sample sizes and complexity of the relationships between the underlying latent variables (Hair et al., 2017).

## 4.2 Structuralmodel

The estimate of the structural model primarily focuses on analyzing the relationships between the exogenous and endogenous constructs. This assessment includes examining collinearity among the constructs and determining the significance of the hypothesized relationships (Hair et al., 2016). To assess the significance of these relationships, a bootstrapping technique with 5,000 samples was used. The statistical analysis results showed that all path relationships were statistically significant, as evidenced by bootstrap critical t-values exceeding 61.65 (one-tailed test). This supports the presence of meaningful and influential connections between the exogenous and endogenous constructs.

Our data analysis outcomes further highlight significant relationships between several constructs and academic integrity in the ChatGPT era. Specifically, PBG (b 0. 129, p 0.003), and ChatGPT use (b 0.093, p 0.009) were positively associated with academic integrity. However, workplace stress (b 0.254, p 0.000) was found to have a negative relationship with academic integrity. Whereas academic competence (b  0.033, p 0.223) had no effect on academic integrity. Hence, these results statistically support hypotheses H1, H3 and H4, but did not support H2.

## 5. Discussion

In the rapidly evolving academic landscape, the ChatGPT era marks a transformative phase where human intellect converges with the innovative capabilities of AI. This integration has significantly reshaped the dynamics of academic integrity among faculty, scholars and researchers. Traditional measures of academic success now intersect with the groundbreaking potential of AI-driven tools like ChatGPT, redefining the core principles and evaluation of academic practices. This study aims to explore the complexities of academic integrity in the context of the ChatGPT era, focusing on key predictors such as PBG, academic competence and workplace stress. Additionally, it examines the moderating role of ChatGPT adoption, shedding light on how these factors collectively influence the ethical conduct of academic professionals in today’s scholarly environment.

In this research, we found a positive relationship between PBG and academic integrity among academic staff. This can be attributed to the intrinsic motivation and self-regulation that PBG foster. According to SCT, individuals are motivated by self-regulation and selfefficacy (Bandura, 1986). That is, when academic staff strives to outperform their own previous achievements rather than competing with others, they are more likely to prioritize ethical behavior and uphold integrity in their academic endeavors. This self-referenced standard encourages accountability and a commitment to authentic success, aligning with the principles of academic integrity. Consequently, PBG act as a driving force that reinforces ethical conduct by emphasizing personal growth and professional excellence. However, we found that academic competence had no direct effect on integrity in the ChatGPT era. This suggests that possessing knowledge and skills alone may not necessarily translate to ethical behavior in academia. This could be due to the increasing reliance on AI tools like ChatGPT, which may shift the focus from individual competence to collaborative or technology-assisted outputs. Furthermore, integrity might be more influenced by factors such as personal values, external pressures or institutional culture rather than by an individual’s level of expertise. This indicates that ethical behavior in the ChatGPT era is shaped by a broader set of social and contextual factors beyond just academic competence. While competence is a core factor in SCT (Bandura, 1986), the absence of a direct relationship with integrity suggests that other external factors, such as social environment or the availability of AI tools, might play a more significant role in influencing behavior. This aligns with SCT’s emphasis on the impact of external environments in shaping individuals’ actions and decisions (Bandura, 1989).

The study also reported a negative relationship between workplace stress and academic integrity while using ChatGPT. The negative relationship between workplace stress and academic integrity, particularly in the context of ChatGPT usage, is supported by SCT’s assertion that stress can diminish self-regulation and decision-making capacity (Bandura, 1989). High levels of stress may impair an individual’s ability to maintain ethical standards, leading to a greater likelihood of unethical academic practices when using AI tools. This result highlights how stress can undermine ethical decision-making in academia. High levels of stress may push individuals toward shortcuts or reliance on AI tools like ChatGPT in ways that compromise integrity, such as bypassing original work or failing to adhere to ethical standards. Stress can also impair judgment, reduce self-regulation and heighten the likelihood of unintentional errors or ethical lapses. In the ChatGPT era, the accessibility of AI tools might exacerbate this issue, as stressed academics may over-rely on such tools without critically evaluating their use. However, the research revealed that academicians’ integrity in the ChatGPT era was positively related to their perceived ChatGPT adoption. This discovery suggests that the tool can support ethical practices in academia when used responsibly. By providing quick access to reliable information, aiding in research processes and streamlining administrative tasks, ChatGPT enables academics to focus on higherorder activities, reducing the likelihood of errors or unethical shortcuts. Additionally, its potential for transparency and accuracy in generating content can encourage proper citation and adherence to ethical standards. This finding implies that when integrated thoughtfully, ChatGPT can enhance the overall integrity of academic practices.

By integrating ChatGPT usage as a moderating factor in the relationships between academic integrity and its key determinants – PBG, academic competence and workplace stress – we discovered some intriguing findings. These findings can be explained by SCT’s focus on how environmental factors (in this case, AI tools) influence behavior. The analysis revealed that ChatGPT usage in academia had a negative moderating effect on the previously established positive relationship between PBG and academic integrity. This suggests that while striving for personal achievements typically fosters ethical behavior, the reliance on ChatGPT may alter this dynamic. The ease and efficiency of AI tools might inadvertently shift the focus from genuine effort and self-improvement to task completion through automation. This could diminish the intrinsic motivation tied to PBG, potentially leading to a weakened commitment to ethical standards. As a result, the use of ChatGPT may dilute the self-driven ethos that traditionally underpins academic integrity.

The analysis further revealed that ChatGPT adoption in academia positively moderated the relationship between academic competence and academic integrity. This suggests that AI tools like ChatGPT can enhance the ethical standards of academically competent individuals. For scholars with strong academic skills, ChatGPT provides an avenue to refine their work, access reliable information and manage complex tasks efficiently, all while upholding high ethical standards. When used appropriately, this technology can act as a supportive resource, helping competent academics maintain both efficiency and integrity by reducing the temptation to cut corners. Additionally, the study found that ChatGPT usage in academia amplified the negative relationship between workplace stress and academic integrity. This finding indicates that increased reliance on AI tools may exacerbate the ethical challenges associated with stress. While ChatGPT can reduce workloads and streamline tasks, it may also lead to stress-induced over-reliance, resulting in ethical lapses such as improper citation or producing unoriginal work. In high-stress environments, the convenience of ChatGPT could tempt academic staff to prioritize expediency over integrity, thereby intensifying the adverse effects of workplace stress on ethical behavior.

## 6. Implications and conclusions

This study provides valuable insights into the integration of AI-driven chatbots like ChatGPT in academic environments. Through empirical research, it explores a relatively new area, contributing to existing scholarly works by examining key factors that affect academic performance through the use of this innovative tool. Additionally, it highlights the important role of academic integrity among faculty and researchers in an AI-driven era. These findings establish a solid foundation for future research, offering meaningful implications that could drive further progress in understanding the impact of AI technology on academic practices.

First, due to the relevance of this study’s topic and the clear theoretical gaps in existing literature, particularly concerning the adoption of ChatGPT in academic settings, this research stands as one of the first empirical investigations into the use of OpenAI among academic staff within educational environments. Second, building on insights from SCT, this study makes a valuable contribution to the literature by examining the global use of ChatGPT among academics, focusing on the most widely used ASNSs like ResearchGate and Academia. Third, the model developed in this study integrates several key factors – PBG, competence and workplace stress – that may influence academic integrity when using ChatGPT. These factors have been underexplored in AI-driven educational contexts, highlighting a significant gap in empirical research. Therefore, the model established here provides a solid empirical foundation for future studies in AI applications, offering a path for further exploration of this critical innovation. Additionally, our study provides important and timely evidence on the use of ChatGPT in academic environments, revealing significant moderating effects on the academic integrity of faculty and researchers.

Practically, the results of this study provide actionable managerial insights and suggestions for academia in the ChatGPT era. Specifically, various stakeholders, including educational institutions, faculty, researchers and ethics and research committees, can use these findings to inform their strategies and decisions. Stakeholders like educational institutions must prioritize comprehensive ethics education programs for faculty members and researchers that specifically address the ethical utilization of AI tools such as ChatGPT. This will instill a robust foundation of academic integrity within technological advancements. Clear protocols and guidelines must be established for the ethical deployment of AI in research and academic endeavors. Institutions should encourage academic staff to set PBG, which can positively influence their academic integrity. However, it’s crucial to provide support and resources that reduce stress, ensuring that AI tools do not diminish the intrinsic motivation to achieve ethical, high-quality work. The negative relationship between workplace stress and academic integrity suggests a need for institutions to foster a supportive work environment. Offering stress management programs, workload balancing and mental health support can help reduce stress and promote ethical behavior among academic staff. Furthermore, research and ethics committees should actively monitor and assess the use of AI tools like ChatGPT in academic research to ensure that AI-assisted work adheres to ethical standards. They should also promote ongoing discussions about the potential ethical challenges posed by AI and foster a culture of transparency in its use. ChatGPT’s positive moderating effect on the relationship between academic competence and integrity suggests that integrating AI can enhance academic staff’s performance and integrity. Training programs should be designed to help faculty and researchers leverage AI to enhance their academic competence while maintaining high ethical standards. AI has the potential to provide tailored learning experiences by adapting to the individual needs of students, offering personalized feedback and facilitating more efficient administrative processes. Educators could leverage these tools to streamline content delivery, assess students’ understanding in real-time, and create more dynamic, interactive learning environments. Additionally, the use of AI can help reduce the burden of repetitive tasks, allowing educators to focus more on meaningful interactions with students. However, it is essential to ensure that AI adoption is coupled with ethical considerations to maintain academic integrity and support the development of critical thinking skills. Further research into the best practices for integrating these tools can help guide educators in maximizing their potential while preserving the quality and authenticity of education.

This study used SCT to examine academic integrity in the context of ChatGPT adoption within educational settings and academia. Positioned as a foundational exploration, future research could broaden the theoretical lens by incorporating additional relevant frameworks, such as expectancy-value theory, interest theory and achievement goal theory. These perspectives could provide a more nuanced understanding of technology use in academia, offering deeper insights into this critical topic. While ResearchGate and Academia.edu were selected as the primary ASNSs due to their prominence, they may not fully represent all users across diverse ASNSs. Future studies could investigate other significant platforms, such as Google Scholar, LinkedIn and Mendeley, to present a more holistic perspective on ChatGPT adoption within academic communities. Moreover, expanding the scope to include students and other academic staff could shed light on their motivations for engaging in positive academic practices with AI tools, further enriching the understanding of the impact of such technologies on various academic stakeholders.
