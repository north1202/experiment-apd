# Technical Students’ Attitudes Toward Academic Cheating and the Misuse of AI in Academic Activities

David Vaněček <sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup> Masaryk Institute of Advanced Studies, Czech Technical University in Prague, Prague, Czech Republic

Abstract – This study investigates student cheating behaviors, the role of artificial intelligence in facilitating academic dishonesty, and the underlying motivations behind such conduct among students at a technical university. It also explores students’ perceptions of what constitutes “excusable” versus “inexcusable” reasons for cheating and evaluates strategies aimed at preventing dishonest practices. The findings reveal that cheating is a widespread phenomenon, often supported by digital tools such as mobile phones, the internet, and AI-based applications. Students frequently attributed their behavior to external pressures, including demanding course structures and limited time, suggesting that cheating is often perceived as a coping mechanism. While students recognized the existence of institutional deterrents, such as plagiarism detection systems, a considerable number were still willing to take risks. According to students, improving academic support, ensuring fairness in assessment, and enforcing rules consistently could effectively promote academic integrity and reduce dishonest behaviour.

Keywords – Student academic cheating, usage patterns in cheating, motivation to cheat, detection and prevention, AI ethics.

## 1. Introduction

Academic integrity is fundamental to the credibility of educational institutions, especially at technical universities, where students are expected to develop critical thinking, problem-solving skills, and an indepth understanding of their field. However, the rise of technological advancements has brought about new challenges in maintaining academic honesty as noted by [1]. Student academic cheating, traditionally involving plagiarism, exam dishonesty, and unauthorized collaboration, has increasingly incorporated more sophisticated methods, such as the use of artificial intelligence. Misuse of AI can compromise the learning process but also undermines the ethical foundation of education, as reported by [2]. This shift raises significant ethical concerns, particularly regarding the integrity of students learning processes and the potential long-term consequences for their professional development.

At technical universities, where the emphasis is placed on technical expertise, students are often under immense pressure to excel academically. This pressure, combined with the availability of AI tools, such as essay generators, code-writing assistants, and even AI-driven exam aids, can tempt some students to bypass the intellectual effort required for true learning. While AI has the potential to enhance education, its misuse for cheating undermines the very purpose of technical education. The most prevalent forms of AIassisted cheating include using AI to complete assignments, write essays, solve complex problems, and even take exams. AI-powered platforms, such as chatbots and writing generators, can produce coherent and well-structured academic content, making it easy for students to submit work that is not their own. Additionally, AI algorithms designed to optimize research can help students find and present information without fully engaging with the material or understanding its underlying concepts as noted by [3]. This leads to a detrimental cycle where students may graduate with degrees but lack the essential knowledge and skills required in their chosen fields.

According to [4], the ethical dilemma becomes even more pronounced when AI-generated solutions obscure the distinction between students' own capabilities and those of automated systems, ultimately compromising their ability to apply what they have learned in real-world settings.

From an ethical standpoint, the use of AI for academic cheating raises questions about fairness, accountability, and the long-term value of a technical degree. It challenges the ethical principles of honesty and respect for the learning process, as students who rely on AI-generated work may be perceived as dishonest or lacking in essential skills. Moreover, these practices may distort assessments of academic achievement, disadvantaging students who put in genuine effort. Additionally, the misuse of AI in academic settings risks eroding trust between students, faculty, and the broader academic community as noted by [5].

As AI tools become more integrated into education, universities must address the ethical implications of their use. According to [6], this involves not only setting clear guidelines for acceptable AI use but also fostering a culture of integrity and personal responsibility among students. Balancing the benefits of AI as a legitimate educational tool with its potential for misuse is key to preserving the ethical standards of academic environments, particularly in technical universities, where innovation and integrity must go hand in hand [7].

## 2. Literature Review

The literature on academic cheating at technical universities highlights the complex and evolving nature of the problem, with AI playing a central role in transforming cheating behaviors as noted by [8], and by [9]. Students in technical programs are increasingly leveraging AI tools to complete assignments and exams, which undermines the integrity of their education and creates disparities in academic achievement. Universities must respond by investing in AI detection tools, promoting academic integrity, and providing ethical education on AI use. By addressing these issues, institutions can help preserve the credibility of technical education and ensure that graduates possess the necessary skills to succeed in their fields. The present literature review examines recent studies, focusing on student cheating behaviors at technical universities, with an emphasis on usage patterns in cheating, emerging trends, ethical concerns and implication, detection and prevention.

## 2.1. Usage Patterns in Cheating

Cheating at technical universities, which focus heavily on fields such as engineering, computer science, and applied sciences, is not a new phenomenon. However, recent literature highlights an increase in the complexity of cheating methods. In a study by [10], it was observed that while traditional cheating methods—such as copying during exams and submitting plagiarized work—remain in some cases prevalent, students are increasingly using digital tools to facilitate dishonest behavior.

In [11], a comprehensive analysis of student cheating across multiple disciplines found that technical students, especially those in computer science and engineering programs, were more likely to use software tools to aid in cheating. This is partly due to their proficiency with technology, which allows them to manipulate systems designed to ensure academic integrity. The authors also note that the anonymity and convenience provided by digital tools make it easier for students to cheat without facing immediate detection.

In [12], study on AI-assisted cheating in higher education, it was noted that AI-based tools are frequently used by students in technical programs to generate solutions to assignments, write essays, and solve coding problems. The study points out that the line between legitimate academic assistance and unethical use of AI is becoming increasingly blurred, complicating efforts to define and detect cheating.

The use of AI-driven systems in technical universities is especially concerning because of the specialized nature of the academic tasks involved. In a report by [7], it was revealed that students in engineering programs used AI to generate complex engineering solutions and even bypass required lab work. This trend has prompted discussions on whether AI's role in academic settings should be restricted to avoid further erosion of academic integrity.

In a survey of 500 engineering students conducted by 40 % of respondents admitted to using unauthorized sources during exams or assignments, while 25 % reported that they believed others engaged in similar behaviors. Peer behavior also significantly influences students' attitudes toward cheating, with many students indicating that if they perceive cheating as widespread, they are more likely to engage in it themselves. This finding aligns with the classical social learning theory, which suggests that students may adopt behaviors seen in their peers [13].

## 2.2. Ethical Concerns and Implications

Academic integrity is a critical concern in higher education, particularly among technical students who are exposed to increasingly sophisticated tools and methods. Cheating, whether in traditional settings or online courses, undermines the value of education and can lead to serious consequences. With the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into education, new opportunities and risks have emerged. Students may misuse AI to cheat or manipulate academic assessments, raising concerns among educators and policymakers alike.

The ethical concerns surrounding AI-assisted cheating are profound. According to [2], the use of AI for cheating not only compromises the learning process but also undermines the ethical foundation of education. In this sense, students may graduate with degrees that do not reflect their true abilities, which can have long-term consequences for their professional careers and the reputation of the institutions they graduate from. In addition to these concerns, issues related to data privacy and the potential marginalization of human educators have also been raised [20].

In addition to individual impacts, AI-driven cheating poses a threat to academic fairness. As noted by [4], the proliferation of AI in academic cheating means that students who do not resort to such shortcuts are at a disadvantage, especially in highly competitive environments like technical universities. This creates an uneven playing field and raises questions about the equity of academic evaluations. Furthermore, institutions face a growing challenge in detecting AI-generated content, as these tools become increasingly sophisticated, making traditional plagiarism detection software less effective.

Moreover, in [8], the authors emphasize the importance of creating educational programs that highlight the ethical use of AI in academic settings. By educating students on the risks and ethical implications of AI misuse, universities can mitigate the temptation to engage in dishonest behaviors. They suggest that promoting academic honesty should be a priority at technical universities, particularly as AI continues to evolve and become more embedded in students' daily lives.

The use of AI in academic writing has generated significant interest in AI-related plagiarism. According to [14] and [19], AI-giarism is an emergent form of academic dishonesty involving AI and plagiarism within the higher education context. She introduces a novel instrument to explore conceptualisation of AI-giarism, offering a significant tool for educators and policy-makers.

AI-giarism refers to the unethical practice of using artificial intelligence technologies, particularly generative language models, to generate content that is plagiarised either from original human-authored work or directly from AI-generated content, without appropriate acknowledgement of the original sources or AI’s contribution.

## 2.3. Detection and Prevention

Several recent studies have examined strategies to detect and prevent cheating in technical universities, particularly in the age of AI. The potential of advanced AI tools to detect AI-assisted cheating is explored by [15]. They analyze patterns in student submissions that might indicate the use of generative models. They suggest that institutions should invest in AI-driven detection systems, as these tools are better equipped to identify the subtle markers left by AI-generated content. Additionally, they advocate for increased emphasis on developing students' ethical decisionmaking and fostering a culture of academic integrity.

A study by [16] found that technical students perceive cheating as more common in online courses compared to face-to-face settings. This is partly due to the reduced supervision in online courses and the availability of technology, which makes it easier for students to cheat. For example, students may use search engines, external devices, or unauthorized sources during assessments. In technical education, where skills like coding, engineering design, and scientific analysis are critical, cheating can also manifest in the form of plagiarizing code or using prewritten solutions without understanding the material. Additionally, motivation for cheating among technical students often ties to the pressure to succeed in competitive fields. Many students report feeling overwhelmed by the demands of their coursework and resort to dishonest practices as a shortcut to achieving high grades or meeting deadlines.

The misuse of AI is not limited to technical fields. In a study by [17], the authors found that many students in STEM programs admitted to using AI tools to generate answers to math problems or to automate tasks in coding assignments. Although these tools may enhance learning when used responsibly, they also present a significant challenge for educators who must find ways to detect and prevent cheating facilitated by AI.

On the other hand, AI also has the potential to improve academic integrity. AI-driven plagiarism detection software, such as Turnitin, can identify copied content across vast databases of academic works.

Additionally, AI-based proctoring systems have been implemented in online courses to monitor students during exams and detect suspicious behaviors, such as the use of unauthorized devices. While these systems have raised privacy concerns, they also provide a means to combat cheating in the digital age.

There is also a growing call for educators to incorporate AI literacy into their curricula. As noted by [12] and [18] and [21], teaching students about the potential risks of AI misuse and the ethical considerations surrounding AI technologies is essential in preparing them for both academic and professional environments. Institutions must create policies and resources that foster responsible AI use, ensuring that students are aware of the consequences of academic dishonesty and how AI can be used to enhance learning, rather than circumvent it.

The misuse of AI and academic cheating among technical students remains a significant issue in higher education. While technical students may perceive academic cheating as more prevalent in online courses, AI technologies introduce both challenges and opportunities for improving academic integrity. Educators must adapt to this changing landscape by implementing ethical guidelines, fostering AI literacy, and using AI-driven tools to detect and prevent academic dishonesty. Addressing these issues will help create a more equitable and effective learning environment for all students.

## 3. Problem Statement - Research Objectives

This research aimed to explore the prevalence, patterns, and underlying motives of academic cheating among technical students, with a particular focus on the influence of recent technological advancements, such as Artificial Intelligence (AI). It examined the frequency of cheating behaviors, the role of AI in facilitating such actions, and the factors that would motivate students to cheat. Additionally, the research investigated what students perceived as "excusable" reasons for cheating and evaluate potfential strategies for detecting and preventing academic dishonesty. Through a questionnaire-based survey, this study sought to provide insights into how universities and educators can address and reduce cheating in academic settings, fostering a more honest and effective learning environment.

## 3.1. Research Questions

In light of the identified problem, this study was guided by a set of research questions designed to comprehensively investigate the complex issue of academic dishonesty among students in the digital age.

1. What is the prevalence of student academic cheating?

2. What are the prevailing usage patterns in academic cheating?

3. What is the role of AI in cheating?

4. What are dominant motives to cheat?

5. What are „excusable” and “inexcusable” motives?

6. Are there any possibilities for detecting student cheating?

7. How can universities and teachers prevent students from cheating?

## 3.2. Research Methodology

A quantitative approach was employed in the study to gather and evaluate data on the perspectives of technical university students on academic cheating. Before conducting the study, research ethical approval was obtained from the university´s administration.

The 12 item-survey measured student attitudes and perceptions about cheating. Students were asked to rate their beliefs for a variety of cheating scenarios and to discuss how often they engaged in any of the behaviours themselves. In addition, demographic data such as study program, year of study, age, gender were collected.

## 3.3. Questionnaire Design and Administration

The present study employed a quantitative survey design to comprehensively define the current state of cheating among students across two technical faculties. A structured questionnaire was used to collect data, enabling both quantitative and qualitative analysis. Before designing the questionnaire, the aim and scope of the study were defined by revisiting relevant literature. Then, interviews were held with several students to get more insight into their views on the issue. During the design, a draft of the questionnaire was created, including general instructions, personal information, and the questionnaire items.

In the introductory part, a short paragraph informed potential respondents about the researchers and the goal of the survey. Personal information enabled to define the characteristics of sample in terms of gender, grade level, study program, age, and faculty. The body of the questionnaire included 18 questions asking students to describe their opinions. Next, opinions were gathered from two experts from each faculty, for a total of four, who had experience and expertise in ethical student behavior in academic activities. Three items were deleted, and the remaining 15 were modified based on the experts' feedback. A pilot implementation phase was then conducted with students to check for intelligibility, and some items were remodified based on their feedback.

Final form consisted of 11 items including both close-ended and open-ended questions: closed questions generally streamline the survey process, making it more efficient for both respondents and researchers while ensuring consistent, easy-tocompare data. Open-ended questions allow respondents to provide detailed, nuanced responses in their own words, rather than selecting from predefined answer choices.

## 3.4. Participants

The research was administered via an online questionnaire. Participants were recruited through an online platform, they were given an informed consent form before completing the survey, and participation was voluntary and anonymous. Participants were allowed to skip questions they were not comfortable answering.

This suggests that many students view cheating as a reaction to external factors they cannot control, such as course design or teaching methods.

Internal reasons (e.g., self-esteem, personal ambition) also play a significant role, with up to half of the students acknowledging that personal pressures—such as fear of failure or the desire for better grades—contribute to their decisions. This suggests a significant emotional or psychological component to cheating, linked to self-worth and expectations.

Practical reasons (e.g., avoiding failure, using available technology) are less frequent but still notable. These motives reflect a pragmatic approach to cheating, where individuals believe that cheating is a low-risk, high-reward option in their particular context.

These findings suggest that universities and institutions may want to consider not only addressing personal or psychological factors (such as self-esteem and fear of failure) but also improving course structures and reducing environmental pressures in order to curb cheating.

## 4.7. “Excusable” and “Inexcusable” Motives for Cheating

The next item aimed to analyze students' perceptions of "excusable" and "inexcusable" motives for academic cheating. It is supposed that this distinction often emerges from students' own perceptions of their actions and the context in which they cheat. Excusable motives are those that students may feel justify their behavior, often linked to external pressures, emotional states, or situational factors. In this category, students may view cheating as a response to circumstances rather than a moral failing.

## Student “Excusable” Motives Included - Time Pressure and Workload:

Students reported heavy workloads or insufficient time for studying often justify cheating as a necessary evil. For example, time constraints due to balancing work or multiple courses can lead students to feel that they have no choice but to cheat to meet academic expectations.

## Perceived Unfairness or Inadequate Teaching:

Students who feel that the course material is too difficult, poorly taught, or unfairly assessed may view cheating as a way to level the playing field. Students frequently justified cheating when they perceived that the teacher´s behavior or course structure was inadequate or when they felt that their efforts would not be properly recognized despite hard work.

## Lack of Interest or Engagement in the Subject:

When students feel disconnected or uninterested in the subject matter, they may rationalize cheating as a way to get through the course without fully engaging with the content. This is related to intrinsic motivation; students who do not see value in the subject are less likely to engage honestly, viewing cheating as a pragmatic choice.

## External Pressure for Good Grades:

Some students feel compelled to cheat due to the high stakes they face in achieving academic success, especially in competitive environments where grades are crucial for future opportunities like scholarships or job prospects. This type of pressure can make cheating seem like the only option to secure a positive outcome.

## Student “Inexcusable” Motives for Cheating:

In contrast, inexcusable motives for cheating are those that are driven more by personal values or intentional disregard for academic integrity. These reasons are often seen as more morally indefensible and less likely to be viewed as justifiable by students themselves. Common inexcusable motives include:

## Lack of Effort or Laziness:

Some students cheat because they do not want to put in the effort required to complete assignments or study for exams. These students may be motivated by a desire for the rewards (grades) without the work, and they may see cheating as a shortcut to success. Students who lack a strong work ethic or commitment to their studies are more likely to justify cheating purely for convenience.

## Peer Pressure and Social Influence:

In some cases, students may cheat because of pressure from peers who normalize academic dishonesty. Peer pressure can lead students to believe that cheating is a common or expected behavior, even when they do not feel justified in doing so. Social learning theory suggests that students who are surrounded by others who cheat may internalize the behavior and come to view it as acceptable or even necessary.

## Dishonest Intentions:

Students who engage in premeditated cheating— such as purchasing essays, plagiarizing, or using unauthorized materials—are less likely to see their behavior as excusable. This motive is more consciously driven by a desire to manipulate the academic system for personal gain without any real concern for the consequences. These actions are usually considered inexcusable because they reflect an intentional disregard for the values of honesty and fairness.

## Cultural or Contextual Factors:

In some cases, students from certain cultural backgrounds or educational systems where cheating is normalized may not see their actions as wrong, even though they are considered inexcusable in the academic context. However, within the culture of academic integrity, these actions are typically considered unethical.

## 5. Discussion

The data received in the survey unsurprisingly confirm the high incidence of student cheating, with almost half of the respondents admitting to frequent cheating. These results align with other literature resources, mentioned by [9].

The prevailing types of student cheating are tied to digital resources, including AI. Cheating practices are evolving alongside advancements in technology, with methods like unethical AI assistance becoming more prevalent. As reported by Chan [14], investigating changes in understandings of academic misconduct has led to the emergence of a new term AI-giarism. This term refers to the unethical practice of using AI, particularly generative language models, to generate content that is plagiarized either from original humanauthored work or directly from AI-generated content.

Academic cheating can be encouraged by the perception of how widespread dishonest behavior is among other students. Since a significant majority of the respondents perceive cheating as a frequent issue within the student community, this unethical behavior may, to some extent, lose its negative connotation.

When asked to suggest possible types of AI misuse in academic activities, students identified several options: essay/paper generation, paraphrasing/rewriting, answering exam questions, solving problem sets, suggesting research objectives, creating fake citations, completing written assignments, and providing homework solutions. This misuse of AI enables students to bypass learning, critical thinking or the development of their own knowledge and skills. On the other hand, it may paradoxically improve some students' verbal skills when they attempt to 'disguise' the AI-generated content and modify the text to reflect their 'personal style'.

The effort to study student motivation to cheat clearly distinguished the dominance of external motives as many students view cheating as a reaction to external factors they cannot control and cause by other members of the academic community (teachers, university management). The results align with general psychological theories, e.g., Social Learning Theory, Self-Determination Theory), or any other theory related to external influences on behavior, which suggest that individuals are more likely to engage in certain behaviors, like cheating, when they perceive external pressures or negative influences as driving forces behind their actions. Students also declare external factors to be excusable motives for cheating, while personal motives are viewed as inexcusable.

Recent research has focused on methods for detecting and preventing cheating in order to mitigate this negative phenomenon. Students in the research sample are aware of plagiarism detection software and AI detection software but are less familiar with other methods, such as using software to analyse inconsistencies in writing style. Nevertheless, most students are still willing to take risks, cheat, or consider the possibility of cheating.

As for the issue of preventing cheating, the results suggest that teacher involvement plays a crucial role. Teachers can enforce the rules, strictly monitor student achievements, or reduce the emphasis on highstakes exams and assignments."

## 6. Conclusion

Developing more awareness programs and campaigns that clearly explain the academic, ethical, and future implications of cheating can discourage students from considering it as an option. Given the high preference for reducing the emphasis on highstakes exams, universities could explore continuous assessment methods like project-based learning, peer reviews, or take-home assignments that encourage deeper learning and reduce the temptation to cheat.

Although advanced cheating detection tools are still valuable, their focus could be on spot-checking or addressing extreme cases rather than being a primary strategy. More proactive methods such as preventive education may be more widely accepted and effective.

In order to anticipate future, the intersection of student academic cheating and AI presents both challenges and opportunities for academic integrity. With the increasing reliance on digital technologies, AI tools will likely continue to evolve, making it easier for students to engage in dishonest practices. However, these advancements also offer powerful solutions for combating cheating. AI-driven plagiarism detection systems, real-time monitoring tools, and even AI-designed assessments that adapt to students' knowledge levels could make academic dishonesty more difficult to conceal. Additionally, AI can be leveraged to foster a culture of academic integrity, with personalized learning tools that encourage ethical decision-making and selfregulation.

In the future, the role of educators will shift towards not only addressing cheating but also integrating technology into the learning process in ways that promote fairness, accountability, and critical thinking. Universities could adopt more proactive, AI-enhanced methods for prevention, creating an environment where academic honesty becomes the standard, and students are encouraged to thrive based on their own abilities rather than seeking shortcuts. The key to a successful future will lie in a balanced approach, where AI helps both detect and prevent cheating while promoting an educational ecosystem based on trust and integrity.
