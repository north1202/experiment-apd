# A ‘low-tech’ Academic Virtue Ethics in the Age of Generative AI

Timothy Daly<sup>1</sup>

Received: 2 July 2025 / Accepted: 31 August 2025 / Published online: 22 October 2025   
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature B.V. 2025

## Abstract

An academic must both read and write papers. Generative artificial intelligence (AI) can now do both. I draw on Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics to argue that reliance on AI outsources activities and reduces virtues necessary to achieve the purpose of academia— flourishing through the cultivation of reason. I highlight empirical research suggesting the threat posed by reliance on AI for the cultivation of intellectual virtues, and the need for sustained engagement with intellectual tasks. I thus urge scholars to resist delegating academic tasks to AI and instead to adopt a ‘low-tech’ virtue ethics, for which I ofer advice on practical implementation.

Keywords virtue ethics · low-tech · flourishing · academia · aristotle · artificial intelligence · writing · reading

## Introduction: Flourishing as the End Goal of Academic Life

A life in academia is ridden with obstacles and frustration, but also moments ofjoy. I follow the Ancient Greek philosopher Aristotle in claiming that the ultimate goal or ‘telos’ of academic life is flourishing or ‘eudaimonia’ through the cultivation of the faculty of reason. Five intellectual virtues are the means by which flourishing is achieved through habit, required for excellence in all ofthe virtues. I describe these virtues before outlining the threats posed to them by generative artificial intelligence (AI) in the form of large language models (LLMs) and suggesting the need for a ‘low-tech’ virtue ethics to promote flourishing in academia in the age of AI. My primary goal is to convince scholars to consider adopting this ethic so as to cultivate their own intellectual virtues. My secondary goal, which is much broader and beyond the scope of this opinion paper, is to encourage reflection around why and how other actors should promote the intellectual virtues in academia, e.g. for educators to cultivate virtue in their students as their primary function, and for decision makers in academic policy to reward virtue (rather than metrics of competition, such as publishing outputs).

## Five Intellectual Virtues To Achieve Academic Flourishing

Aristotle—born in 384 BCE and who died in 322 BCE—was an Ancient Greek philosopher and polymath who lectured on almost every domain of knowledge in human thought. A student of Plato, he founded the Peripatetic school ofphilosophy in the Lyceum in Athens, where he began the wider Aristotelian tradition that set the groundwork for the development ofmodern science.

Aristotle lectured extensively on virtue ethics (Aristotle, 1925). The fundamental claim of virtue ethics is that things and actions have a telos—a final end. Here, I claim that the end or goal of academic life is flourishing through the cultivation of reason. Intellectual virtues are the means by which an academic can reach that end.

Aristotle identified three classes ofintellectual virtues—theoretical (episteme, nous, sophia), practical (phronesis), and productive (techne). Episteme is knowledge of one’s academic field, its aims, methods, and the conclusions drawn from them. Nous is intuition based on that knowledge—it is about knowing which ideas are good ideas, and making judgements within the field based on one’s expertise. Sophia is a combination ofepisteme and nous, a higher form of wisdom that is not merely related entirely to one’s field, but rather how one’s field relates to the rest of life and society. As a practical virtue, phronesis is the ability to decide how to reach a certain end, and also the ability of reason to determine what counts as a desirable end. Finally, the productive virtue of techne is the ability to make something through the use of true reasoning. Across all domains of academia, scholars tend to be artisans of academic papers, which when published are read, discussed and cited by academic and broader society.

All five of these intellectual virtues must be practiced through habit, and have been throughout history, to achieve the end of flourishing through the cultivation of reason. To this end, students are given tasks that academics perform throughout their academic careers. Relevant tasks include reading the literature for relevant concepts and papers; reading and summarising important and/or recent papers; and producing a paper on a given topic, including the use of statistical analysis in technical domains. All of these tasks cultivate the five intellectual virtues. However, with the recent arrival of LLMs, all five of them are vulnerable to being diminished by the use of generative AI.

## The Threat of Generative Artificial Intelligence To Intellectual Virtue in Academia

Professors and students alike can now use generative AI to perform most intellectual tasks that they would previously have undertaken themselves, from literature research, reading, writing, and performing statistical analyses. This means that AI can now perform any or all tasks involved in academic life.

However, LLMs function through “prompts,” a goal-oriented command. This means that they need a human prompter through which to achieve a certain end. Academics with some degree of familiarity with their field and its norms will know which prompts are necessary to achieve a convincing end. Anecdotal and bibliometric evidence suggest that LLMs are being increasingly used by students and academics alike to produce papers, suggesting that an increasing amount ofintellectual tasks are being delegated to AI (Joelving, 2024; Kobak et al., 2025).

This increasing reliance on generative AI amounts to a community-wide voluntary replacement ofcertain intellectual tasks. This has important negative implications for academia, and also for the individuals involved in using LLMs to perform certain tasks. Fasoli et al. (2025) have recently provided a framework for the study oftechnology-induced cognitive diminishment (TICD) that applies to both single episodes of cognitive performance and longer-term cognitive abilities. They summarize several empirical and review studies (ibid., Fig. 2) providing evidence that TICD applies to working memory; attention, learning and metacognition; and spatial abilities. Moreover, in a recent preprint, Kosmyna and colleagues propose a mechanism linking short-term TICD to long-term TICD: the empirically-grounded concept of ‘cognitive debt’ in AI use, suggesting that task delegation in writing essays in an academic context leads to underperformance “at neural, linguistic, and behavioral levels” over a 4-month period (Kosmyna et al., 2025). ## A ‘low-tech’ Ethic To Promote Virtue in Academia in the Age of AI

What can be done to promote intellectual virtue when any or all academic tasks can be replaced by AI? I consider that a ‘low-tech’ philosophy is necessary to restore academic virtue:

““Low-tech” is not a noun but an adjective … a low-tech mindset is simply one that is critical about new technologies. It refuses to assume that a new technology is inherently better than the one it replaces. A low-tech approach also questions the criteria which make us decide whether or not something is “better” than something else” (De Decker, 2025).

Some degree oftechnology—whether it be pen-and-paper, a typewriter or a computer—is a necessary part ofacademic life. A low-tech philosophy encourages researchers to use no more tech than necessary to cultivate academic flourishing. This means not using LLMs for any task delegation. This, however, is a negative framing of what is actually a very positive decision: choosing to use the full spectrum of human potential to perform tasks oriented towards the goal of flourishing. To this end, low-tech is a useful means, and certainly not an end in itself.

Neurocognitive studies suggest the superior brain activation and better learning outcomes associated with using pen and paper instead of digital writing (Ihara et al., 2021; Wiley & Rapp, 2021; Van der Weel & Van der Meer, 2024). Practically speaking, academics could use pen-and-paper to draft their ideas, and typing to write them up into full papers. Instructors and lecturers could encourage and reward sustained engagement with the tasks given to students, such as longhand note-taking in lectures, which has been associated with favourable impacts on attention, memory and emotional regulation (Desselle & Shane, 2018).

However, what about the pragmatic objection that since students are already using AI, should their teachers not simply encourage them to improve the complexity of their prompt writing as something that could contribute to the development of virtuous intellectual habits? I would argue that prompt-writing cannot be a satisfying pedagogical objective of the university because it does not require sustained attention with the entire task of, for example, writing an essay. In this way, reducing a task to prompt-writing is likely to frustrate what Aristotle calls the “proper pleasure” ofa task, and thus reduce the likelihood that “Cognitive activities become more discriminating and precise when increased by their proper pleasure” (p. 23, Cagnoli Fiecconi, 2020). Increasing the proper pleasure of a task requires sustained attention throughout the entirety of the task. Daniel Kahneman argued in “Thinking, fast and slow” (Kahneman, 2011) that humans have two cognitive systems in place: a fast “System 1” to inform short-term, automatic decision-making and a slow “System 2” for careful thinking about complexity. It is true that honing a prompt would require more sustained engagement and use of intellectual virtues than a simple prompt. But once the student has clicked “Send,” virtue-producing engagement is likely to end there: LLMs produce output at a rate that is likely to distract the prior sustained engagement, and lead to activation of visually-biased, System 1-like automatic processes (Turo et al., 2023). The presence of rapidly-generated, salient content may reinforce other cognitive biases, such as over-rating the LLM output content (Brem & Rivieccio, 2024). Thus, prompt-writing is a limited form oftraining that interrupts sustained engagement with the entirety ofa task, and while trained students might become better prompt writers, they are unlikely to become better judges of content. In response to such pitfalls, what is sought is not a ‘low-tech’ ethic for its own sake, but rather, one that increases the likelihood of sustained engagement throughout strenuous cognitive tasks, and reduction of distractions, for the sake of gaining intellectual virtues.

## Conclusion

The increasingly widespread use of LLMs in academic publishing is a symptom of perverse incentives that confuse the means of academia (e.g. the publishing of papers) with its ends (i.e. human flourishing through the use of reason). I urge scholars to adopt a ‘low-tech’ virtue ethics to help them to cultivate their own reasoning abilities. Moreover, I have not addressed the ethics of declaration of intellectual contributions in the age of AI, a separate and pressing ethical dilemma (Kendall, 2024). A low-tech rethink of academia should invite us to reflect on both the means and the ends of individual and collective intellectual endeavours, and invites a more human-centred approach in an increasingly technocratic world.
