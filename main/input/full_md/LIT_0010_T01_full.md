# Student perceptions of generative AI tools in higher education: A cross-regional study of use, ethics, and educational utility

## Abstract

Despite the rapid uptake of generative AI (GAI) tools in higher education, there is limited empirical understanding of how students balance perceived educational utility with ethical concerns such as privacy, bias, and overreliance, including risks of cognitive ofloading and weakened epistemic authority. This study provides a cross-regional comparison of students’ perceptions and use of GAI by focusing on Europe and Australia, two higher education contexts shaped by contrasting policy and institutional cultures, with Europe often characterised by stronger regulatory attention (e.g., data protection and academic integrity governance) and Australia by comparatively pragmatic, implementation-oriented approaches. Using a cross-sectional mixed-methods design, we surveyed 788 university students (Europe n = 402, Australia n = 386) via an online questionnaire and analysed the data using ANOVA and chi-square tests, complemented by qualitative content analysis of open-ended responses. Across both regions, students reported strong perceived benefits, most notably accessibility to a fast knowledge base (72.5%) and support for personalised learning (66.4%). Importantly, ethical concerns were widely acknowledged but did not consistently deter adoption, suggesting a pattern of risk normalisation and pragmatic prioritisation of eficiency. However, the results also reveal a statistically meaningful nuance: privacy concerns were more clearly linked to willingness to adopt among Australian students, whereas concerns about bias and dependence showed weaker or non-significant relationships with adoption. Qualitative responses help explain these patterns by indicating that students often recognise ethical risks but accept them as manageable trade-ofs when academic productivity gains are salient, while also referencing regional diferences in integrity and data-protection expectations. The findings provide actionable implications for higher education institutions and policymakers by supporting the need for structured, multidimensional

AI literacy initiatives that integrate technical, ethical, and critical competencies, and by highlighting the value of context-specific guidance aligned with distinct regulatory and pedagogical cultures. Limitations include self-reported measures, convenience sampling with disciplinary overrepresentation, and aggregation of diverse European countries into a single regional category, which constrain generalisability and call for longitudinal and more diferentiated cross-cultural analyses in future work.

Keywords Generative artificial intelligence (GAI) · Large language models (LLMs) · Student perceptions · Ethical concerns · Higher education

## 1 Introduction

The rapid integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into higher education has reshaped contemporary learning environments by expanding access to information, supporting personalised learning pathways, and enabling real-time academic assistance (Larsen, 2023; Yang et al., 2025). In recent years, generative AI (GAI) tools, particularly large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, have gained exceptional prominence due to their ability to generate human-like responses, assist with complex academic tasks, and support self-directed learning (Kasneci et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025; Rudolph et al., 2023). As a result, GAI tools are increasingly embedded in students’ everyday learning practices across disciplines, raising fundamental questions not only about their pedagogical value but also about their ethical, cognitive, and epistemic implications.

Empirical studies have demonstrated that GAI systems can perform at levels comparable to or exceeding those of students in specific academic domains, including business and medical education (Gilson et al., 2022; Kung et al., 2023; Tajik, 2024; Wecks et al., 2024a). While these findings highlight the disruptive potential of GAI for assessment and academic support, they also intensify concerns regarding academic integrity, overreliance on AI-generated outputs, and the shifting boundaries of epistemic authority in higher education (Cassidy, 2023; Schnabel, 2025; Wu & Shi, 2025). In particular, scholars have warned that students may increasingly defer judgment and problem-solving to AI systems, a phenomenon described as cognitive ofloading, which may weaken critical thinking, deep learning, and independent knowledge construction over time (Gerlich, 2025; Lewandowsky, 2025). At the same time, issues related to algorithmic bias, data privacy, and AI “hallucinations” continue to challenge the responsible integration of GAI into educational contexts (Turner et al., 2024; Pérez-Escolar et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2025).

Despite growing ethical awareness in policy and scholarly discourse, emerging evidence suggests that students’ actual use of GAI tools is often driven by pragmatic considerations such as eficiency, accessibility, and academic productivity. This tension between ethical concern and continued adoption has led researchers to question whether ethical risks meaningfully shape student behaviour or whether such risks are increasingly normalised in AI-mediated learning environments. Understanding this ethical–pragmatic gap is particularly important, as it directly afects how institutions design AI governance, assessment practices, and AI literacy initiatives.

Existing research on GAI in higher education has predominantly focused on technical performance, instructional afordances, or educator perspectives, while students’ subjective perceptions and decision-making processes remain comparatively underexplored. Moreover, many survey-based studies treat ethical concerns, AI literacy, and adoption as isolated dimensions, ofering limited insight into how these factors interact in shaping students’ engagement with GAI tools. In particular, little is known about how students reconcile perceived educational utility with ethical awareness, or how this balance may difer across educational systems shaped by distinct regulatory, cultural, and pedagogical traditions.

This study addresses this gap by examining students’ perceptions, use patterns, and ethical considerations related to GAI tools in higher education through a crossregional comparison of Europe and Australia. These two contexts provide a theoretically meaningful contrast. European higher education systems operate within a strongly regulated environment, most notably shaped by the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and institutionalised academic integrity frameworks, which foreground data protection, accountability, and ethical compliance. In contrast, Australian universities have generally adopted a more pragmatic and implementationoriented approach to educational innovation, emphasising flexibility, employability, and applied learning outcomes (Ibrahim et al., 2023; Australian Government Department of Education, 2023). Rather than relying on broad stereotypes, this comparison allows us to explore how diferent regulatory and pedagogical cultures mediate students’ ethical sensitivity, perceived risks, and willingness to adopt GAI tools in practice.

The central research problem addressed in this study is therefore not whether students use generative AI tools, but how they balance high perceived educational utility with ethical concerns that may or may not translate into behavioural restraint. Drawing on literature on technology adoption, AI literacy, and epistemic authority, this study examines whether ethical concerns function as meaningful deterrents to GAI adoption or whether they are subordinated to pragmatic learning priorities. In doing so, the study also considers the role of AI literacy as a multidimensional construct encompassing technical competence, ethical awareness, and critical evaluation of AI-generated content.

By adopting a mixed-methods approach and foregrounding students’ perspectives, this research contributes to the literature in three keyways. First, it provides empirical evidence on the ethical–pragmatic tension underlying students’ engagement with GAI tools. Second, it ofers a theoretically grounded cross-regional comparison that moves beyond descriptive accounts of adoption to examine how regulatory and educational cultures shape student perceptions. Third, it informs institutional and policy discussions by identifying areas where existing ethical frameworks and AI literacy initiatives may not meaningfully influence student behaviour.

Beyond descriptive cross-regional comparison, this study contributes theoretically by positioning regional educational context as a contextual moderator in students’ ethical–pragmatic negotiation of generative AI adoption. Rather than treating adoption as culturally neutral, the study conceptualises regulatory and pedagogical environments as shaping how ethical concerns are interpreted, prioritised, or normalised in practice.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on generative AI adoption, ethical concerns, and AI literacy in higher education and develops the conceptual foundation for the study. Section 3 outlines the research design, methodology, and data collection procedures. Section 7 presents the empirical results, followed by a discussion of theoretical and practical implications in Sect. 8. The paper concludes by outlining limitations and directions for future research.

## 2 Review of literature

This section synthesises prior empirical and conceptual research on students’ engagement with generative AI (GAI) tools in higher education. Rather than providing a broad overview of AI in education, the review focuses on three interrelated strands that inform the study’s conceptual model: perceived educational utility as a driver of adoption, ethical concerns and their behavioural implications (including risk normalisation, epistemic authority, and cognitive ofloading), and AI literacy as a mediating and moderating mechanism shaping responsible and critical use. By integrating these strands, the review moves beyond descriptive accounts of GAI adoption and establishes a theoretically grounded foundation for the proposed research framework.

## 2.1 Generative AI adoption and perceived educational utility

Research on generative AI (GAI) adoption in higher education has consistently highlighted perceived educational utility as a primary driver of student engagement with these tools. Drawing from broader technology adoption literature, perceived utility reflects learners’ assessments of whether a technology enhances task performance, learning eficiency, and academic outcomes. In the context of GAI, this includes support for information retrieval, academic writing, problem-solving, and conceptual clarification (Das et al., 2025). Empirical studies across disciplines report that students perceive GAI tools as valuable academic assistants, particularly for reducing cognitive load associated with routine or time-intensive tasks (Kasneci et al., 2023; Larsem et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2025).

Recent survey-based research indicates that students frequently prioritise pragmatic benefits such as speed, accessibility, and convenience when deciding whether to use GAI tools in their studies (Davis et al., 2025; Rudolph et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). These findings align with evidence that GAI systems can produce outputs comparable to high-performing students in structured academic tasks, further reinforcing perceptions of usefulness and eficiency (Gilson et al., 2022; Tajik, 2024; Wang, 2024). As a result, adoption decisions are often framed less as ethical or pedagogical choices and more as instrumental responses to academic demands.

However, while perceived utility is widely acknowledged as a key adoption factor, existing studies tend to treat it as a self-evident or unproblematic construct. Much of the literature emphasises positive learning afordances without suficiently interrogating how reliance on GAI tools may reshape students’ learning strategies, epistemic judgments, or depth of engagement. This gap is particularly salient in light of concerns that frequent reliance on AI-generated outputs may encourage surface learning and reduce opportunities for independent reasoning and knowledge construction.

Moreover, adoption-focused studies often examine utility in isolation, without considering how it interacts with ethical awareness or students’ broader understanding of AI systems. This fragmented approach limits explanatory power, as it fails to account for situations in which students acknowledge potential risks but continue to adopt GAI tools due to strong perceived benefits. As a result, the literature provides limited insight into the mechanisms through which perceived utility may override ethical hesitation or critical reflection.

In response to these limitations, recent scholarship has begun to call for more integrated models of GAI adoption that situate perceived utility within a broader framework of ethical considerations and AI literacy (Ng, 2021; Ng, 2024; Choi et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2023). From this perspective, perceived educational utility remains a central driver of adoption, but its influence cannot be fully understood without examining how students interpret risks, assess AI-generated knowledge, and negotiate trade-ofs between eficiency and responsible use. This study builds on this emerging direction by examining perceived utility not as an isolated determinant, but as one component within a multidimensional model of student engagement with generative AI in higher education.

## 2.2 Ethical concerns, risk normalisation, and epistemic authority

The rapid difusion of generative AI (GAI) tools in higher education has intensified scholarly debate around ethical risks, particularly those related to data privacy, algorithmic bias, academic integrity, and overreliance on AI-generated content (Wei et al., 2025b). Normative and policy-oriented literature frequently positions these concerns as critical barriers to responsible adoption, emphasising the need for regulatory safeguards, institutional guidelines, and ethical governance frameworks (UNESCO, 2024; Turner et al., 2024). Within this perspective, ethical awareness is often assumed to translate into cautious or restrained use of GAI tools by students.

However, emerging empirical evidence challenges this assumption. A growing number of studies report a disconnect between students’ ethical awareness and their actual usage behaviour, suggesting that ethical concerns do not consistently deter adoption (Pérez-Escolar et al., 2025; Davis et al., 2025). Instead, students appear to acknowledge ethical risks while continuing to engage with GAI tools due to strong perceived benefits. This pattern has been described in prior literature as risk normalisation, whereby potential harm becomes accepted as manageable or unavoidable features of everyday technology use, particularly when eficiency and performance gains are salient.

Risk normalisation is closely linked to broader transformations in students’ epistemic practices. As GAI tools increasingly generate plausible, fluent, and contextually relevant outputs, students may shift their trust from traditional sources of knowledge toward AI systems, thereby reconfiguring notions of epistemic authority. Rather than serving solely as supplementary aids, GAI tools can assume an authoritative role in shaping interpretations, arguments, and academic decisions. Scholars have cautioned that such shifts may encourage uncritical acceptance of AI-generated content, especially when students lack robust strategies for verification and evaluation (Lewandowsky, 2025).

Relatedly, concerns have been raised about cognitive ofloading, whereby learners delegate cognitive efort to AI systems, potentially reducing opportunities for deep processing, metacognitive engagement, and independent problem-solving (Gerlich, 2025). While cognitive ofloading is not inherently detrimental and may support learning when strategically employed, excessive reliance on GAI tools risks transforming learning into a process of optimisation rather than understanding. Importantly, existing studies rarely examine how students themselves perceive or negotiate these cognitive risks in relation to perceived academic utility.

Despite the prominence of ethical discourse in institutional policies, students’ ethical reasoning about GAI use often remains situational and pragmatic. Qualitative research suggests that learners tend to diferentiate between abstract ethical principles and concrete academic practices, accepting certain risks as justified trade-ofs in high-pressure learning environments. In such contexts, ethical concerns related to bias or dependence may be downplayed, while privacy or assessment-related risks receive greater attention, depending on institutional norms and perceived enforcement (Pérez-Escolar et al., 2025).

These dynamics highlight a critical limitation of existing literature: ethical concerns are frequently treated as static attitudes rather than as context-sensitive judgments shaped by educational culture, assessment regimes, and students’ understanding of AI systems (Humr & Canan, 2025). As a result, current research provides limited insight into when and why ethical awareness translates into behavioural restraint, and when it is subordinated to pragmatic learning priorities. Addressing this gap requires moving beyond normative assumptions and examining ethical concerns as part of a broader decision-making process that includes perceived utility, epistemic trust, and learners’ capacity to critically engage with AI-generated knowledge.

## 2.3 AI literacy as a mediating and moderating mechanism

Recent scholarship has increasingly emphasised AI literacy as a critical competence for navigating AI-mediated learning environments. Rather than being limited to technical proficiency, AI literacy is commonly conceptualised as a multidimensional construct encompassing technical understanding of AI systems, ethical awareness of their societal and educational implications, and critical capacity to evaluate the reliability and limitations of AI-generated outputs (Ng, 2024). Within higher education, AI literacy is therefore positioned as a foundational capability for responsible, informed, and reflective engagement with generative AI tools.

Importantly, AI literacy plays a central role in shaping how students interpret both the benefits and risks associated with GAI use. Students with higher levels of AI literacy may be better equipped to recognise the probabilistic nature of AI-generated content, identify potential biases or inaccuracies, and apply verification strategies when integrating such outputs into academic work (Dijkstra et al., 2022). In contrast, limited AI literacy may increase the likelihood of uncritical reliance on AI systems, amplifying risks related to epistemic authority and cognitive ofloading.

From an adoption perspective, AI literacy does not function solely as an antecedent or outcome of GAI use, but as a mediating and moderating mechanism that conditions the relationship between perceived utility and ethical concerns. When AI literacy is high, students may be more capable of balancing eficiency gains with ethical and epistemic considerations, thereby engaging with GAI tools in a more reflective and selective manner. Conversely, when AI literacy is limited, strong perceptions of utility may dominate decision-making, while ethical risks are normalised or overlooked.

Despite its growing prominence in policy and pedagogical discourse, empirical research examining AI literacy in relation to students’ actual adoption behaviour remains limited. Existing studies often discuss AI literacy normatively or at an institutional level, with fewer investigations exploring how students’ self-reported literacy relates to their willingness to adopt GAI tools or their interpretation of ethical risks. Moreover, AI literacy is frequently examined independently of contextual factors, such as regulatory environments or institutional cultures, which may shape how literacy translates into practice.

In response to these limitations, recent frameworks advocate for integrating AI literacy into broader models of technology adoption and ethical engagement, rather than treating it as a standalone skill set (UNESCO, 2024; Davis et al., 2025). Aligning with this perspective, the present study conceptualises AI literacy as a key mechanism through which students negotiate trade-ofs between perceived educational utility and ethical concerns, while also accounting for the influence of regional educational context. This integrative approach provides a more comprehensive foundation for understanding students’ engagement with generative AI in higher education and directly informs the conceptual model presented in the following subsection.

To ensure conceptual clarity in empirical analysis, AI literacy is analytically distinguished from general familiarity with GAI tools. While familiarity refers primarily to exposure and frequency of use, AI literacy encompasses students’ self-reported understanding of system limitations, awareness of ethical implications, and reported practices of verification. Given the survey design, AI literacy is operationalised as perceived competence rather than objectively tested knowledge, which has implications for the interpretation of the findings.

## 2.4 Conceptual model

Building on prior research on technology adoption, AI literacy, and ethical engagement with artificial intelligence in education, this study is guided by a conceptual model that explains students’ adoption and use of generative AI (GAI) tools as the outcome of an interaction between perceived educational utility, ethical concerns, and AI literacy, situated within distinct regional educational contexts.

At the core of the model is perceived educational utility, which captures students’ assessments of the practical value of GAI tools for learning-related tasks such as accessing information, supporting academic writing, clarifying complex concepts, and enhancing productivity. Consistent with adoption-oriented perspectives, perceived utility is expected to exert a direct positive influence on students’ willingness to use GAI tools in higher education.

In parallel, the model incorporates ethical concerns related to generative AI, including data privacy, algorithmic bias, and overreliance on AI-generated content. While prior normative and policy-oriented literature often assumes that such concerns act as barriers to adoption, emerging empirical findings suggest that ethical awareness does not necessarily translate into behavioural restraint. Accordingly, ethical concerns are modelled as a potential, but not dominant, influence on adoption, allowing for the possibility of weak, indirect, or context-dependent efects.

Crucially, the model integrates AI literacy as a mediating and moderating mechanism linking utility perceptions and ethical awareness to actual adoption behaviour. AI literacy is conceptualised as a multidimensional construct encompassing technical understanding of GAI systems, awareness of ethical and legal implications, and the critical capacity to evaluate and verify AI-generated outputs. Rather than treating ethics and literacy as isolated dimensions, the model assumes that AI literacy shapes how students interpret ethical risks and how they balance those risks against perceived benefits in practice.

Finally, the model explicitly accounts for regional educational context as a contextual moderator influencing the relationships among utility, ethical concerns, AI literacy, and adoption. Europe and Australia are treated not as homogeneous cultural entities, but as higher education environments characterised by diferent regulatory and pedagogical orientations. European contexts are more strongly shaped by formalised data protection and academic integrity frameworks, while Australian contexts are more commonly associated with pragmatic, application-driven approaches to educational innovation. These contextual diferences are expected to condition students’ ethical sensitivity and the extent to which ethical concerns influence adoption decisions.

Taken together, the conceptual model positions students’ engagement with generative AI not as a purely technical or ethical choice, but as a pragmatic negotiation shaped by perceived utility, mediated by AI literacy, and situated within specific regulatory and educational cultures. This framework provides the theoretical foundation for the study’s research questions and hypotheses and guides the interpretation of both quantitative and qualitative findings.

While the model outlines potential mediation and moderation relationships, the present study primarily tests associations between perceived utility, ethical concerns, familiarity, and reported adoption attitudes. The full structural relationships proposed in the model warrant future confirmatory and longitudinal analysis.

## 2.8 Research methodology

This study focuses on the application of GAI tools, particularly LLMs such as Chat-GPT, in higher education learning environments. It explores how these tools contribute to personalised learning, problem-solving support, and real-time feedback. Special attention is given to students’ perceptions, user experiences, and the ethical and technical challenges related to AI integration in education.

## 2.9 Scope, aim and research tasks

The primary aim is to conduct a comprehensive analysis of university students’ perceptions and experiences regarding the use of GAI tools as learning aids. In addition, the study evaluates their perceived impact on the quality of the learning process and identifies potential barriers to efective and ethical implementation. Based on the findings, the research proposes guidelines for the successful and responsible integration of GAI technologies into higher education. This study aims to:

● Analyse students’ perceptions of the benefits and challenges of using GAI tools in academic activities.

● Evaluate the perceived impact of GAI on learning quality and eficiency, particularly in terms of personalised support.

● Identify ethical concerns related to the use of GAI tools, including data privacy, algorithmic bias, and over-reliance on AI-generated outputs.

Develop recommendations for integrating GAI tools, such as ChatGPT, into existing LMS infrastructures at higher education institutions.

● Compare usage patterns across disciplines and regions, with an emphasis on cross-cultural diferences in student attitudes toward GAI.

By addressing these objectives, this study provides insights into how students perceive and utilise GAI tools and similar tools in their educational journeys. The findings aim to inform educators, academic administrators, and policymakers in designing AI-enhanced learning strategies. Moreover, the study highlights best practices for integrating GAI technologies into pedagogical models while maintaining ethical standards and student well-being.

The integration of GAI into higher education presents both opportunities and challenges. While GAI tools ofer students personalised learning support, accessible resources, and interactive problem-solving capabilities, their adoption also raises concerns about academic integrity, data privacy, and potential over-reliance on AI-generated content (Searson et al., 2024). Understanding students’ perceptions of these tools is crucial for their responsible implementation in educational settings.

## 2.10 Research methods used

This study employs a combination of qualitative and quantitative research methods to analyse the role of GAI tools in students’ learning experiences and their integration into existing Learning Management Systems (LMS). The selected methods align with the research objectives, focusing on students’ attitudes, perceived benefits, ethical concerns, and practical applications of GAI in education.

## 2.10.1 Core research methods

1. Theoretical analysis. A comprehensive literature review was conducted to examine existing research on implementing GAI in higher education. This method provided a foundation for understanding previous findings on student engagement, personalised learning, and ethical considerations such as data privacy and algorithmic bias.

2. Descriptive Research Method. A descriptive approach was applied to summarise the data collected through student surveys. This method facilitated the identification of trends in students’ attitudes toward GAI tools, enabling a structured comparison of perceived advantages and challenges across diferent academic disciplines and cultural contexts.

3. Statistical analysis. The study employed quantitative data analysis techniques examine the research questions and hypotheses and identify relationships between key variables. Statistical methods were employed to examine the research questions and hypotheses and to identify patterns and group diferences across key variables:

● ANOVA (analysis of Variance): Used to determine significant diferences in perceptions of GAI tools across various student groups (e.g., IT vs. social sciences).

● Correlation analysis: Applied to measure the relationship between students’ familiarity with GAI tools and their comfort in using them for educational purposes.

● Chi-Square Tests: Used to examine categorical data, particularly related to ethical concerns and adoption rates of AI in education.

Linear Regression: Implemented to explore associations between independent variables (e.g., prior experience with GAI tools) and students’ reported readiness to adopt such tools.

## 2.10.2 Applied research techniques

## ● Survey research

The primary data collection method involved structured online questionnaires designed to capture students’ experiences, attitudes, and behavioural patterns regarding the use of GAI tools. The survey included questions on frequency of use, perceived benefits, ethical concerns, and its role in academic tasks such as research and writing support.

## ● Likert scale measurements

A Likert scale (ranging from 1 to 5) was used to quantify students’ attitudes toward the usefulness of GAI tools in education. Respondents evaluated their comfort level, willingness to integrate AI-driven tools into their learning process, and perceived impact on their academic performance.

## ● Content analysis

Qualitative content analysis was conducted on open-ended survey responses to gain deeper insights into students’ perspectives on the practical applications and potential risks of GAI tools. This technique enabled a richer understanding of individual concerns and experiences beyond predefined survey categories.

## 2.11 Description of the survey instrument

To investigate the application of GAI in education, a structured questionnaire was developed to assess students’ perceptions, experiences, and attitudes regarding GAIbased tools. The complete survey instrument is provided in Appendix A. It consists of four distinct sections, each addressing a specific aspect of GAI usage in higher education.

1. Demographic and Academic Background. Collected data such as students fields of study, year of study, and prior exposure to AI-related courses.

2. Familiarity and Experience with GAI Tools. Evaluated students’ awareness of and engagement with GAI tools.

3. Perceptions of GAI in Education. Questions covered potential benefits, ethical concerns, and recommended strategies for addressing risks.

● Potential benefits – personalised learning, enhanced access to academic resources, and real-time assistance (referring to instant academic support, immediate problem-solving, and live feedback on learning tasks).

● Ethical concerns – data privacy, AI bias, and dependency on AI-generated content.

● Proposed strategies – recommendations for mitigating ethical risks and factors

influencing students’ willingness to use GAI tools in the future.

4. Application Potential and Training Needs. Explored students’ readiness for formal training on GAI tools and interest in learning how to integrate these tools into their educational process. It also includes:

● The perceived impact of GAI on hands-on learning experiences within study programs.

● Students’ willingness to engage in formal training to enhance their understanding and efective use of GAI tools in educational settings.

Before launching the full-scale survey, the questionnaire was pilot tested on a sample of 20 students to evaluate the clarity of the items, the reliability of the instrument, and potential areas for refinement. Reliability analysis was conducted using Cronbach’s α coeficient, with all constructs exceeding the 0.7 threshold, indicating a satisfactory level of internal consistency. The pilot study ensured that the final questionnaire provided valid and reliable data for subsequent analysis.

It is important to note that the study captures students’ perceived ethical concerns and self-reported adoption intentions rather than observed behavioural constraints. The findings, therefore, reflect subjective evaluations, which may not fully correspond to actual usage patterns in institutional contexts.

## 2.12 Research sample

The study involved a total of 788 university students, with 402 participants from various European countries and 386 from Australia. Although participants were drawn from multiple European countries, for the purposes of statistical analysis and crossregional comparison, the data were aggregated into a single European category. This decision was made to ensure a suficient sample size for regional comparison and to maintain analytical consistency between the two contexts (Europe and Australia).

A convenience sampling method was employed to ensure accessibility while capturing a diverse sample across academic disciplines and educational levels. The sample includes students from undergraduate (first to fourth year), master’s (first and second year), and doctoral levels, providing a comprehensive view across diferent stages of higher education. ## 4 Discussion

This study set out to examine patterns in students’ perceptions, ethical considerations, and readiness to adopt generative AI (GAI) tools in higher education across European and Australian contexts. Rather than interpreting the findings in terms of binary hypothesis confirmation, the discussion adopts an interpretative lens aligned with the study’s exploratory and comparative design. The results reveal a consistent tension between perceived educational utility, ethical awareness, and actual adoption behaviour, ofering insight into how students pragmatically negotiate the opportunities and risks associated with GAI use.

## 4.1 Perceived utility as a dominant driver of adoption

Across both regions, perceived educational utility emerges as the most stable and influential factor shaping students’ engagement with GAI tools. Students consistently emphasise accessibility, eficiency, and support for academic tasks such as writing and project work, reinforcing prior survey-based findings that position GAI tools as instrumental academic resources (Wecks et al., 2024b). Importantly, these perceptions persist across disciplines and regions, suggesting that pragmatic learning benefits constitute a shared motivational foundation for adoption.

However, the findings also indicate that perceived utility is not uniformly distributed across all dimensions of use. While accessibility is consistently recognised as a key advantage, other anticipated benefits, such as personalised learning or enhanced convenience, receive weaker empirical support. This nuance challenges overly optimistic narratives surrounding GAI as a transformative learning technology and suggests that students primarily value these tools for task optimisation rather than deep pedagogical innovation. In this sense, adoption reflects pragmatic alignment with academic demands rather than an endorsement of GAI as a comprehensive learning partner.

## 4.2 Ethical awareness and the normalisation of risk

A central contribution of this study lies in its examination of the relationship between ethical concerns and adoption behaviour. The findings indicate that students do acknowledge ethical risks associated with GAI use, including data privacy, algorithmic bias, and overreliance. However, these concerns do not uniformly translate into reduced willingness to adopt GAI tools. Instead, the results may suggest patterns consistent with selective risk normalisation, whereby certain ethical risks appear to be perceived as acceptable trade-ofs in exchange for perceived academic benefits. However, longitudinal evidence would be required to confirm the stability of such processes.

Notably, privacy-related concerns demonstrate a more consistent association with adoption behaviour than concerns related to bias or dependency. This pattern suggests that ethical sensitivity is not absent, but rather diferentiated. Risks that are more tangible, institutionally regulated, or personally consequential appear to carry greater weight in students’ decision-making than abstract or long-term epistemic risks. This finding aligns with qualitative responses in which students articulate awareness of ethical issues while simultaneously expressing confidence in their ability to manage or mitigate them through responsible use. The qualitative responses further suggest that students do not interpret ethical risks as abstract moral dilemmas but as situational trade-ofs embedded in academic pressures. Ethical reasoning, therefore, appears to operate within performance-oriented environments rather than as an independent normative framework.

Rather than indicating ethical disengagement, this pattern reflects a pragmatic ethical stance shaped by institutional norms, assessment pressures, and the perceived inevitability of AI integration in higher education. Ethical reasoning thus appears to function contextually, embedded within everyday academic practices rather than guided solely by normative principles.

## 4.3 Epistemic authority and cognitive ofloading

The limited behavioural impact of ethical concerns raises important questions about shifts in epistemic authority and learning practices. As GAI tools increasingly generate fluent and contextually appropriate outputs, students may recalibrate their trust in traditional academic sources and processes. The findings suggest that many students position GAI tools as legitimate auxiliary knowledge resources, particularly for clarification, drafting, and problem-solving tasks.

This dynamic is closely connected to concerns around cognitive ofloading. While strategic use of GAI tools may support learning by reducing extraneous cognitive load, habitual reliance risks narrowing opportunities for critical engagement and independent reasoning. Importantly, the present findings indicate that students rarely frame their use of GAI in epistemic terms. Instead, adoption decisions are predominantly guided by functional considerations, with limited reflection on how GAI use might reshape knowledge construction or evaluative judgment.

These results underscore the need to interpret student engagement with GAI not as a simple deficit in ethical awareness, but as an adaptive response to evolving academic environments in which eficiency and performance are strongly incentivised. Epistemic risks, while recognised in principle, are often deprioritised in practice.

## 4.4 The role of AI literacy in mediating adoption practices

AI literacy emerges as a critical mechanism shaping how students balance perceived utility and ethical considerations. The comparable levels of AI familiarity across certain disciplines may reflect the difusion of GAI tools beyond traditional technical domains.

As generative AI interfaces are increasingly intuitive and accessible, disciplinary barriers appear to diminish, suggesting that tool usability and platform design may play a more significant role than formal curricular exposure. Students reporting higher familiarity with GAI tools also report greater readiness to adopt them, suggesting that exposure and confidence play an important role in normalising use. However, the findings also indicate that familiarity does not necessarily equate to critical engagement.

This distinction is significant. Without structured opportunities to develop critical and ethical dimensions of AI literacy, increased familiarity may reinforce instrumental use rather than reflective practice. The results therefore support calls for multidimensional AI literacy frameworks that extend beyond technical proficiency to include epistemic evaluation, ethical reasoning, and contextual awareness.

Rather than acting as a simple protective factor, AI literacy appears to mediate how students interpret risks and benefits. In the absence of explicit pedagogical guidance, literacy may facilitate more eficient use without necessarily fostering deeper ethical or epistemic reflection. This insight highlights the importance of embedding AI literacy within curriculum design and assessment practices rather than treating it as an optional or peripheral competence.

## 4.5 Regional context and educational cultures

Although the study does not treat Europe and Australia as homogeneous entities, the comparative analysis reveals meaningful contextual patterns. European students demonstrate greater sensitivity to privacy and data protection concerns, reflecting the influence of regulatory frameworks and institutional discourse around data governance. In contrast, Australian students’ responses suggest a more pragmatic orientation, with ethical considerations exerting a weaker influence on reported adoption behaviour.

These diferences should not be interpreted as fixed cultural traits, but rather as reflections of broader educational and regulatory environments that shape how ethical risks are framed and prioritised. The findings thus reinforce the importance of contextualising GAI adoption within specific institutional and policy settings, rather than assuming universal patterns of ethical engagement.

## 5 Implications

The findings of this study have several implications for theory, educational practice, and policy development in higher education. Importantly, these implications are grounded in observed empirical patterns rather than prescriptive assumptions, reflecting the exploratory and comparative nature of the research.

## 5.1 Theoretical implications

From a theoretical perspective, the study contributes to the growing body of research on generative AI adoption by demonstrating that perceived educational utility, ethical awareness, and AI literacy operate in a non-linear and context-dependent manner. While prior survey-based studies often assume that ethical concerns act as direct barriers to adoption, the present findings suggest a more nuanced relationship characterised by selective risk normalisation. This challenges models that position ethical concern as a uniformly inhibiting factor and highlights the need for theoretical frameworks that account for pragmatic trade-ofs in student decision-making.

The integration of epistemic authority and cognitive ofloading into the interpretation of student adoption behaviour extends existing technology adoption perspectives beyond instrumental use. The findings indicate that students’ engagement with generative AI tools cannot be fully explained through traditional usefulness-oriented models alone. Instead, adoption reflects an adaptive negotiation shaped by performance pressures, institutional norms, and evolving conceptions of legitimate knowledge sources. This insight supports calls for adoption models that explicitly incorporate epistemic dimensions alongside ethical and literacy-related factors.

Furthermore, the study underscores the role of AI literacy as a mediating mechanism rather than a simple antecedent or outcome of AI use. Increased familiarity with generative AI tools appears to facilitate adoption, but does not necessarily promote critical or reflective engagement. This finding refines theoretical understandings of AI literacy by demonstrating that its impact depends on how literacy is conceptualised and embedded within educational contexts.

## 5.2 Practical implications for higher education institutions

The practical implications of the study are closely tied to the finding that students prioritise functional benefits such as accessibility and eficiency over abstract ethical considerations. Given that ethical concerns alone do not consistently deter adoption, institutions should not assume that awareness campaigns or policy statements are sufficient to guide responsible use. Instead, ethical and epistemic considerations need to be operationalised within teaching, assessment, and curriculum design.

Specifically, the finding that privacy-related concerns carry greater weight than other ethical risks suggests that students respond more strongly to risks that are clearly regulated and institutionally enforced. Higher education institutions may therefore achieve greater impact by aligning ethical guidance with concrete academic practices, such as data anonymisation protocols, clear rules regarding the use of AI tools in assessments, and transparent communication about data handling when interacting with large language models.

The results also highlight the need to move beyond generic AI literacy initiatives. While students reporting higher familiarity with GAI tools are more willing to adopt them, this familiarity does not automatically translate into a critical evaluation of AIgenerated content. Institutions should therefore design AI literacy interventions that explicitly address epistemic risks, including verification strategies, bias recognition, and reflective use. Embedding such components into coursework, rather than ofering standalone training sessions, may better align literacy development with students’ actual learning practices. For example, institutions could develop short AI literacy modules integrated into first-year orientation courses, focusing on verification strategies, bias detection, and responsible prompting practices. In parallel, instructors might implement structured AI-reflection assignments requiring students to document and critically evaluate how generative tools were used in their academic work.

## 5.3 Policy and context-sensitive implications

At the policy level, the cross-regional findings underscore the importance of contextsensitive approaches to generative AI governance in higher education. The observed diferences between European and Australian students suggest that regulatory environments and institutional cultures shape how ethical risks are perceived and prioritised. Policies developed in highly regulated contexts may place greater emphasis on data protection and compliance, whereas more flexible environments may prioritise pedagogical innovation and practical integration.

These findings imply that one-size-fits-all policy frameworks are unlikely to be efective. Instead, policymakers should consider how national regulations, institutional norms, and disciplinary cultures interact to influence student behaviour. For example, policies that foreground data protection and academic integrity may resonate more strongly in contexts where such issues are already salient, while policies emphasising responsible innovation and guided experimentation may be more efective in environments oriented toward pragmatic adoption.

Finally, the study highlights the need for closer alignment between institutional policies and students’ lived academic experiences. The persistence of adoption despite acknowledged ethical risks suggests that policies perceived as disconnected from everyday learning practices may have limited influence. Policymakers and institutional leaders should therefore prioritise mechanisms that integrate ethical considerations into routine academic workflows, ensuring that responsible use of generative AI is supported not only in principle, but also in practice.

## 6 Limitations and future research

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged, as they directly shape the interpretation and scope of the findings. First, the study relies on convenience sampling, which constrains the generalisability of the results. Participants self-selected into the survey, and the sample is disproportionately composed of students from Information Technology and Computer Science disciplines. This disciplinary imbalance likely amplifies pragmatic perceptions of generative AI tools and may contribute to the observed normalisation of risks, particularly with respect to eficiency-oriented use. Consequently, the findings should not be interpreted as representative of the broader student population across all academic fields.

Second, the aggregation of data from multiple European countries into a single regional category introduces an additional limitation. While this approach enables cross-regional comparison with Australia, it inevitably obscures intra-regional diversity in educational cultures, institutional policies, and regulatory environments. Differences between Northern and Southern Europe, as well as between EU and non-EU contexts, may influence students’ ethical sensitivity and adoption behaviour in ways that cannot be disentangled within the present design. As such, the regional findings should be understood as indicative of broad contextual tendencies rather than precise cultural distinctions.

Third, the study is based on self-reported data, which introduces the possibility of social desirability bias and retrospective rationalisation. As with all self-reported survey data, the results may also be influenced by social desirability bias and selfenhancement efects. Students may underreport ethically questionable practices or overestimate their level of critical engagement with AI tools. Students’ reported ethical awareness and readiness to adopt generative AI tools may not fully reflect their actual practices, particularly in assessment contexts where AI use is regulated or discouraged. This limitation is especially relevant given the finding that ethical concerns do not consistently translate into behavioural restraint, as reported attitudes may overestimate reflective engagement relative to real-world use.

Finally, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits its ability to capture temporal dynamics in students’ perceptions and practices. Generative AI technologies, institutional policies, and pedagogical norms are evolving rapidly, and students’ ethical reasoning and adoption behaviour are likely to change over time. The present findings therefore provide a snapshot rather than a developmental account of student engagement with generative AI in higher education.

These limitations point to several directions for future research. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine how students’ perceptions, ethical concerns, and actual use of generative AI tools evolve as institutional policies mature and as students gain more sustained experience with these technologies. Given the rapidly evolving nature of generative AI technologies and institutional policies, longitudinal research designs are particularly important to examine whether ethical sensitivity increases, decreases, or stabilises over time as students accumulate experience with GAI tools. Future research would also benefit from stratified sampling strategies that ensure balanced representation across disciplines, allowing for more robust comparison of epistemic and ethical orientations beyond technically oriented fields. Additionally, disaggregated regional analyses within Europe could provide deeper insight into how specific regulatory and cultural contexts shape student engagement with generative AI. Finally, mixed-method designs that integrate behavioural data, classroom observations, or learning analytics with self-reported measures could ofer a more comprehensive understanding of how ethical awareness and AI literacy translate into practice.

## 7 Conclusion

This study examined students’ perceptions, ethical considerations, and readiness to adopt generative AI (GAI) tools in higher education, drawing on a comparative analysis of European and Australian contexts. Rather than approaching adoption as a purely technical or ethical decision, the findings highlight how students pragmatically negotiate perceived educational benefits and associated risks within their academic environments.

Across both regions, perceived educational utility emerges as a consistent driver of engagement with GAI tools, particularly in relation to accessibility, eficiency, and support for academic tasks. While students demonstrate awareness of ethical concerns such as privacy, bias, and overreliance, these concerns do not uniformly translate into reduced willingness to adopt GAI tools. Instead, the results may indicate patterns consistent with selective risk normalisation, whereby certain risks appear to be deprioritised in favour of perceived academic benefits. Given the cross-sectional design, these interpretations should be understood as indicative rather than conclusive.

Importantly, the study contributes to existing literature by moving beyond binary assumptions about ethical concern as a barrier to adoption. The findings indicate that ethical awareness, AI literacy, and adoption behaviour interact in context-dependent ways, shaped by institutional norms, regulatory environments, and students’ familiarity with GAI systems. Diferences observed between European and Australian students further underscore the importance of situating student engagement with generative AI within specific educational and policy contexts, rather than assuming homogeneous patterns of use.

The contribution of this study lies in its empirically grounded insight into how students balance eficiency, responsibility, and epistemic judgment when engaging with generative AI tools. By framing adoption as a pragmatic negotiation rather than a linear ethical decision, the study ofers a more nuanced understanding of student behaviour in AI-mediated learning environments.

These conclusions should be interpreted in light of the study’s methodological constraints, including its cross-sectional design and sampling strategy. Nevertheless, the findings provide a timely foundation for future research that seeks to examine how students’ perceptions and practices evolve as generative AI technologies, institutional policies, and educational norms continue to develop. Longitudinal and contextsensitive studies will be essential for advancing understanding of responsible and sustainable integration of generative AI in higher education.
