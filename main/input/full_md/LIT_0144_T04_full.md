## 1 Introduction

published online November 3, 2025

Abstract: This study aims to understand the factors influencing academic integrity in the age of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) through the lens of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). A seven-factor measurement model was developed, hypothesizing the relationship between TPB constructs – e.g., perceived behavioral control (PBC), past behavior (PB), moral obligations (MO), social norms (SN), information literacy (IL)—and GenAI adoption. It was tested using structural equation modeling (SEM). The findings showed that both PBC (β = 0.223, CR = 4.234, p=<0.05) and SN (β = 0.508, CR = 5.644, p=<0.05) significantly increased behavioral intention. MO (β = -0.273, CR = -4.234, p=<0.05) and IL (β = -0.253, CR = -3.386, p=<0.05) helped uphold academic integrity. A strong association was found between behavioral intention (BI) and GenAI adoption (AD) for academic dishonesty (β = 0.651, CR = 11.780, p = 0.000). The findings suggest that protecting academic integrity needs a multitiered approach, including implementing AI literacy programs, research, and critical thinking training, implementing strong plagiarism detection tools, developing and maintaining comprehensive and adaptable guidelines for the ethical use of GenAI, citation management training, and cultivating a culture valuing academic honesty and originality.

Keywords: academic misconduct; generative artificial intelligence (GenAI); ChatGPT; AI literacy; academic integrity; ethical implications

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI), like AI chatbots, uses large volumes of text data to generate human-like content. This is possible due to its generative pre-trained transformer (GPT) architecture (Akbar et al 2023). These models are trained on massive datasets. They are optimized for specific natural language processing (NLP) tasks like language synthesis and summarization. ChatGPT, a popular GenAI tool, has grown rapidly, reaching over 100 million users, making it one of the fastest-growing applications (Van Bulck and Moons 2024). The rise of ChatGPT and similar LLMs has sparked interest in their capabilities and impacts. Researchers and developers are exploring their potential benefits and risks.

Generative AI (GenAI) is transforming every sector, including higher education and healthcare. AI is already improving healthcare delivery, paving the way for medical education (Kamal 2024). Numerous discussions and predictions of AI in medical education have focused on its potential capabilities, applications, and concerns (Civaner et al. 2022; Watson 2024). In nursing education, AI can enhance training with personalized, data-driven learning. AI prepares nurses for an evolving, tech-driven healthcare system. Taira et al (2023) investigated ChatGPT’s performance in passing the National Nursing Exam in Japan from 2019 to 2023. The findings indicated that ChatGPT scored 80 % in 2019 and passed all subsequent years’ exams. However, a high success rate of ChatGPT on standardized exams does not show that AI use can supplement nursing education by providing access to required theoretical and practical information. It is essential to consider the limitations of AI in replicating the critical thinking and clinical decision-making skills essential for nurses (Taira et al 2023).

The objective ofthe study is to determine the factors that influence behavioral intentions to adopt generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) tools for academic dishonesty.

## 2 Literature Review

The use of generative AI tools in nursing education has multiple challenges such as concerns over academic integrity, the possibility of plagiarism and copyright violations, and the potential for disseminating inaccurate information.

The excessive reliance on AI generative tools may significantly impede students’ problem-solving and critical thinking skills (Chen and Chen 2023). However, AI generative tools can supplement but not replace these critical skills. While ChatGPT is extensively trained on a massive amounts of data, it is important to acknowledge the potential for errors in its training process (Lee 2023). Because the training data itself may contain incorrect information, there is a risk that the system may be unable to deliver the latest and most accurate information, thus compromising its reliability (Gupta et al. 2023). Therefore, relying solely on generative AI information for clinical decisions is potentially dangerous at this point when AI system is evolving and learning.

Moreover, students’ use of generative AI tools for assignment writing raises concerns about academic integrity. There are multiple factors that contribute to students’ plagiarism practices. For example, intentional plagiarism occurs when students experience time constraints and perceive pressure to complete an academic task (Fischer and Zigmond 2011; Azam and Naeem 2022). Some other risks and limitations associated with the use of AI generative tools are ethical issues, dubious accuracy, and citation issues (De Gagne et al. 2023). In conclusion, integrating GenAI into education has the potential to significantly improve learning outcomes (Irwin et al 2023). To maximize the benefits ofGenAI while maintaining educational standards, scope, and relevance, it is essential to integrate these tools after careful consideration, taking into account both the opportunities and challenges presented by GenAI (Sallam 2023). It demands developing well-defined institutional policies, the provision of clear instructions to students on the responsible use of GenAI, and the implementation of detective AI tools (Borsari et al. 2018; Ko and Song 2024). Thus, in order for successful and ethical integration, it is important to understand students’ perspectives on GenAI tools adoption, as well as factors that influence their misuse of GenAI in completing educational tasks.

Researchers have developed numerous theoretical frameworks and models to understand the adoption and usage behavior of information technology and systems. These models and theories include “the difusion of innovation theory” that explains how new ideas, technologies, or products spread through a population over time (Rogers 1995). The motivational model primarily focuses on human motivation in general and understanding these motivations can help explain why some individuals are more eager to adopt new technologies than others (Maslow 1943). The social cognitive theory suggests that individuals learn to use and adopt new technologies by observing others, interacting with social networks, and through social reinforcement (Bandura 1991). The model of personal computer utilization focuses on factors that influence individuals’ use of personal computers (Thompson et al 1991). Innovation difusion theory focuses on the spread of innovations within social systems (Ryan and Gross 1943). The theory of reasoned action suggests that intentions to use technology are influenced by attitudes towards technology and social norms regarding its use (Ajzen and Fishbein 1977). Technology acceptance model (TAM) emphasizes key factors, such as perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, that influence technology acceptance (Devis 1989). The unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) suggests that factors including performance expectance, efort expectancy, social influence etc., influence technology acceptance and use (Venkatesh et al. 2003). It was developed by Venkatesh et al. (2003), and its extension, UTAUT2 (Venkatesh et al 2012), which includes constructs such as hedonic motivation, price value, and habit, provides a comprehensive framework for understanding new technology adoption behavior.

In 1977, Ajzen and Fishbein introduced the theory of reasoned action, which claims that an individual’s intentions to engage in a particular behavior is determined by their attitude towards the behavior and subjective norms. This theory was expanded by incorporating perceived behavioral control, leading to the development of the theory of planned behavior (TPB), According to the TPB, understanding behavior requires examining the intentions, which are influenced by three key factors: attitudes towards the behavior, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control (Ajzen 1985). Previously, several studies have successfully applied the TPB to understand technological adoption behavior (Yi et al. 2006; Cronan and Al-Rafee 2008; Alwahaishi and Snášel 2013; Hsiao and Tang 2014). Although TPB has been applied to study various behavioral domains, its specific application to understanding the factors influencing academic integrity within the context of GenAI tools adoption represents a novel contribution to the literature. Therefore, recognizing the applicability ofthis framework in the context ofthis study, we adopted the extended theoretical framework incorporating key constructs from the TPB – perceived behavioral control (PBC), moral obligations (MO), past behavior (PB), and subjective norms (SN) – along with an additional construct: information literacy (IL) (as shown in Figure 1).

The relationships between the TPB constructs (e.g., past behavior, moral obligations) and behavioral intention to adopt AI were hypothesized based on the relevant literature (Yang et al. 2021). It also supported the research model’s predicted positive/negative relationships (Figure 1).

Perceived Behavioral Control (PBC) is how easy or hard someone thinks a behavior is. People with high PBC believe they can do the behavior well. They also expect fewer obstacles in their way. In contrast, low PBC means a person doubts their ability to perform the action (Ajzen and Fishbein 1977; Conner and Armitage 1998; Yi et al. 2006). The literature indicate that individuals with higher PBC are more likely to form strong intentions to carry a behavior (Ajzen 1991; Morris and Venkatesh 2000; de Andrés-Sánchez and Gené-Albesa 2023). Students with higher PBC may perceive it easier to use GenAI to complete academic assignments without proper acknowledgement. This PBC could lead to a stronger intention to misuse GenAI tools for academic purposes. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

Moral obligation (MO) refers to an individual’s subjective sense of responsibility to either participate in or avoid a specific activity (Ajzen 1991). Moral reasoning is the cognitive process through which individuals diferentiate between morally right and wrong actions in a particular situation (Rest et al 1997). The revised version ofthe theory of planned behavior incorporates “moral obligation” as an important construct. This modification enhances the theory’s efectiveness in predicting dishonesty in academia (Rujoiu and Rujoiu 2014).

Thus, individuals with a strong sense ofmoral obligation are likely to perceive the misuse of GenAI tools as ethically wrong (Yang et al. 2021), thereby reducing their intention to engage in such behavior (Newell and Dahlenburg 2024). This aligns with the theory of planned behavior (TPB), which suggests that moral obligation can act as a deterrent to unethical actions (Ajzen 1991; Rujoiu and Rujoiu 2014). Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2: Moral obligations significantly and negatively influence the behavioral intention to adopt GenAI tools for academic dishonesty.

Past behavior (PB) pertains to an individual’s prior activities or conduct in a comparable circumstance. It is regarded as a significant predictor of future actions (Ajzen 1991). Past actions can shape an individual’s intentions and probability of repeating a behavior. Examining an individual’s past performance provides insight into their potential future behavior. Few studies have reported past behavior as a significant predictor of indulging in academic dishonesty (Harding et al. 2007; Imran and Nordin 2013). It suggests that individuals who have engaged in academic dishonesty in the past are more likely to do so again.

Thus, individuals who have previously misused technology or engaged in academic dishonesty are more likely to form intentions to repeat such behavior, as past actions create a behavioral precedent. This is consistent with the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), which acknowledges the role of habit and past behavior in shaping intentions (Ajzen 1991). Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H3: Past behavior significantly and positively influences the behavioral intention to misuse GenAI tools for academic dishonesty.

Subjective norms (SN) refer to the societal standards of individuals regarding a particular action, as influenced by others. Students who think that social norms encourage cheating are more likely to engage in cheating compared to other students (Ajzen 1985). This notion, also sometimes referred to as injunctive norms, reflects the perception of other people’s approval or disapproval. The literature indicated that peer-related factors have a significant impact on behavioral intention (de Andrés-Sánchez and Gené-Albesa 2023). McCabe, Trevino and Butterfield’s (2002) study of nine colleges found that peer-related contextual factors have a significant influence on students’ behavior. Thus, if students perceive that their peers or social groups engage in misusing GenAI tools, they are more likely to do so. This notion aligns with the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), which posits that subjective norms are a key determinant of behavioral intentions. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4: Subjective norms significantly and positively influence the behavioral intention to adopt GenAI tools for academic dishonesty.

According to the Association of College and Research Libraries (ACRL) (2016), information literacy (IL) includes the cognitive skills necessary to recognize, locate, evaluate, and efectively utilize information. Students with low information literacy tend to passively consume information from the Internet. A deficiency in critical thinking skills can also be linked to poor IL. It can result in poor information seeking behavior and can also increase the likelihood of engaging in academic dishonesty through the misuse of technology such as GenAI (Khan et al 2021; Islam et al 2022). The literature suggests that IL is a strong predictor of behavioral intention (Lund et al 2023). Individuals with IL skills are more able to adopt, use, and utilize GenAI tools responsibly and efectively than those with poor information literacy skills (Jones-Jang et al 2019; Pennycook et al. 2021). They are also better equipped to evaluate the ethical implications of using GenAI tools and are less likely to misuse them (Lund et al 2023). Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H5: Information literacy significantly and negatively influences the behavioral intentions to adopt GenAI tools for academic dishonesty.

Behavioral intention (BI) is a key concept in theories of human behavior, such as the theory of planned behavior (TPB). It refers to how likely a person is to perform a specific action (Ajzen 1991; Venkatesh et al. 2003; Venkatesh 2022). According to TPB, behavioral intention strongly influences the adoption and use of GenAI (Andrews et al 2021; Bilquise et al 2023). Thus, if students have a firm behavioral intention to misuse GenAI tools, they are likelier to adopt them for academic dishonesty. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis, which is aligned with the constructs of the TPB and provides empirical support for the relationship between behavioral intention and adoption (Ajzen 1991; Venkatesh et al. 2003; Venkatesh 2022).

H6: Behavioral intention to misuse generative AI (GenAI) tools has a significant and positive influence on the adoption of GenAI for academic dishonesty.

## 3 Methodology

## 3.1 Participants and Procedures

A cross-sectional survey design was employed to collect data from the participants. The study participants included enrolled students in the Bachelor ofScience in Nursing (BSN)

program at the four participating institutions in the South Punjab region of Pakistan. The study included: College of Nursing, Islamia University of Bahawalpur; KIMS Mustafa Kamal Institute of Nursing and Medical Sciences, Vehari; New Life Institute of Nursing, Multan; and College of Nursing, Bahawalpur.

A cross-sectional survey was used. It collected data on factors influencing GenAI use for academic dishonesty. The collected data is used to test the relationship based on the theory of planned behavior (TPB). The nursing population was selected for several reasons, including that nursing education increasingly uses AI for learning and skill development. Nursing requires high ethical standards due to its impact on patient care. Thus, insights from nursing students can provide broader implications for academic integrity across various disciplines, including social sciences, sciences, and the arts.

The South Punjab region has a diverse socio-cultural background and has well-established nursing institutions, which is why it was selected.

## 3.2 Research Tool

The questionnaire was developed based on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and generative AI adoption literature (Ostick et al. 2025; Park et al 2024; Topaz et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2021). It had three sections. The first section collected data on demographic information, which included gender, age, institution type (public/private), and program year. The second section ofthe questionnaire was based on statements on seven constructs adopted from the TPB (Figure 1). For example, the construct “perceived behavioral control” was measured using five statements, and “moral obligations” also comprised of five statements. The sub-scale on the construct “past behavior” comprised of six statements, “subjective norms” contained three statements, the construct “information literacy” consisted of five measurable statements, and “behavioral intention” included four measurable statements. The third part of the questionnaire included questions measuring participants’ perceptions and behavioral intentions towards “generative AI tools adoption.”

## 3.3 Validity and Reliability Measures

The questionnaire was pre-tested by two academicians in the field of nursing, including a nursing practitioner. The proposed changes were incorporated into the scale such as removing double barreled questions, avoiding technical jargon, and shufling a few questions from one sub-scale to another. Following these changes, the questionnaire was pilot tested on a cohort of thirty nursing students to assess its reliability and clarity in a real-setting. Feedback from the pilot phase confirmed the questionnaire’s appropriateness for the target population after a few minor adjustments to working and formatting based on participant suggestions.

Cronbach’s alpha was applied to measure the questionnaire’s internal consistency of each subscale and reliability, which provided a score of 0.898 for the thirty-two items across seven constructs, indicating the questionnaire’s good reliability. The Cronbach’s alpha coeficients values for each construct estimated in this study are reported in Table 1.

The Cronbach’s alpha values for three measures fall within the 0.6 range, which is considered acceptable for exploratory research (Streiner et al 2024; Streiner 2003).

Another area of focus was univariate and multivariate normality assessment. Assessing normality is crucial in SEM analysis. It impacts parameter estimates, standard errors, and hypothesis testing. We checked skewness and kurtosis using maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to assess normality. All variables met skewness criteria (<3) (Kline 2023), and kurtosis values were also acceptable (<7). Multivariate kurtosis was within limits (<5) (Yuan et al 2005). The findings suggest reasonable symmetry. Both univariate and multivariate normality assumptions were met (West et al 1995).

## 3.4 Sample Size Calculation and Method

We used purposive sampling to select participants from the participating colleges. During participant recruitment, respondents were explicitly asked: “Have you ever used AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT, Gemini, DeepSeek) for academic assignments?” Only those participants who answered “Yes” were included in the final sample, which ensured the recruitment of participants who had direct experience with AI-assisted academic work. Moreover, to select the minimum required sample size, we used a-Priori Sample Size Calculator (Soper 2020). An average efect size of 0.3 (small: 0.1, medium: 0.3, large: 0.5) was set while calculating the sample size. Moreover, considering the model’s complication (seven constructs and 32 observed variables), a preferred statistical power of 0.95 (conventionally, >0.80) was used (Cohen 1988), as well as an alpha coeficient value set at 0.05. We calculated a minimum sample size of 212 participants. To ensure suficient statistical power and minimize the margin of error, we continued data collection beyond the minimum sample size of 212 participants.

## 3.5 Data Collection Process

The questionnaire was administered in English, which is the de facto oficial language of Pakistan and the medium of instruction in the participating institutions. There were more than 1,500 nursing students enrolled in the participating institutions. In total, 600 copies of the questionnaire along with the consent form to participate in the study were administered among participants between June 2024 and August 2024. A combination of online and paper-based questionnaires was used to collect the data. A five-point Likert type scale (from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree) was used to obtain the responses. The researchers personally visited the participating colleges to collect the data. The heads of participating colleges served as study facilitators, assisting in the process of data collection at their respective institutions. The participation of the respondents and study facilitators was voluntary.

Regarding the response rate, out of the 600 questionnaires distributed, 331 were returned (55.16 % return rate). Ofthese, 314 responses were valid for data analysis, resulting in a response rate of 52.33 %. Seventeen questionnaires were excluded due to missing data or multiple responses to singleanswer questions. The response rate was boosted by three follow-up requests, each fifteen days apart.

## 3.6 Data Analysis Procedure

The data was analyzed using “IBM’s Statistical Package for Social Sciences” (SPSS v26 by IBM) and Analysis of Moment Structure (AMOS). An alpha level of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance for hypothesis testing. Demographic information is presented as frequencies, percentages, mean, and standard deviation. The proposed conceptual model, based on the Theory of Planned Behavior (Figure 1), was tested using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) (Figure 2).

## 3.7 Ethical Approval

The study strictly adhered to established ethical guidelines for research involving human participants. All procedures, including data collection, participant recruitment, and confidentiality protocols, complied with institutional and international ethical standards.

Of the 314 respondents, the majority of respondents (62.1 % or 195) were female, while (37.9 % or 119) were male. The majority of respondents fell within the 18–25 age range. Specifically, 147 respondents (46.8 %) were aged 21–25, and 138 (43.9 %) were under 20. Only twenty-nine respondents (9.2 %) were aged 26–30. ## 5 Discussion

The theory of planned behavior (TPB) provides a comprehensive framework to understand students’ adoption of novel technologies like generative AI tools in healthcare settings. Previously several studies adopted TPB to explain technological adoption behavior (Ajzen 1985; Morris and Venkatesh 2000; Yi et al. 2006; Harding et al. 2007; Cronan and Al-Rafee 2008; Alwahaishi and Snášel 2013; Wu et al 2017). Recognizing the importance of understanding students’ intentions behind their use of GenAI (Yang et al. 2021), particularly the factors that may influence their misuse for academic dishonesty, this study shifts its focus beyond previous research, which has primarily concentrated on the adoption of GenAI tools.

## 5.1 Implications of the Study

Our study has strong implications for academicians and librarians to play a vital role in addressing the root causes of plagiarism and potentially unethical behavior, as well as promoting a culture of ethical practices within nursing education. The literature strongly supports the notion that academic staf’s commitment to upholding academic honesty plays an important role in reducing a culture of cheating and academic dishonesty (Dench and Joyce 2022). Moreover, research showed that student perception of faculty commitment to academic integrity influences their own behavior (Godin and Kok 1996). As a result, students are less likely to cheat when they perceive a high risk of detection and consequence; similarly, when they think their teachers take academic dishonesty seriously, they are more likely to adopt fair academic practices themselves. Therefore, there is a need to introduce evidence-based interventions that could influence behavioral intentions of nursing students towards academic integrity.

Our findings revealed that the majority of the nursing students possess a good set of information literacy skills, which is good in terms ofrecognizing information needs and seeking behavior as well as using information ethically and responsibly. Previous research also supports the efectiveness of information literacy skills in reducing plagiarism (Jiang et al 2013). Librarians have an obligation to equip nursing students with critical evaluation skills to assess sources’ credibility (Goodsett and Schmillen 2022), efective research strategies to find and cite reliable information sources eficiently and ethically (Redmond et al. 2020), the use of citation management tools such as Mendeley, Endnote, Zotero, etc. to streamline the citation process and minimize unintentional plagiarism (Rabasa and Abrizah 2022), and plagiarism detecting services, so as to maintain academic integrity in the age of AI generative tools (Liu et al. 2023).

Moreover, there is a need for academicians and librarians to conduct educational initiatives such as workshops or seminars specifically aimed at influencing students’ behavior regarding plagiarism (attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control) and promoting positive attitudes towards ethical practices in nursing education along with introducing strategies for time management and coping with peer pressure, stress, and fear of failure.

Therefore, there is a need that librarians of the participating nursing colleges should develop LibGuides or online research guides specifically tailored to nursing students, containing resources on ethical research, guidelines on citation styles, and plagiarism detective tools and preventive strategies.

## 5.2 Limitations of the Study

The researchers acknowledge a few limitations of the study, such as the data for this study being collected through a survey. The self-reported data through surveys does have always limitations. In the context of this study, participating students might have responded in a way that they think is socially acceptable. For example, they might have underestimated instances of cheating or overstating their moral obligations. It is also possible that they forget specific instances of cheating or are unable to recall past behavior, especially if it happened some time ago. They might have interpreted the question statement diferently, leading to inconsistencies in responses.

Furthermore, some survey items may have been subject to varying interpretations. While these limitations exist, we tried to minimize them by ensuring respondent anonymity in the questionnaire to encourage honest responses. Moreover, the questionnaire was pilot-tested with a small sample of the target population to refine item clarity and reviewed by two experts for content validity. The questionnaire’s reliability was further examined using Cronbach’s alpha.

The generalizability of the findings can be noted as a limitation due to diferences in contexts, demographic factors, availability of infrastructure, policy guidelines, ethical considerations, and level of AI literacy. Therefore, the findings of the study should be generalized carefully. In order to gain a broader perspective and enhance the generalization, a cross-country study is recommended.

Another limitation may be of measuring and reporting perceived behavioral intentions towards the misuse of GenAI tools for academic dishonesty, rather than attempting to directly observe actual intentional or unintentional misuse. Therefore, the findings primarily reflect participants’ perceived intentions, which require further validation through empirical observations and may not perfectly predict actual behavior.

It is also acknowledged that the statements in the survey are not specific to nursing students. In fact, the same survey could be run with other groups (medical students, law students etc.) to generate interesting comparative insights which could also help contextualize the results and draw conclusions.

## 5.3 Novelty and Originality

This study makes a novel contribution to the literature by investigating the factors that influence students’ behavioral intention to adopt generative AI (GenAI) tools for academic dishonesty. While previous research has primarily focused on the adoption of GenAI tools, this study shifts its focus to examine the role of perceived behavioral control, moral obligations, past behavior, and subjective norms in shaping students’ intention to misuse GenAI tools for academic dishonesty. Furthermore, it explores the subsequent impact of this intention on the adoption of GenAI tools.

## 6 Conclusion

This study developed a seven-factor GenAI adoption model grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) to examine its relationship with academic dishonesty among nursing students. The findings indicate that perceived behavioral control (PBC) and subjective norms (SN) strongly predict behavioral intentions, while moral obligations (MO) and information literacy (IL) serve as protective factors for academic integrity. Moreover, behavioral intentions (BI) were found to directly correlate with generative AI tools adoption (AD) for academic dishonesty, highlighting the need for proactive institutional responses.

To bridge the gap between ethical principles and practice, evident in our contradictory findings (e.g., 74 % valuing honesty vs. 54 % admitting to plagiarism), we propose a locally tailored, multi-tiered interventions. These include: (i) AI literacy programs co-developed by academics and librarians, focusing on ethical use, citation management, and critical thinking; (ii) faculty-librarian partnerships to embed integrity training into curricula, promoting librarians’ expertise in information ethics; and (iii) stress-reduction initiatives (e.g., time management workshops) to mitigate situational pressures driving misconduct.

To extend this work, we recommend cross-disciplinary validation of the GenAI adoption model (e.g., medicine, engineering) to assess universal versus field-specific predictors. The study also suggests that experimental studies should be carried out to measure the efectiveness of AI literacy interventions in reducing academic dishonesty. There is also a need for policy analysis examining how institutional rules (e.g., grading incentives, GenAI bans) shape misconduct rates.
