## ABSTRACT

Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a transformative tool in academic writing, leveraging advanced algorithms and natural language processing to significantly improve efficiency, quality and productivity. This study investigates the use of AI tools among Indonesian doctoral students, with a particular focus on ethical standards and their impact on critical thinking. Adopting a phenomenological approach, the research involved 81 participants who provided data through open-ended questionnaires, which were analysed thematically. The findings reveal that AI tools—such as ChatGPT, Deepl, Zotero, Scite AI, Connected Papers and Humata—are extensively used for generating ideas, brainstorming, data analysis, drafting, grammar correction, translation and literature management. While students acknowledge the advantages of AI in enhancing speed and efficiency, they express concerns regarding its ethical implications and potential impact on critical thinking. Key ethical issues include maintaining academic integrity, ensuring manual verification of AI-generated content and avoiding overreliance. The study underscores that, although AI facilitates idea generation and technical tasks, it may undermine critical thinking and analytical skills. To uphold ethical standards and preserve intellectual rigour, the study advocates for a balanced use of AI, positioning it as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, critical scholarly practices.

## 1 | Introduction

The concerns raised by the Ministry of Research, Technology, and Higher Education (Kemenristekdikti) over the low number of research publications in reputable international journals prompted significant policy interventions. According to the Science and Technology Index (Sinta) from the Ministry of Education, Culture, Research, and Technology, Indonesia's scientific publications from 2008 to 2015 did not exceed 10,000 articles (Amsal 2023). To address this issue, Kemenristekdikti introduced policies aimed at increasing the number of publications in high-impact journals. To qualify for a professorship, lecturers must meet stringent requirements, including holding a doctoral degree (S-3) and publishing scholarly works as the primary author in reputable international journals. However, the administrative pressure to publish has led to various forms of academic misconduct.

Indonesian lecturers recorded 7639 Scopus-indexed articles published in 2015 and 9454 articles in 2016 (Syahputra 2024). Furthermore, in the period 2019–2021, the number increased to approximately 50,000 articles. It brings Indonesia as the leading

ASEAN country in terms of scientific output (Amsal  2023). However, this surge in publications has raised concerns about the quality and integrity of the research. Many lecturers may have resorted to shortcuts to meet the publication demands, leading to issues in scholarly publications. Over the past 5 years, the number of retracted or withdrawn journal articles by Indonesian authors has increased significantly, from 13 articles in 2020–2021 to 27 articles in 2023–2024. These retractions were attributed to various reasons, including publisher errors, investigations, self-retractions, duplicate articles, authorship issues, plagiarism, publication in another journal, fake peer reviews, and reference or attribution problems (Adyatama et al. 2024).

The rapid increase in scientific publications in Indonesia can be largely attributed to the integration of technology, particularly artificial intelligence (AI) into academic writing and research practices. The adoption of AI tools has significantly transformed the landscape of scholarly communication, offering both opportunities and challenges that impact academic integrity and the quality of research outputs. AI-powered writing assistants, such as OpenAI's ChatGPT, have revolutionised how researchers conduct literature reviews, draft manuscripts and disseminate knowledge by facilitating the generation of coherent text and streamlining the writing process, thus enhancing productivity (Rahman et al. 2023). It can reduce the time for writing projects and user-friendly nature (Al-Sofi  2024), improved writing skills, and facilitated multilingual and interactive support (Shofiah et al. 2023), so that the convenience provided by AI ignites the intention to adopt AI in their projects (Wang and Reynolds 2024; Wu et al. 2024). However, the works of AI raise critical concerns about the authenticity and originality of scholarly work. Besides, it contained notable mistakes and inaccuracies (Safrai and Orwig  2024) and was not always able to integrate the given information to form meaningful connections between concepts (Kacena et al. 2024).

The ability of AI to produce human-like text can blur the lines between original authorship and machine-generated content, leading to potential ethical dilemmas in academic publishing (Sallam 2023; Schmidt and Meir 2024). For example, the use of AI in generating research papers may inadvertently introduce inaccuracies or superficial content, which could compromise the quality of scholarly discourse (Zielinski et al. 2023). Moreover, reliance on AI tools can foster an over-dependence that may undermine the development of essential research and writing skills among students and early-career researchers.

As noted by Rahman et al. (2023), while AI can assist in various aspects of academic writing, it cannot replace the depth of critical thinking and nuanced analysis required for high-quality scholarly contributions. This shift necessitates a re-evaluation of pedagogical approaches in higher education to ensure that students are equipped with the analytical skills necessary to engage critically with their research (Lin 2023). The challenge lies in harnessing AI's capabilities responsibly, ensuring that it serves as a supplementary tool rather than a crutch that diminishes the quality and integrity of academic work (Schmidt and Meir 2024; Thomas et al. 2023).

Furthermore, the academic community is actively grappling with the implications of AI's integration into scholarly publishing.

The establishment of uniform policies regarding the use of AI in academic publishing is crucial for fostering trust and credibility within the scholarly community (Lund and Naheem 2023). Many leading journals are currently developing guidelines to address the ethical use of AI, emphasising the importance of transparency in disclosing AI's involvement in the research process (DeVilbiss and Roberts 2023; Flanagin et al. 2023). The need for robust ethical guidelines is underscored by the potential for AI-generated content to introduce biases or inaccuracies into published literature, which could have far-reaching consequences for the integrity of scientific communication (Wright et al. 2024; Zielinski et al. 2023).

The ongoing dialogue surrounding AI's impact on academic writing also highlights the necessity for continuous research and policy development to uphold academic standards while leveraging technological advancements effectively. As the landscape of scholarly publishing evolves, institutions must prioritise the cultivation of critical thinking, scholarly rigour and ethical conduct in their educational outcomes (Sullivan et  al.  2023). Balancing innovation with the preservation of core academic values is essential to ensure that the benefits of AI integration do not come at the expense of academic integrity (Pividori and Greene 2023; Zielinski et al. 2023).

This research seeks to explore human-AI collaboration in academic contexts, focusing specifically on the experiences and perspectives of doctoral students in Indonesia. It aims to address the following research questions: (1) What AI tools are frequently used by doctoral students, and what are their perspectives on the use of AI in scholarly writing? (2) What are the experiences of doctoral students in Indonesia with AI-assisted writing? (3) How do ethical principles, critical thinking and scholarly rigour influence the use of AI-assisted writing among doctoral students in Indonesia? By examining these questions, this study contributes to the ongoing dialogue about the role of AI in academic writing, highlighting the importance of balancing technological innovation with the preservation of core academic values.

## 2    |    Method

## 2.1    |    Research Design

This study employs a qualitative research method, specifically utilising a phenomenological approach as outlined by Creswell and Creswell  (2018). Phenomenology is particularly suited for exploring the lived experiences and perceptions of individuals, making it an appropriate choice for understanding how doctoral students interact with and perceive the use of AI-assisted writing tools in their academic work. By focusing on the shared experiences of the participants, this approach seeks to uncover the essence of their engagement with AI, highlighting the commonalities and differences in their perspectives.

## 2.2 | Participants

The study involved 81 participants, all of whom share the same characteristic of being doctoral students in educational-related programmes. These participants were selected to provide a comprehensive understanding of how AI tools are being used in academic writing within this specific demographic. Their insights are valuable in exploring the ethical considerations, challenges and benefits associated with AI in the context of doctoral education.

## 2.3 | Instrument and Data Collections

Data were collected through open-ended questions distributed via Google Forms. This method was chosen to allow participants the flexibility to express their thoughts and experiences in detail, providing rich qualitative data for analysis. The researcher reached out to participants through WhatsApp groups associated with various scholarship programmes, including the S3 LPDP (Ministry of Finance), BPI (Indonesian Education Scholarships—Ministry of Education and Culture—Ministry of Finance) and BIB (Indonesia Rising Scholarships—Ministry of Religious Affairs—Ministry of Finance). This method of recruitment ensured a diverse yet relevant sample of doctoral students who are engaged in advanced academic work.

## 2.4 | Data Analysis

The data collected from the open-ended questionnaires were analysed using thematic analysis, a qualitative method that identifies, analyses and reports patterns (themes) within the data. Thematic analysis was selected due to its flexibility and capacity to provide a rich, detailed and nuanced account of participants' experiences. This method aligns with the phenomenological approach adopted in the study, enabling an in-depth exploration of participants' perspectives on the use of AI tools in academic writing. The thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke  (2006) and Braun et  al.  (2023) six-phase framework, a widely recognised and systematic approach for conducting qualitative data analysis:

1. Familiarisation with the data: The researchers immersed themselves in the data by repeatedly reading the participants' responses to gain a comprehensive understanding of the content.

2. Generating initial codes: Meaningful segments of data relevant to the research questions were systematically coded. The coding process was conducted inductively, allowing themes to emerge from the data rather than being predetermined. To enhance the reliability and validity of the coding process, inter-coder reliability was established. Two researchers independently grouped quotes that expressed the same or similar things in each answer to the research question, then formed a code, followed by the discussion to reconcile any differences. Based on these discussions, the coding scheme was refined to ensure consistency and accuracy. For instance, responses indicating ‘using ChatGPT for brainstorming’ were coded as ‘AI for idea generation’.

3. Searching for themes: The generated codes were organised into potential themes that captured recurring patterns across the dataset. An iterative and reflexive approach was adopted, with two researchers independently identifying preliminary themes before collaboratively refining them. Related codes, such as ‘AI for idea generation’, ‘AI for grammar correction’ and ‘AI for literature management’, were grouped under broader themes like ‘Benefits of AI in Academic Writing’.

4. Reviewing themes: The preliminary themes were critically examined to ensure coherence, distinctiveness and alignment with the data. This involved revisiting the coded data and refining the themes through multiple iterations to accurately represent participants' perspectives.

5. Defining and naming themes: Each theme was clearly defined and labelled to encapsulate its core meaning and relevance to the research objectives.

6. Producing the report: The final phase involved constructing a cohesive narrative that interwove the themes, supported by illustrative participant quotes to provide evidence and enhance the depth of the analysis. To quantify the prevalence of participants' perceptions, the researchers included the number of participant quotes representing the same or similar codes in a table, indicating how many participants contributed to each code. This approach provided a more transparent and comprehensive representation of the data patterns, strengthening the reliability of the findings.

## 2.5 | Ethical Considerations

The study adhered to rigorous ethical standards to ensure the rights, privacy and well-being of the 81 doctoral student participants. Informed consent was obtained through a detailed consent form embedded in the Google Forms survey. The form explicitly outlined the research purpose, which aimed to explore the use of AI tools in academic writing within doctoral education. It provided comprehensive information about participants roles, emphasising their voluntary participation, the confidentiality of their responses and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence.

To mitigate ethical concerns related to plagiarism, authorship and the potential loss of critical thinking, the survey questions were carefully designed to prompt participants to reflect on both the advantages and challenges of using AI tools in academic writing. This approach encouraged participants to provide nuanced and balanced perspectives rather than endorsing AI use uncritically. Additionally, participants were informed that the study sought to understand their experiences and perceptions rather than promote or legitimise AI tools for academic writing.

Participant recruitment via WhatsApp groups associated with reputable scholarship programs was conducted in a respectful and non-coercive manner. To protect participants' privacy, all data collected through Google Forms were anonymized by removing any personally identifiable information. The findings were reported in aggregate, maintaining participants' confidentiality throughout the research process. The researchers transparently communicated the potential implications of the study, including how the results could contribute to the development of ethical guidelines and institutional policies on AI usage in academic settings.

## 3    |    Results

## 3.1    |    Experiences of Using AI in Academic Writing

The integration of AI into academic writing represents a significant paradigm shift in higher education, challenging traditional notions of authorship, creativity and intellectual rigour. As educational practices continue to evolve in the 21st century, the reliance on AI tools underscores a complex interplay between human cognition and machine learning, where the boundaries between human and artificial contributions to scholarly work become increasingly blurred. From a constructivist perspective, this evolution marks a critical juncture in the way knowledge is constructed and disseminated. AI's role in academic writing is not merely a matter of enhancing efficiency or refining outputs; it raises profound questions about the nature of learning itself.

AI tools have become indispensable for doctoral students, particularly in supporting various aspects of writing such as drafting, grammar correction and translation. Table  1 displays the experiences of doctoral students in using AI for academic writing tasks. The ability to expedite the writing process, enabling quicker production of drafts and improved language accuracy, is highly valued. However, this reliance on AI for technical aspects of writing raises significant concerns about the erosion of students' linguistic skills and their ability to maintain these skills independently. The convenience provided by AI may inadvertently lead to a diminished capacity for students to engage deeply with the mechanics of language, which is crucial for academic rigour.

The role of AI in literature search and management is profound, with tools like Google Scholar and Scite AI significantly streamlining the process of finding relevant literature, managing references and verifying citations. This has led to enhanced research efficiency, particularly in tasks like systematic literature reviews (SLRs), which can now be completed in shorter timeframes. However, the data also suggest a potential downside: the dependence on AI for such critical tasks could weaken students research skills and their ability to critically engage with the literature. This reliance on AI might reduce the depth of their understanding and their capacity to perform thorough, independent literature analysis.

The use of AI for generating and developing research ideas is another prominent theme. Tools like ChatGPT have proven particularly helpful when students struggle with formulating ideas or connecting theoretical concepts. While AI provides a valuable crutch for brainstorming and overcoming writer's block, there is a growing concern that over-reliance on AI for creative thinking could stifle the development of original, critical thought. The fear is that students might become dependent on AI for intellectual stimulation, thereby compromising their ability to generate innovative ideas independently.

One of the most appreciated features of AI tools is their ability to enhance efficiency and productivity. By accelerating various research and writing processes, AI enables students to manage larger volumes of work more effectively. However, this efficiency comes with a potential cost: the risk of students becoming overly dependent on AI, which could erode their intrinsic motivation and their ability to manage research tasks autonomously. The ease provided by AI might lead to a superficial approach to complex academic tasks, undermining the depth and rigour that are essential to scholarly work.

Despite the clear benefits, there are notable concerns about the quality of AI outputs and their alignment with academic standards. Students express apprehension that AI-generated content may not always meet the required academic rigour, raising fears that reliance on AI might compromise the authenticity and integrity of their work. Furthermore, the simplification of complex academic tasks by AI tools poses a risk of promoting a shallow understanding of the material, which could detract from the educational experience and the development of critical analytical skills.

A recurring theme in the data is the need for caution in relying too heavily on AI. While students acknowledge the convenience and efficiency that AI tools provide, they are also acutely aware of the potential drawbacks, such as reduced critical thinking, creativity and the ability to perform independent analysis. The metaphor of ‘opening Pandora's box’ aptly captures the sentiment that, while AI offers considerable advantages, it also carries hidden risks that could lead to a generation of researchers lacking deep analytical skills and independent thought. The data suggests a growing awareness among students of the need to balance the use of AI with the cultivation of their intellectual capacities, ensuring that AI serves as a tool for enhancement rather than a substitute for critical academic engagement.

## 3.2 | Commonly Used AI Tools in Academic Writing

AI tools designed to enhance various aspects of the writing process range from basic grammar and style checkers to advanced applications that assist with data analysis, citation management and even content generation. The growing reliance on AI in academia underscores the increasing demand for efficiency, accuracy and innovation in scholarly work. This section delves into the most commonly used AI tools in academic writing, analysing their functionalities, benefits and the crucial role they play in upholding the intellectual rigour of academic outputs.

Table  2 shows a list of the top 12 AI tools frequently used by doctoral students in academic writing tasks. ChatGPT emerges as the most frequently employed AI tool, particularly valued for its versatility in idea generation. Students rely on it not only for brainstorming but also for answering questions, conducting analyses and translating text. This widespread use underscores a significant shift towards AI handling complex, multifaceted tasks that traditionally demanded considerable human effort. The increasing dependence on ChatGPT reflects a broader trend in academia where efficiency and versatility are highly prized.

While ChatGPT is favoured for its broad applicability, DeepL and Grammarly are specifically utilised for translation and grammar correction, respectively. DeepL's adjustable style makes it a preferred choice for accurate translations, while Grammarly's reputation for precision in grammar correction remains strong. However, the gradual shift of some users towards ChatGPT for these tasks suggests a growing preference for comprehensive tools that can streamline multiple functions within a single platform, signalling a potential consolidation of AI tools in academic writing.

Zotero and Mendeley remain indispensable for citation management, reflecting their entrenched role in academic writing. The emergence of Scite AI, a tool aiding in the crafting of discussion sections, highlights an increasing demand for AI that transcends basic citation management and contributes more actively to the writing process. This shift points to a future where AI's role in academic writing becomes more integrative, potentially influencing not just the management but also the content and structure of scholarly work.

Frequent use of tools like Connected Papers and Litmaps, which are designed for mapping literature and visualising research trends, underscores their critical importance in academic research. These tools enable students to identify gaps in the literature, a crucial step in crafting original research contributions. Their prevalence in the dataset highlights the growing recognition of the need for AI to assist in navigating and synthesising vast bodies of research, thus facilitating more informed and innovative academic writing.

## 3.3 | Ethical Principles in the Use of AI-Assisted Writing

As AI-assisted tools become increasingly prevalent in educational and research contexts, it is imperative to address the ethical considerations surrounding their application. Central to this discussion is the question of academic integrity, where the line between legitimate assistance and unethical dependence must be clearly defined. Ethical principles in the use of AI-assisted writing extend beyond concerns of plagiarism; they encompass issues of transparency, authorship and the responsible use of technology to enhance, rather than replace, the human element of scholarly work. Additionally, there is a need to consider the broader implications of AI on equity and access, as the availability and sophistication of AI tools may vary, potentially exacerbating existing disparities in educational opportunities.

The dataset reveals a nuanced and multifaceted relationship between the ethical use of AI-assisted writing tools and the academic integrity of doctoral students, underscoring the complexities inherent in this evolving academic landscape. Table 3 shows several critical themes that emerge from the data: ethical use of AI, concerns about overreliance, ethical challenges in academia and preventive measures, each shedding light on the intricate dynamics at play.

While AI tools are increasingly prevalent, the data underscores a strong emphasis on the necessity of manual verification and adjustment of AI-generated outputs. This indicates a recognition among students of AI's limitations and the critical importance of maintaining human oversight to uphold academic rigour. The concern for academic honesty and integrity is palpable, with a clear call for transparency in AI use. Students advocate for restricting AI's role to technical assistance, such as translation or citation management, ensuring that the core intellectual tasks remain within the human domain.

A recurring and significant concern is the potential overreliance on AI, which students fear could undermine their mastery of theoretical frameworks and critical analysis. This anxiety reflects a broader apprehension about the erosion of essential academic skills, as the convenience and speed of AI tools might tempt students to bypass the deep engagement necessary for robust scholarship. The fear is that an overdependence on AI could lead to a superficial engagement with content, thereby weakening the critical thinking skills that are foundational to doctoral research.

The pressure associated with academic publishing create a context ripe for ethical breaches. The dataset highlights the theme of publication pressure, revealing how the demand to publish in reputable journals can drive students towards unethical AI use, such as data fabrication or employing ghostwriters. This issue is compounded by the complexities of ethical challenges in Indonesia's academic tradition, where power dynamics can foster practices like name-dropping in publications to strengthen authorship positions, further muddying the ethical waters.

In response to these challenges, the data suggests the necessity of implementing robust laws and regulations that hold individuals accountable for ethical breaches, including plagiarism and data fabrication. Beyond legal frameworks, there is a call for the cultivation of supportive academic environments that can alleviate the pressures leading to unethical AI use. This includes addressing excessive workloads and fostering a scholarly community that emphasises guidance and mentorship, encouraging both doctoral candidates and professors to adhere to ethical practices in their academic endeavours.

The prominence of ethics reflects the central concern of ensuring that AI tools are used responsibly, particularly in preserving the honesty and originality of scholarly work. Similarly, integrity recurs as a crucial theme, indicating that students are acutely aware of the potential risks of compromising their academic principles when relying on AI. These two concepts are deeply interconnected, emphasising the need to maintain academic integrity and adhere to ethical guidelines when utilising AI in writing.

This connection suggests that while AI offers valuable support, there is a strong recognition of the need for critical oversight to maintain the quality and originality of the work produced.

## 4 | Discussion

This phenomenological study tried to unpack the doctoral students' experiences and perspectives in using AI for conducting their scholarly writing project. The study's findings enrich the theoretical understanding of the use of AI in academic settings by highlighting the academic values and students' experiences and perceptions in the writing project. The integration of AI in academic writing has indeed transformed the landscape of scholarly communication, presenting both remarkable advantages and significant ethical dilemmas. Tools, such as ChatGPT, enhance clarity and assist in idea generation, respectively, yet they also raise critical concerns regarding academic integrity and the potential erosion of essential cognitive skills. For instance, Hemingway Editor's suggestion to remove content for brevity can inadvertently lead to the omission of vital arguments, thereby compromising the depth of academic discourse (Buriak et al. 2023). Similarly, while ChatGPT can facilitate brainstorming and alleviate writer's block, it cannot replicate the nuanced development of research ideas that are fundamental to scholarly work (Dergaa et al. 2023).

This study revealed that ChatGPT stands out for its versatility in idea generation, drafting and translation. At the same time, tools like DeepL, Grammarly, Zotero, Mendeley and Scite AI play key roles in translation, grammar correction and literature management. AI supports academic writing through idea generation, writing and language refinement while also aiding research organisation through summarisation and simplification, illustrating the reliance on AI to support basic skills and simplify complex tasks in academic writing. It indicates that the reliance on AI tools may foster a culture of superficial engagement with content, as users may become overly dependent on these technologies for generating ideas rather than cultivating their analytical capabilities (Dwivedi et al. 2023); whereas, AI still had significant inaccuracies and errors (Kacena et al. 2024; Safrai and Orwig 2024) that raise academic concerns, such as plagiarism, creativity and critical thinking (Al-Sofi  2024). It highlights that AI cannot be used as a stand-alone tool but needs human verification to grab the benefits of AI for improving students' critical thinking and enhancing students' social, cognitive and emotional engagement (Guo and Wang 2025; Liu and Wang 2024).

The use of AI in literature management, exemplified by tools like Scite AI and Zotero, streamlines citation processes and enhances the accuracy of references. However, this automation risks diminishing scholars' engagement with the intricacies of citation practices, potentially leading to a lack of understanding of the ethical implications surrounding authorship and intellectual property (Hastuti and Syafruddin 2023). While the automation of bibliographic tasks increases efficiency, it may also inadvertently contribute to a decline in critical thinking skills, as scholars might rely on AI to navigate complex citation networks without fully grasping the underlying principles of scholarly communication (Ryan and Stahl 2021). In other words, the tendency to overreliance on AI can result in carelessness about its limitations leading to academic dishonesty and decreased academic writing skills (Al-Sofi 2024).

This study also confirmed that students perceived AI tools are valued for expediting writing, improving language accuracy, enhancing research efficiency and allowing students to handle larger workloads. They assist in literature management, idea generation and boosting productivity. These findings corroborate the findings of Al-Sofi  (2024), Kacena et  al.  (2024) and Shofiah et al. (2023), who confirmed that AI tools are perceived as useful and promising for conducting academic tasks, thus predicting students are more likely to have a higher intention to use AI (Wang and Reynolds 2024; Wu et al. 2024). However, students still had concerns about overreliance and fears of the impact on their critical and analytical thinking, academic rigour and AI output quality.

It indicates that students are still aware of the risk of adopting AI in their work. Besides, Wu et al. (2024) found that perceived usefulness did not affect attitude or intention to use AI. Students would stop using AI if they experience difficulties or dissatisfaction.

The ethical considerations surrounding AI usage in academia are further complicated by the pressures of the ‘publish or perish’ culture, which can lead to unethical practices such as data fabrication and plagiarism (Nam and Bai  2023). The ethical landscape of AI in academia necessitates a careful balance between leveraging technological advancements and maintaining academic integrity. AI should be positioned as a supplement, not a replacement (Al-Sofi 2024), and evaluating AI responses is essential to prevent misinformation and inaccuracies (Safrai and Orwig 2024). Zhang et al. (2023) highlighted the integration of ethics into AI curricula is crucial for preparing future scholars to navigate the ethical challenges posed by AI technologies. This is particularly pertinent in light of the increasing prevalence of AIgenerated content, which raises questions about authorship and the authenticity of academic work (Dergaa et al. 2023). The need for transparency in AI usage is paramount; ethical guidelines should delineate the acceptable boundaries of AI assistance, ensuring that it serves as a complement to human intellect rather than a substitute (Lin 2023).

The prevalence of predatory publishing among Indonesian academics highlights a critical issue within the academic community, where the pressure to publish often overshadows the importance of ethical practices and quality scholarship. Research indicates that a significant portion of Indonesian scholars engage with predatory journals, which compromise the integrity of academic output and undermine the credibility of research (Latan et  al.  2023). The systemic issues contributing to this phenomenon include a lack of information literacy and inadequate guidance in selecting reputable journals, which exacerbate the vulnerability of researchers to exploitative publishing practices (Wiratningsih 2018). Furthermore, the regulatory framework in Indonesia, which prioritises quantity over quality in academic publications, has inadvertently fostered an environment conducive to such unethical practices (Pourret et al. 2021). This situation underscores the urgent need for a robust academic community that emphasises ethical standards and promotes critical engagement with scholarly work.

The misuse of artificial intelligence (AI) in academic writing further complicates the landscape of academic integrity. AI tools, while offering efficiencies in generating content, can also serve as shortcuts that diminish the originality and integrity of scholarly work. The reliance on AI for writing can lead to a culture of complacency, where researchers may resort to automated solutions rather than engaging deeply with their research (Miao et al. 2023). This concern is compounded by the pressures of high publication rates, which can drive unethical behaviours in academic writing such as fabrication and falsification of data and images, and plagiarism (Nazarovets 2024). The integration of AI in academic contexts necessitates stringent regulations and accountability measures to mitigate these risks and uphold the standards of academic integrity (Uddin and Abu 2024).

Moreover, ethical issues surrounding authorship and publication practices are prevalent in academia, with ghostwriting and academic bullying being significant concerns. Senior academics often exploit their positions to claim authorship on papers without contributing meaningfully, undermining the integrity of academic authorship (Himawan 2021). This exploitation calls for a cultural shift within academic institutions to promote transparency and accountability in research practices. While individual responsibility is crucial, institutional support and clear guidelines are essential to foster an environment that prioritises ethical scholarship (Bozkurt 2024).

The dual-edged nature of AI integration in academic work presents both opportunities and challenges. On one hand, AI can enhance efficiency and creativity, aiding in tasks such as literature review and data analysis (Slimi and Villarejo-Carballido 2024). On the other hand, there is a growing anxiety that over-reliance on AI tools may erode critical thinking and analytical skills among researchers (William 2024). The need for manual verification of AI-generated outputs emphasises the importance of maintaining rigorous intellectual engagement with content, as AI cannot replace the depth of analysis required for high-quality scholarship (Tang et al. 2024). This nuanced perspective advocates for a balanced approach to AI use, where it serves as a complement to human intellect rather than a substitute (Rodrigues et al. 2024).

## 5 | Conclusions

## 5.1 | Research Findings

Academic writing is an indicator of one's creativity and competence. The use of AI can be an effective tool to help doctoral students produce higher-quality academic writing. Our study explores the most frequently used AI tools by doctoral students for their academic writing tasks, their experiences and the influence of ethical principles, critical thinking and scholarly rigour in academic writing tasks. It revealed that ChatGPT, DeepL, Zotero, Mendeley, Scite AI, Connected Papers, Litmaps, Elicit, SciSpace, Genei and Humata are the most popular AI tools among doctoral students. They frequently used those AI tools for various academic writing tasks such as generating ideas, brainstorming, outlining, drafting, translating, managing literature, summarising, mapping literature and visualisation. They perceived that AI tools are very helpful, enhance the quality of writing and save time. The study also reveals a complex relationship between AIassisted writing tools and academic integrity among doctoral students. Students recognise the tools' benefits but express concerns about overreliance and potential ethical breaches.

## 5.2 | Practical Implications

While AI can be helpful, human oversight is crucial to maintain academic rigour. Therefore, on the practical level, our findings suggest that a set of practical guidelines on AI use in academic settings is essential, especially in academic writing. It can support the academic environment to enhance the quality of students' writing and prevent unethical practices.

## 5.3 | Research Limitation

Despite these contributions, this study had some limitations. Firstly, while the study focuses on experiences and perspectives for using AI on academic writing projects, it does not deeply explore specific cases in AI-assisted academic writing such as authorship, plagiarism and transparency. Lastly, it also does not focus on students' critical thinking skills and overreliance on using AI.

## 5.4 | Future Research Directions

Future studies should explore the ethical dilemmas posed by AI in academic writing, including authorship, plagiarism and transparency. Moreover, future research should also assess the impacts of using AI on students' critical thinking skills in literature synthesis and argument development in writing.
