# AUTOGEN: A Personalized Large Language Model for Academic Enhancement—Ethics and Proof of Principle

Sebastian Porsdam Mann<sup>a</sup> , Brian D. Earp<sup>a</sup> , Nikolaj Møller<sup>a</sup>, Suren Vynn<sup>b</sup>, and Julian Savulescu<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>University of Oxford; <sup>b</sup>Independent Researcher; <sup>c</sup>National University of Singapore

## ABSTRACT

In this article, we explore the potential of enhancing academic prose and idea generation by fine-tuning a large language model (here, GPT-3) on one’s own previously published writings: AUTOGEN (“AI Unique Tailored Output GENerator”). We develop, test, and describe three distinct AUTOGEN models trained on the prior scholarly output of three of the current authors (SBM, BDE, JS), with a fourth model trained on the combined works of all three. Our AUTOGEN models demonstrate greater variance in quality than the base GPT-3 model, with many outputs outperforming the base model in format, style, overall quality, and novel idea generation. As proof of principle, we present and discuss examples of AUTOGEN-written sections of existing and hypothetical research papers. We further discuss ethical opportunities, concerns, and open questions associated with personalized academic prose and idea generators. Ethical opportunities for personalized LLMs such as AUTOGEN include increased productivity, preservation of writing styles and cultural traditions, and aiding consensus building. However, ethical concerns arise due to the potential for personalized LLMs to reduce output diversity, violate privacy and intellectual property rights, and facilitate plagiarism or fraud. The use of coauthored or multiple-source trained models further complicates issues surrounding ownership and attribution. Open questions concern a potential credit-blame asymmetry for LLM outputs, the legitimacy of licensing agreements in authorship ascription, and the ethical implications of coauthorship attribution for data contributors. Ensuring the output is sufficiently distinct from the source material is crucial to maintaining ethical standards in academic writing. These opportunities, risks, and open issues highlight the intricate ethical landscape surrounding the use of personalized LLMs in academia. We also discuss open technical questions concerning the integration of AUTOGEN-style personalized LLMs with other LLMs, such as GPT-4, for iterative refinement and improvement of generated text. In conclusion, we argue that AUTOGEN-style personalized LLMs offer significant potential benefits in terms of both prose generation and, to a lesser extent, idea generation. If associated ethical issues are appropriately addressed, AUTOGEN alone or in combination with other LLMs can be seen as a potent form of academic enhancement.

## INTRODUCTION

Large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT or Google’s Bard have shown significant performance on a variety of text-based tasks, such as summarization, translation, and even the generation of new ideas (Bommasani et al. 2022; Bubeck et al. 2023). Despite such impressive, seemingly domain-general abilities, LLMs such as GPT-3<sup>1</sup> (Brown et al. 2020) can benefit from additional, specialized training in certain narrower domains (Moradi et al. 2022). Known as fine-tuning, this process involves training the final few layers of an LLM’s neural network on a specialized corpus of text, such that the resulting model retains its fundamental model of language but produces text influenced by the features of the specific corpus on which it was fine-tuned (Church, Chen, and Ma 2021).

The potential for LLMs to facilitate the academic research and writing process is increasingly explored (Lin 2023). However, such use is attended by significant technical and ethical problems and uncertainties. Recent, high-performing LLMs such as ChatGPT and GPT-4 tend to produce text in a shorter, more conversational, and less rigorous format than that typical of academic prose. This text also tends toward a flat, homogenous style (Grimaldi and Ehrler 2023). Among the novel ethical issues are questions of responsibility, credit, and blame for generated text and the need to vet such text for accuracy (Porsdam Mann et al. 2023).

The extent to which these technical and ethical issues can be addressed through fine-tuning an LLM for academic prose generation is largely an an open question. Early findings show that fine-tuned GPT-3 models can in fact produce text stylistically akin to specific authors (Elkins and Chun 2020). Notably, a GPT-3 model, fine-tuned by Schwitzgebel et al. to respond to philosophical questions in the style of philosopher Daniel C. Dennett, has produced text convincingly similar to Dennett’s own writing (Schwitzgebel, Schwitzgebel, and Strasser 2023). Yet, to the best of our knowledge, the question of whether a model finetuned on personal academic output can be used to help generate better academic prose or even to suggest novel ideas so far remains unexplored.

The question seems worth exploring not only for practical reasons (for example, potential implications for scholarly efficiency or productivity), but also due to the ethical puzzles it raises for such core academic notions as assigning intellectual credit. Intuitively, a human scholar who produces a novel text with a general (i.e., non-personalized) LLM by employing minimal effort (e.g., inputting a sequence of short questions or prompts, with the LLM doing most of the “work”) deserves little intellectual credit for the output. But what about a scholar who produces a text in the very same way, only using a personalized LLM? That is, an LLM trained more specifically (i.e., fine-tuned) on their own previous academic writing: writing that reflects past effort, skill, and insight on their part of the kind that usually is deemed sufficient for claiming credit. (We are currently undertaking empirical and normative-philosophical work to investigate the answer.)

Here, we describe and reflect on the potential for enhancing the format, style, and overall quality of academic prose and idea generation by fine-tuning GPT-3 (the latest model available to us) on the published writings of three of the authors, individually and in combination (SPM, BDE, JS).

Our efforts result in three personalized academic prose generators and one combined, mixed-author model, which we collectively refer to as AUTOGENs (“AI Unique Tailored Output GENerators”). In the first part of this paper, we present and comment on selected outputs. As we do not formally evaluate our models, but rather give a qualitative summary of striking features, our results should be seen as a proof of principle only. Nevertheless, while the output of our AUTOGEN models show greater variance in quality than the base GPT-3 model,<sup>2</sup> it seems to us that many outputs clearly outperform the base model in format, style, overall quality, and novel idea generation. As expected, this is especially the case when asked to generate text on topics close to those included in its training data. It should be noted that although the models often produce impressively coherent responses sometimes developed in detail, they do less well in introducing or developing arguments. We expect that this weakness may be partially addressed in the future by fine-tuning the next-generation GPT-4 which shows much greater apparent reasoning abilities than the model used here (Bubeck et al. 2023).

In the second part of the paper, we reflect on ethical opportunities, concerns, and open questions associated with personalized academic prose and idea generators. Personalized LLMs like AUTOGEN present opportunities such as increasing academic efficiency, preserving writing styles and cultural traditions, and aiding consensus building. However, they also present ethical concerns including exacerbating inequalities, reducing output diversity, potential privacy and intellectual property rights violations, and misuse for plagiarism or fraud. We also address some open questions concerning what we have elsewhere identified as a credit-blame asymmetry for generative artificial intelligence (AI) outputs, such as texts produced by LLMs (Porsdam Mann et al. 2023). We further comment on the complexities of ownership and attribution in coauthored or multiplesource trained models, the legitimacy of licensing agreements in authorship ascription, and the ethical implications of coauthorship attribution for data contributors. Finally, we discuss open technical questions concerning the integration of AUTOGEN-style personalized LLMs with other LLMs, such as GPT-4, for iterative refinement and improvement of generated text. These opportunities, concerns, and open issues highlight the intricate ethical landscape surrounding the use of personalized LLMs in academia.

## METHODS

In order to examine the potential of fine-tuning to effectively mimic one’s personal academic writing style, as well as the effect of training dataset size on model performance producing substantive content, we created several variations of a fine-tuned LLM using the OpenAI API (Davinci003).

To train our models, we used text taken from all individual sections of articles written by three of the authors (SPM, BDE, JS). We defined a “section” of an article as the text under a subheading (introduction, main text subheadings, and conclusion). We used text from each section of all<sup>3</sup> articles, books, and book chapters that:

1. were published in peer-reviewed journals or by an academic publishing house; AND

2. feature either SPM, BDE, or JS as first author; AND

3. contain an abstract or equivalent description.

The first two criteria were chosen to ensure that training data was of sufficient academic quality and was written primarily by one or more of the selected authors. The third criterion was chosen for methodological reasons, allowing us to generate matching prompt-completion pairs, as explained below.

Fine-tuning requires a database of example promptcompletion pairs. A prompt is the user-inputted text which functions as a query or instruction to the language model. A completion is the text outputted by the language model in response to the prompt. The idea behind fine-tuning is that by offering the language model many examples of desired prompt-completion pairs, it can adapt and provide better completions on queries and instructions that are similar to those used in the fine-tuning training data.

For one series of fine-tuned models, we used blank prompts. That is, we used empty space as input and sections of text from our published studies as completions. This training method was motivated by the above-mentioned success using this method reported by Schwitzgebel, Schwitzgebel, and Strasser (2023) in fine-tuning GPT-3 on Daniel Dennett’s writings (15 books, 269 articles; ca. three million tokens<sup>4</sup> total, resulting in 1,828 prompt-completion pairs). However, we abandoned this method of training after early comparisons between this and the method described below clearly favored the latter method in terms of performance (see Supplementary Information). We also created one fine-tune model based on our smallest set of writings, those with SPM as first author, using 12 instead of the default 4 training epochs. This, too, resulted in poorer performance and will not be discussed further.

For the other, more successful series, we generated matching prompts using an introductory instructional text followed by the title and abstract of our articles. From prior experience with GPT models, we reasoned that the inclusion of contextual information about the structure of the paper and its arguments would lead to better, more coherent completions. We also envisaged that practical use of these models would involve the provision of such contextual information, and therefore thought it likely that using training data in a similar structure would improve the performance of the resulting models.

The template we used for prompts was:

Imagine that you are an academic writing a research paper. The paper should be as interesting, comprehensive, clear, and concise as possible. Based on the below title and abstract, write the section on “[section X]”. Title: [Title]. Abstract: [Abstract]. Section:

This resulted in a set of four fine-tuned models, three trained on each of the SPM, BDE, and JS datasets and one combined model trained on the combined writings of all three authors. We named these models AUTOGEN-SPM, AUTOGEN-BDE, AUTOGEN-JS, and AUTOGEN-CO. Table 1 shows the number of publications, prompt-completion pairs, and tokens on which these models were trained, as well as the associated costs. AUTOGEN-SPM2 and AUTOGEN-BDE2 are fine-tunes in which the prompts were left blank in the training data. This resulted in less text and thus lower cost. SPM3 is a version of SPM2 using 12, rather than 4, training epochs. However, as these models performed consistently worse during early testing, as noted previously, we abandoned this method of fine-tuning before advancing to the larger dataset models (JS and CO).

Due to resource and time constraints, we have not yet formally evaluated our models. Instead, we informally evaluated them by tasking them with generating sections of existing or potential papers based on a title and abstract.

## RESULTS

Figure 1 shows an example prompt and completion of the base and AUTOGEN-JS models. The title and abstract used in the prompt are from one of JS’s actual publications: a publication on the possible use of AI for moral enhancement: “moral AI” (i.e., Savulescu and Maslen 2015; see also Giubilini and Savulescu 2018; Demaree-Cotton, Earp, and Savulescu 2022). Importantly, however, the actual text of this paper (i.e., the main body of the paper) was left out of the training data for testing purposes. While the base model essentially regurgitated the information provided in the abstract, the fine-tuned model provides a much more detailed and comprehensive completion. In fact, the completion is, in certain respects, surprisingly similar to the text from the main body of the actual published paper, even though, again, this text was not included in the training data. It is impossible to determine whether these papers were included in the training data of the underlying GPT-3 model, as the training data is not public. However, even if they were, a verbatim regurgitation of text from training data is generally unlikely except in the case of popular books (Liang et al. 2022), and a comparison to the original paper shows no directly matching text (see Figure 1).

The impressive results on this task presumably are due in part to the substantive similarity between its subject matter (namely, AI for moral enhancement) and many of the articles included in the training data. More specifically, JS has published widely on the topic of moral enhancement in general and by various means (albeit only rarely—that is, thrice—by means of AI specifically; see above references). The training data for AUTOGEN-JS did contain several of these non-AI-related moral enhancement articles. That being said, however, it does not include any publications on moral AI specifically. Thus, the model appears capable of generating high-quality text in areas that contain at least a partial overlap with the areas covered in the articles used as training data (i.e., a near-transfer task)—though it is important to note that the model sometimes produces misinformation, as in the case of the below quote by Hume, where both the year and exact wording are incorrect.<sup>5</sup> Another problematic occurrence is model performance trailing off or becoming increasingly tangential, as can be seen in the last paragraph generated by AUTOGEN-JS, Figure 1. Consequently, any usage of outputs requires rigorous human vetting and editing before use.

While the result is striking, it does not add new information or ideas to those contained in the abstract. This is typical of the completions we have generated to date. While the AUTOGEN models seem capable of taking existing ideas and expanding them into a longer, more complete description, they have more difficulty generating new ideas. ## ETHICAL OPPORTUNITIES

## Type 1: Further Increasing the Benefits of Non-Personalized LLMs

To the extent that personalized LLMs, compared to non-personalized LLMs, generate texts<sup>8</sup> and ideas of greater relevance and quality, their use can be expected to increase productivity for academic writers who use them as compared to those who do not use LLMs or use non-personalized, general models. Instructing a personalized LLM to generate a draft article, section by section, by first providing it with an article abstract may reduce the time needed to convert ideas into draft text, freeing time for other academic tasks. As demonstrated by our results, LLMs can also be used as brainstorming devices that can indicate areas and arguments worthy of further exploration. Models thus have significant potential to enhance academic workflows, contribute to creativity and epistemic progress and potentially increase work satisfaction. In other words, personalized LLMs amplify the existing benefits widely associated with nonpersonalized LLMs. Though productivity gains from integrating personalizing LLMs across various types of work tasks and flows are unknown, one early study estimated a 37% gain in productivity for non-personalized LLM use for various writing tasks (Noy and Zhang 2023), as well as increases in reported job satisfaction. Increased productivity in turn has numerous potential benefits, including increased rates of progress, innovation, and economic growth; and additional leisure time (e.g. shifting to a 4-day work week) which could improve mental and physical health as well as work and life satisfaction (Shao 2022).

It should be noted that these benefits are contingent on the careful vetting of generated text and on ethical use of personalized models. Transparency about LLM use is crucial to address issues surrounding credit, accuracy, and plagiarism, as discussed in more detail below.

## Type 2: Benefits and Opportunities Unique to Personalized LLMs

However, there are also ethical opportunities unique to fine-tuned LLMs. One is their use for the preservation of unique writing styles. Individuals could preserve their own writing style, such that they have access to it in the case of illness, incapacity, or old age. Those already suffering from these or other ailments rendering it difficult to write could have their ability to express themselves partially maintained or restored. Similar remarks can be made for individuals whose first language is not English (Zohny, McMillan, and M. King 2023). If trained on sufficiently personal information, fine-tuned models might also be used to approximate personal preferences, for example in the case of medical decision-making under incapacity (see, e.g., Rid and Wendler 2014), an idea we are exploring in ongoing work.

So far, we have discussed LLMs fine-tuned on individual writing. Yet there are also significant opportunities at the collective level. One such opportunity would be the preservation of cultural and groupspecific writing styles that could be considered instrumentally or intrinsically valuable. For example, indigenous or other communities facing linguistic or cultural domination or elimination/extinction may one day use an AUTOGEN to help preserve or expand their written heritage, folklore or traditions. Organizations may fine-tune LLMs to ensure their unique brand, voice and perspective are always evident in their content and products. They might also employ fine-tuned models as a means of training and/or support for newer or less skilled workers. A study on the non-personalized LLM use in call centers found that they disproportionately boosted the performance of less skilled workers (Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond 2023). These leveling effects might well be greater, or attainable across a wider range of tasks or functions, for fine-tuned LLMs.

Another ethical opportunity is AUTOGEN-CO-style models trained on the writings of multiple authors to represent group consensus. Recent work shows that fine-tuned LLMs can produce texts on select issues obtaining higher approval rates from a group than human-written consensus statements (Bakker et al. 2022). Three potential use cases are in science communication, evidence-based policy (e.g. a model trained on all articles that meet certain criteria, similar to literature reviews on a particular topic), and in deliberative democratic processes. These are cases where arriving at group consensus is valuable and often requires an arduous process. Such models may even turn out to serve a purpose as a science dissemination or democratic tool in its own right, as demonstrated by the Danish Synthetic Party, the first political party to be “led” by an AI (Hearing 2022).

More generally, personalized LLM training datasets can be tailored and curated for specific purposes. One can imagine personalizing a model based on the Pope’s circulars to achieve a (religiously) virtuous LLM. Other training sets might be curated to help minimize gender or racial bias in outputs. Such finetuned LLMs could then at least tacitly improve the moral quality of the writing of their users.

Use of such models could be layered, such that each step of a workflow is carried out by a different, specialized LLM. For example, an in-house researcher at a company might generate a first draft based on a personalized LLM. The first draft could then be fed into another LLM fine-tuned on company documents to help increase its fit with corporate branding and messaging priorities. The output could then be further refined by LLMs specialized for de-biasing of language, for increasing the clarity of the writing, for evaluating its alignment with specific values or causes, or for any other specific purpose.

## ETHICAL CONCERNS

LLMs in general raise questions around the instrumentalization and alienation of humans from work and creative endeavor. They open the door to cheating and plagiarism. We gesture to a response to these concerns in the abstract of a potential future paper on co-creation included in Figure 3. In brief, when human beings evaluate and transform the results of LLMs, particularly building on their analytic strength or originality, the use of LLMs is not merely permissible but an attractive and morally desirable example of human-technological co-creation. We plan to develop this idea in a subsequent publication.

The creation and use of personalized LLMs involve several additional issues of ethical concern. Prominent among these is the potential for personalized LLMs to contribute to inequalities via the so-called Matthew effect. Named after a passage in the Gospel of Matthew (25:29), the Matthew effect refers to the tendency of advantages such as social or financial capital to accrue faster to those who already enjoy much of them than to those who do not: “For unto every one that hath shall be given, and he shall have abundance: but from him that hath not shall be taken away even that which he hath.” Since the performance of finetuned LLMs is positively correlated with the volume of data available to train them, the Matthew effect means that proficient and prolific writers, who already enjoy the benefits of an established publication record, can benefit disproportionately from personalized LLMs. This is clearly demonstrated in the difference in performance between our models trained on few (SPM) versus many (BDE, JS, CO) previous publications (see Supplementary Information). The extent to which this can be addressed by creating synthetic text based on existing training data or other means remain open questions.

Another ethical concern is related to the potential homogenizing effect of LLMs on writing styles. At the individual level, reliance on personalized LLMs might prevent or inhibit the further evolution of writing styles. At the collective level, models adapting newly generated or formatted outputs to a specific style may inadvertently reduce diversity and potentially diminish the authenticity of LLM-produced texts as well as the enjoyment gained from reading them (Dwivedi et al. 2023).

Issues surrounding consent and the use of publications as training data are particularly salient for finetuned models. While the use of writings of which one is the sole author seems to us to be ethically unproblematic, this is not necessarily the case for coauthored publications (see discussion on this point below). Even more suspect is the use of writings to which one has not contributed at all. Using an individual’s writing style or data without permission might violate privacy and intellectual property rights. However, issues of authenticity and the assignment of credit might remain contentious even in cases where consent has been obtained, but those using the resulting finetuned models have not themselves contributed to its training data.

Relatedly, misusing LLMs for various purposes, particularly style theft, poses significant ethical risks. Given the significant market opportunity for assisting individuals or companies with fine tuning models, websites such as chatbase.co and reword.com are already making it possible to easily personalize LLMs based on someone’s writings at cheap or no cost.<sup>9</sup> A malicious actor could therefore easily train and exploit personalized LLMs without consent for plagiarism or fraud, compromising academic integrity or professional reputation.

Given that AUTOGEN appears to perform better on topics similar to those on which it was trained, there is a risk that access to AUTOGEN-type models would perversely incentivize scholars to focus on work that is only marginally different to what they have written before, while still crossing some minimal threshold of publishability. This may be analogous to so-called “salami slicing”—the tendency to take a set of studies or ideas and slice them into as many tiny, incrementally advancing papers as one can, just to get more publications, when really, they should all be in the same paper. Similarly, the higher performance on familiar topics of AUTOGEN might incentivize scholars to produce ever more papers, each one only marginally different from the last, rather than to make bigger, more substantive leaps of scholarly or intellectual progress. Although this is likely to be a genuine problem, especially given the already unmanageable volume of papers published in many disciplines each year, there may be practical ways around this. For example, it might become an academic norm or even a policy requirement that one can use AUTOGEN to generate introductions, conclusions, or background sections in which a more or less rote synthesis of existing ideas and scholarship is necessary, while still needing to manually develop the bulk of the substantively new material.

## OPEN ETHICAL QUESTIONS

LLM use by humans, like human use of generative AI more generally, entails a moral credit-blame asymmetry (Porsdam Mann et al. 2023). On the one hand, blameworthiness for harmful content, or other bad outcomes caused by human use of LLMs, arguably only requires recklessness or negligence (a low bar). On the other hand, and by contrast, creditworthiness for good or valuable outcomes in such a context requires that one invest substantial effort, talent, or sacrifice—specifically of a kind that may be directly undermined or diminished by the use of LLMs (a high bar). Thus, individuals may be blameworthy for loweffort, unskilled, yet careless or reckless use of LLMs to generate bad or harmful content, but cannot similarly take credit for good or valuable content generated through a similar (low) investment of effort or skill.

Personalized LLMs like AUTOGEN complicate this issue. Insofar as such models facilitate the production of valuable writing, their use may diminish the effort and skill put into the writing process and thus, arguably, creditworthiness. However, unlike more general, non-personalized models, personalized models trained on one’s own prior writing represent the outcome of previous applications of labor, skill and sacrifice. Perhaps, then, personalized LLM use occupies a middle ground with respect to praiseworthiness: between the generation of text by non-personalized models (least credit) to entirely human-generated writing of text (most credit). Insofar as we care about human moral creditworthiness, that may favor training and using personalized, over non-personalized, models for text generation.

This position seems plausible to us in the case of LLMs trained by an individual using only their own past writings. However, when the LLM is trained on a mixed dataset with coauthored texts or even other authors’ works, more complicated questions of ownership and attribution arise. One set of issues concern the proportion of text written by a specific author of a multi-author paper. The approach we took in training AUTOGEN was to use as training data only those publications on which SPM, BDE, or JS are first authors, as in the bioethical context first authorship is typically a reasonable proxy for main drafting responsibilities. This assumption may not hold for other fields, such as law, where coauthor rankings may be determined by seniority or alphabetical order. As the proportion of published text attributable to the author seeking its use for LLM customization decreases, the moral importance of obtaining the consent of coauthors increases.

In cases where an individual is using a model not owned by them, the output’s ownership becomes even more challenging to determine. Licensing agreements can play a critical role in clarifying rights and responsibilities associated with using LLMs, specifying who should be credited and under what circumstances. However, the extent to which certain licensing arrangements should be considered legitimate for authorship ascription remains an open question that should be discussed as early as possible. For example, roughly half of the training data used in the combined AUTOGEN model derives from JS’s writings; the large majority of the rest derives from BDE’s writings; and only a small minority from SPM’s writings. Should SPM be allowed to license AUTOGEN-CO? If not, what percentage of contribution should serve as a cutoff value? If yes, what about someone who has contributed nothing to the training data?

Further pressing questions concern whether data contributors should be listed as coauthor. This might depend on the level of their contribution to the model, and their role in the creation of the output. If someone’s work significantly impacts the final output, coauthorship might be justified. However, listing someone as a coauthor—or even as text contributor— who has not personally written any material in the text seems problematic. Doing so without their consent could lead to further ethical concerns.

Additionally, the risk of plagiarizing arises when the LLM generates content that closely resembles the original works used in training. Ensuring that the output is sufficiently distinct from the source material is crucial to maintaining ethical standards in academic writing.

## OPEN TECHNICAL QUESTIONS

There are also numerous open questions relating to more technical matters. In presenting our findings, we have only shown unedited AUTOGEN completions using the fixed input format described in the methods section above. However, other methods of using AUTOGEN are possible and we are exploring these in ongoing work. For example, completions can be made to continue manually written text by including leading sentences in the prompt. By writing out the first or more sentences in a given section, and including these in the prompt, AUTOGEN can be made to continue the line of thought expressed in those sentences. Similarly, AUTOGEN outputs can be edited and fed back into the prompt. For example, if AUTOGEN provides a five-paragraph completion but only the first paragraph is on-topic or otherwise interesting, the last four paragraphs can be deleted and AUTOGEN can be made to generate new completions continuing from the first paragraph.

Furthermore, and very importantly, AUTOGEN can be used as a discrete step in a wider workflow. As mentioned, AUTOGEN is based on the GPT-3 model, which shows much inferior apparent reasoning abilities than the successor GPT-4 model (Bubeck et al. 2023). However, AUTOGEN and GPT-4 can be easily combined. The rough material generated by AUTOGEN could be fed into GPT-4 for improvement. For example, we have had promising early results using prompts such as “Fix the format of the following text and correct errors of reasoning and structure. Based on the following text and abstract, write an introduction to a scholarly paper on [x]." GPT-4 can also be used to rewrite AUTOGEN completions according to specific instructions, such as “Rewrite the above. Get rid of the second objection and combine the third and fourth into one.”

The potential benefits of integrating both AUTOGEN and GPT-4 into a workflow have recently been expanded by the release of plugins and browsing for GPT-4, and the ongoing release of the Code Interpreter. This allows GPT-4 to access and process information from the internet (browsing), third-party applications (“plug-ins”), and manually uploaded documents in a variety of formats (Code Interpreter). Other developments, such as AutoGPT, expand these capabilities even further by allowing individuals to assign multiple goals and tasks to LLMs which then attempt to carry these out without further instruction. Thus, the completions we present above could likely be substantially and easily improved using other LLMs such as GPT-4.

## CONCLUSION

We introduce the use of a fine-tuned, personalized LLM for academic writing, which we dubbed AUTOGEN. Though our experiments are preliminary and informal, we believe they are sufficient to demonstrate the potential benefits of LLM fine-tuning on scholars’ own prior publications, especially for the generation of text and relevant ideas close to the subject matter of included writings.

The potential benefits, such as increasing academic efficiency and preserving writing styles, must be weighed against serious concerns including intellectual property rights, privacy, and the potential for misuse. Attempts to regulate personalized LLMs at both societal and institutional levels should address how to mitigate these concerns whilst pursuing benefits associated with the opportunities. Further, the ethical complexities surrounding authorship and credit attribution, particularly for multi-source trained models, present novel challenges that remain to be worked out.
