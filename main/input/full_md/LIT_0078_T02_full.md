# Generative large language models and academic integrity: ethical risks, detection challenges, and Governance in the age of AI

Yongzhi Liang<sup>a</sup>, Jun Zhang<sup>a</sup>, Jiayu Chen<sup>a</sup>, Jiayu Wu<sup>a</sup>, Hui Shen<sup>a</sup>, and Wenrui Liang <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Graduate School, Hong Kong Baptist University; <sup>b</sup>Department of Media and Communication Studies, Univeristy of Malaya

## ABSTRACT

This paper analyzes the impact of generative large language models on academic integrity as a socio-technical issue, rather than a strictly individual problem. The paper does a cross-analysis of 30 high-level Chinese academic articles indexed by SSCI/CSSCI Core, conducts controlled experiments on the generative ability of four major models (ByteDance Doubao, Tencent Yuanbao, Baidu Wenxin Yiyan, and OpenAI Chat-GPT), and does semi-structured interviews with 18 individuals, including editors, professors, integrity oficers, publishers, and AI engineers.This paper finds that “surface originality can successfully conceal derivation to circumvent plagiarism and AIGC detection tools such as PaperPass.” The paper finds three themes: surface originality masking derivative content, length-dependent evasion patterns, and institutional responses to evolving evasion. This paper proposes to “enrich the Technology Acceptance Model by perceived ethical risk, develop a concept of a surface-originality evasion ecology.”

## KEYWORDS

Ethics; plagiarism; detection; academic integrity; artificial intelligence generated content

## INTRODUCTION

The rapid development of generative artificial intelligence, particularly large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT (OpenAI), Gemini (Google), Claude (Anthropic), and domestic Chinese models like Wenxin Yiyan (Baidu) and Doubao (ByteDance), has fundamentally reshaped knowledge production and academic writing practices in higher education. An LLM is a deep neural network trained on massive text corpora, capable of understanding context and generating coherent, human-like text on demand (Zhao et al., 2023). These models no longer merely retrieve or summarize existing information but can autonomously produce full-length essays, literature reviews, and even research papers with minimal human input, thereby challenging conventional notions of authorship and originality (Balalle & Pannilage, 2025; Rudolph et al., 2023).

Despite their powerful assistance capabilities, the integration of LLMs into academic ecosystems has created profound threats to academic integrity. Traditional plagiarism – defined as verbatim copying – has become less common, yet a subtler and harder-to-detect form of misconduct has emerged: students and researchers can now generate seemingly original content that is essentially paraphrased or reorganized from training data without proper attribution or genuine intellectual contribution (Dehouche, 2021; Dwivedi et al., 2023). Current plagiarism detection systems, primarily based on string matching and shallow semantic comparison, struggle to identify such AI-assisted pseudo-original texts, resulting in widespread concern about fairness in assessment, erosion of critical thinking skills, and the devaluation of academic credentials (Sengar et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2024). As a consequence, academic integrity, once upheld through mutual trust and verifiable originality, is undergoing a radical transformation in the digital age.

Investigating these emerging risks is of urgent theoretical and practical significance. Theoretically, it extends discussions on technology ethics and responsible innovation by revealing how perceived usefulness and ease of use – core constructs of the Technology Acceptance Model – may inadvertently encourage unethical behavior when not balanced by perceived risk (Al-Kfairy et al., 2024). Practically, clarifying the evasion mechanisms of LLMs provides direct evidence for upgrading detection algorithms, refining institutional policies, and redesigning assessment methods that emphasize process over product. Such efforts are essential to preserving public trust in higher education and scholarly publishing in an era where generative AI is becoming ubiquitous.

Although the academic community has recognized the disruptive potential of LLMs, three critical gaps remain underexplored. First, while existing studies confirm that LLMs can generate plausible academic text, few have systematically demonstrated whether such content can simultaneously appear highly original on the surface while remaining fundamentally derivative or plagiarized, thereby evading established review mechanisms (Morocco-Clarke et al., 2024). Second, the influence of generated text length on detection evasion has received almost no empirical attention, despite its practical importance for distinguishing short assignments from theses and journal articles (Wang & Chen, 2024). Third, from a developmental perspective, there is a lack of forward-looking analysis on how the academic community should respond if future iterations of LLMs achieve near-perfect imitation of human scholarly writing, rendering current detection tools obsolete (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2024).

Addressing these gaps, the present study poses the following three interrelated research questions that remain unchanged from the original submission: 1) Can generative large language models produce content that appears highly original on the surface but is fundamentally plagiarized, thereby evading existing review mechanisms? 2) How do differences in the length of generated papers influence their evasion capabilities? 3) From a developmental perspective, how should the academic community respond to the possibility that generative papers may evolve to fully evade detection?

To ensure focus and methodological rigor, this study delimits its scope to text-based generative LLMs applied to academic writing tasks (e.g., essays, literature reviews, and full research papers), explicitly excluding image-generation models (e.g., DALL-E, Stable Diffusion) and code-generation models (e.g., GitHub Copilot). The empirical component centers on four mainstream Chinese and international LLMs – ByteDance Doubao, Tencent Yuanbao, Baidu Wenxin Yiyan, and OpenAI ChatGPT – evaluated primarily through the Chinese plagiarism detection platform PaperPass, which offers superior performance in identifying AI-generated traces in Chinese-language academic texts.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

## Generative Large Language Models and the Crisis of Academic Integrity in Education

Generative large language models (LLMs) have profoundly changed academic writing practices in higher education (Bao et al., 2025; Meyer et al., 2023; Mo & Crosthwaite, 2025). Models such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Wenxin Yiyan, and Doubao can generate coherent essays, literature reviews, and complete manuscripts within seconds (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Sengar et al., 2024). Students and researchers now use these tools to complete assignments and draft papers with minimal personal effort (Cui & Zhang, 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024). Traditional plagiarism, which depends on direct copying, has become rare because LLMs automatically paraphrase and restructure existing knowledge (Cotton et al., 2024; Dehouche, 2021; Morocco-Clarke et al., 2024). This shift creates a new form of misconduct that appears original on the surface but lacks genuine intellectual contribution (Cutri et al., 2021; Perkins, 2023). Current detection tools, including Turnitin and PaperPass, struggle to identify such AI-assisted pseudo-original texts because they mainly rely on string-matching algorithms (Currie, 2023; Wang & Chen, 2024).

Academic integrity traditionally rests on honesty, trust, and proper attribution of ideas (McIntire et al., 2024; Pudasaini et al., 2024). The widespread adoption of LLMs now threatens these core values (McIntire et al., 2024). Many institutions have responded with strict policies: several Chinese universities completely prohibit the submission of LLM-generated content for degree requirements (Jiao et al., 2025; Nozza et al., 2020), while international journals hurriedly require authors to declare AI assistance (Peters et al., 2024; Sagnier et al., 2020). Despite these measures, enforcement remains inconsistent across countries and institutions (Tammeleht & Löfström, 2024). Educators and publish ers face growing difficulty in distinguishing human-authored work from machine-generated text, which undermines fair assessment and erodes public trust in academic credentials (Tahar et al., 2020; Yenduri et al., 2024). The educational community therefore urgently needs evidence-based understanding of how LLMs actually evade existing safeguards.

## Theoretical Framework and Identified Research Gaps

Academic integrity in the age of generative AI constitutes the central theoretical concern of this study (Browne, 2022; Cao et al., 2023). Generative large language models dramatically lower the cost of producing apparently original academic work, thereby transforming the very meaning of authorship, originality, and honest effort (Dehouche, 2021; Dwivedi et al., 2023; Morocco-Clarke et al., 2024). To explain why students and researchers willingly adopt tools that enable potential misconduct, this study employs the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) as its primary theoretical lens (Al Kurdi et al., 2020; Davis, 1989). TAM posits that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are the two primary determinants of technology adoption (Buscemi, 2023; Hadi et al., 2024). In academic contexts, users perceive LLMs as highly useful and extremely easy to use, which strongly predicts their intention to integrate these models into writing and assessment tasks (Gupta, 2024; Kim, 2024).

However, classical TAM was developed for productivity tools in organizational settings and does not originally account for ethical risk perception (Bengesi et al., 2024; Katebi et al., 2022). This study therefore explicitly extends TAM by introducing perceived ethical risk as a critical moderating construct when the same technology can be weaponized to evade academic integrity policies (Alto, 2023; Korinek & Vipra, 2024). This extension directly responds to the unique challenge of the GenAI era: the easier and more useful a tool becomes, the greater the temptation to misuse it for unacknowledged authorship (Amankwah-Amoah et al., 2024). The extended TAM thus provides a coherent framework for interpreting both the rapid adoption of LLMs in education and the resulting threats to research integrity (Li et al., 2024; Mannuru et al., 2023). The three research gaps identified earlier (surface originality with underlying plagiarism, influence of text length, and future institutional responses) are therefore examined through this academic-integrity-focused extension of TAM.

## RESEARCH METHODS

This study employs a cross-analysis of textual data and a multi-case interview approach to explore the evasion capabilities of generative large language models (LLMs) in academic plagiarism detection and their broader implications. The analysis focuses on four mainstream LLMs in China: Byte Doubao, Tencent Yuanbao, Wenxin Yiyan, and ChatGPT, using PaperPass as the primary plagiarism detection tool alongside the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) framework. PaperPass was chosen for its advantages in Chinese-language plagiarism detection, cost-effectiveness, data privacy protection, and the ability to identify traces of AI-generated content. By contrast, tools like CNKI and Turnitin are more suited to large institutions and international contexts. The research team evaluated these models’ performance in generating academic content and assessed the effectiveness and limitations of their evasion mechanisms. For the interview portion, the research team obtained verbal consent from the participants and recorded and transcribed the interviews to facilitate subsequent review of the study.

## Cross-Analysis of Textual Data

Cross-analysis of textual data serves as the core method of this study, aimed at evaluating the originality and replicability exhibited by generative LLMs in academic writing by comparing their outputs with original texts. On 15 January 2025, the research team conducted eight separate targeted searches in the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), the largest and most authoritative academic database in mainland China, using the following precise Chinese terms: “生成式人工智能,” “ChatGPT,” “大语言模型检测,” “学术不端,” “AI学术诚信,” “生成式AI 学术写作,” “大模型论文代 写,” and “ChatGPT 学术伦理” (Their direct English equivalents are “generative AI academia,” “ChatGPT,” “large language model plagiarism detection,” “academic misconduct,” “AI academic integrity,” “generative AI academic writing,” “large model paper ghostwriting,” and “ChatGPT academic ethics”). All searches were uniformly restricted to peer-reviewed journal articles published between 1 January 2022 and 15 January 2025 in China’s highest-tier journals (CSSCI, Peking University Core, AMI, and CSCD). The eight searches returned 789, 642, 598, 476, 513, 429, 387, and 355 records respectively (total before deduplication: 4189). After rigorous automated and manual deduplication across all result sets and removal of non-article items, exactly 300 unique, full-text articles remained as the final source corpus. The complete list of these 300 articles is permanently and openly accessible at https://docs.qq.com/sheet/DZnNsd2pWVHVPZEp5.

The selection of the final 30 experimental articles followed a rigorous, fully documented, two-stage purposive sampling process. In Stage 1 (12–14 January 2025), two researchers independently screened all 300 articles first by title, abstract, and keywords, and then read the full texts of all promising candidates. An article was classified as “high relevance” only if its central argument explicitly and substantively addressed the intersection of generative AI and academic integrity concerns (plagiarism or pseudo-originality, detection evasion mechanisms, authorship ethics, or governance implications); it was rated “medium” if the topic appeared but was not the core focus; and “low” if entirely absent. Minor initial differences were thoroughly discussed until complete consensus was achieved, yielding exactly 118 high-relevance articles. In Stage 2 (15–16 January 2025), the two researchers jointly reexamined these 118 articles and selected the final 30 based on three explicit criteria: (a) richest empirical evidence of real-world detection evasion or failure, (b) deepest theoretical discussion of authorship and originality in the GenAI era, and (c) clearest and most actionable policy or governance recommendations. Representative inclusions are ID 006 (direct empirical comparison of LLMgenerated vs. human academic texts), ID 012 (detailed documentation of detection tool failures), and ID 030 (exceptional methodological rigor). High-quality articles such as ID 002 and ID 014 were excluded because their primary focus lay on technical model architecture or non-academic applications rather than integrity issues. This transparent, replicable, and consensus-driven procedure guarantees that the final 30 articles represent the most authoritative and directly relevant Chinese scholarship for the subsequent experiments.

## Semi-Structured Interviews

To complement the textual cross-analysis with firsthand practitioner insights into real-world detection challenges, ethical risk perception, and institutional responses, this study employed semi-structured interviews. This method was chosen because it ensures consistency across core topics while remaining flexible enough to capture unexpected yet critical nuances that typically emerge when experienced reviewers and developers discuss rapidly evolving generative AI practices.

Participants were recruited exclusively through purposive sampling to achieve maximum informational relevance and diversity of perspectives. Selection criteria required at least five years of seniorlevel experience in evaluating or producing Chinese academic manuscripts, active familiarity with current plagiarism and AI-detection tools, and direct exposure to suspected LLM-generated content within the past two years. The final sample of 18 participants was deliberately balanced across four stakeholder groups: journal editors from CSSCI-listed outlets, full professors supervising postgraduate theses at top universities in China, senior publishing-house reviewers, university research integrity officers, and AI enterprise engineers from leading Chinese large-model development teams (Tencent). This balanced composition ensured that academic evaluation perspectives were systematically confronted with the technical viewpoints of those closest to model design and training (Shown in Table 1).

Interviews were conducted one-on-one via Tencent Meeting between 20 February and 10 March 2025, lasting 45–70 minutes each, and were audio-recorded with explicit prior consent. A common protocol of nine open-ended questions was used, with flexible probing tailored to each participant’s expertise. Recordings were transcribed verbatim within 48 hours. Manual inductive thematic analysis was performed in three stages: initial open coding to identify recurring patterns directly from the raw data, grouping of related codes into sub-themes, and final abstraction into higher-order themes. The two researchers first coded several transcripts independently, reconciled differences through discussion until full consensus on the codebook was reached, then divided and cross-checked the remaining transcripts. The resulting themes provided essential real-world triangulation and explanatory depth for the experimental findings on evasion mechanisms, length effects, and necessary governance reforms.

## Ethics Statement

Ethical approval for the human-participant elements of this research was granted by the authors’ institutional research ethics committee (committee name and approval reference provided on the title page for editorial use). All procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki. Adult participants provided informed consent with the option to withdraw at any time. Personal identifiers were removed during transcription, and anonymized data were stored on password-protected drives accessible only to the research team. The textual dataset comprised published journal articles and contained no personally identifiable information.

## RESEARCH FINDINGS

This section presents the empirical results from the textual generation experiments, plagiarism detection tests, and semi-structured interviews, structured into three themes that directly correspond to the research questions. All findings are interpreted through the extended Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) framework, linking users’ perceived usefulness and ease of use of LLMs to their evasion capabilities, while emphasizing the moderating role of perceived ethical risk in amplifying potential misconduct. As a qualitative study, we deepen descriptive findings through thematic analysis and triangulation with interview data, revealing underlying patterns and insights rather than testing quantitative hypotheses. This approach aligns with established qualitative standards, providing rigorous depth without inferential statistics, as the focus is on contextual understanding of AI-enabled academic risks.

## Surface Originality Masking Derivative Content

To examine whether generative LLMs can produce content that appears highly original on the surface but is fundamentally plagiarized, thereby evading review mechanisms, the research team conducted human confusion experiments on short paragraphs and full-paper generation tests using the four models. These directly relate to TAM: high perceived ease of use allows rapid creation of plausible texts, yet low perceived ethical risk encourages unacknowledged use when outputs evade detection. The human evaluations and detection metrics consistently demonstrated that LLMs excel at superficial restructuring of existing ideas from training data, creating outputs that pass initial scrutiny but lack true intellectual novelty upon deeper examination. This not only underscores the models’ perceived usefulness in academic workflows but also highlights the ethical risk when such tools enable misconduct without immediate consequences, potentially eroding trust in scholarly processes. Participants often misidentified AI content as human, indicating basic evasion in short texts. This highlights TAM’s perceived usefulness, as models produce realistic snippets that could tempt users to substitute for original work without raising alarms. Such evasion fosters a false sense of security among users, lowering perceived ethical risk and encouraging broader adoption in high-stakes academic tasks like abstract drafting or introduction composition. A participant shared:

In my recent thesis reviews, I’ve seen abstracts that read perfectly fluent but lack that human spark of novel insight. They’re like polished summaries of existing ideas, not true synthesis. When I probe the student, they can’t explain the logic deeply, which makes me suspect heavy AI assistance, but without proof, I can only recommend revisions. It’s frustrating because tools like PaperPass catch obvious copies but miss these subtle paraphrases, eroding trust in the entire process. We need better training for reviewers to spot the absence of creative leaps, especially in concise sections where AI shines. (P03, Section Editor (CSSCI Education Journal))

Full-paper pseudo-originality mechanisms. Full-paper tests using Yuanbao, Doubao, and Wenxin Yiyan (Tables 3 and 4) showed low plagiarism rates but high AIGC suspicion, confirming surface originality masking derivative nature. Paragraph-by-paragraph generation with ChatGPT (Table 5) further improved evasion, linking to TAM’s perceived ease of use enabling “human-mimicking” outputs that heighten ethical risk when undetected. Participants elaborated on how full manuscripts generated by AI often maintain structural coherence but reveal underlying plagiarism through inconsistent depth or over-reliance on common phrases from the training corpora, making them appear as reorganized compilations rather than original contributions. This pattern exacerbates ethical concerns, as users perceive LLMs as highly useful for producing submission-ready documents quickly, yet the risk of undetected misconduct grows when detection tools fail to flag semantic similarities to source materials. A participant explained:

As an editor, I handle dozens of submissions weekly, and lately, full manuscripts come in with impeccable structure but repetitive phrasing in longer sections. It’s like the AI ran out of creativity midway. In one case, a student’s paper had flawless structure but no evolving argument, which screamed AI. We use suspicion rates to flag them, but authors deny it, claiming heavy editing. Without better tools, we’re forced to accept on faith, which undermines our journal’s integrity standards and makes me worry about future floods of such content. Ethical guidelines must evolve to mandate disclosure, or we’ll see a surge in pseudo-original works. (P02, Managing Editor (CSSCI Law Journal))

## Length-Dependent Evasion Dynamics

Our results from varying generation modes reveal that differences in generated paper length influence evasion capabilities, with longer texts exposing more “machine-like” traits. Through TAM, this demonstrates perceived usefulness declining with length, as ease of use for short outputs fosters low ethical risk, but complexity in long texts increases detectability. The experiments illustrated that while short segments evade scrutiny effectively due to their simplicity, extended papers amplify flaws like logical repetition and shallow analysis, making evasion less reliable as length grows. This dynamic ties directly to users’ perceptions: LLMs are seen as highly useful for quick tasks, but ethical risk escalates when longer outputs reveal the models’ limitations, prompting calls for length-specific safeguards in academic settings.

Short versus long text detection variability. Short-paragraph tests showed strong evasion, with low suspicion rates, but full-paper outputs exhibited higher AIGC flags in extended sections. This ties to TAM’s perceived ease of use enabling quick short-text success, yet amplifying ethical risk in academic misuse.

## DISCUSSION

## Surface Originality Masking Derivative Content: Implications for Evasion and Integrity

The findings from the short-text confusion experiments and full-paper generation tests affirm that generative LLMs can indeed produce content that appears highly original on the surface while being fundamentally derivative, thereby evading conventional review mechanisms. As demonstrated in Theme 1, models like Yuanbao and Doubao achieved low plagiarism rates (averages of 12.21% and 18.31%) but high AIGC suspicion (76.64% and 70.14%), indicating a capacity for superficial restructuring that mimics human writing without true innovation. This aligns with prior literature highlighting how LLMs paraphrase and reorganize existing knowledge from training data, creating pseudo-originality that bypasses string-matching tools like Turnitin (Dehouche, 2021; Morocco-Clarke et al., 2024). Critically, this evasion capability extends the crisis of academic integrity discussed in the literature review, where traditional plagiarism has evolved into subtler AI-assisted misconduct (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Sengar et al., 2024). The extended TAM framework is justified here, as perceived usefulness drives users to adopt LLMs for efficient content creation, but the absence of perceived ethical risk enables deliberate exploitation, undermining authorship and originality. This theoretical extension critiques classical TAM for overlooking ethics in academic contexts, revealing how ease of use not only facilitates generation but also normalizes unacknowledged reliance, potentially devaluing scholarly credentials if unchecked.

Participants’ accounts further deepen this critique, emphasizing that surface fluency in AI outputs often conceals intellectual shallowness, echoing literature on the erosion of critical thinking in AIpermeated education (Wang & Chen, 2024; Yan et al., 2024). For instance, the confusion experiment’s misidentification patterns suggest that short texts exploit localized context to evade detection, but full manuscripts reveal repetitive structures, supporting a critical view that LLMs represent a double-edged sword: useful for brainstorming yet risky for final submissions. This finding challenges optimistic views in the literature review of LLMs as mere assistants (Gupta, 2024; Kim, 2024), arguing instead for a more skeptical stance – generative models may amplify inequality, favoring those who misuse them over ethical practitioners. Theoretically, this justifies integrating perceived ethical risk into TAM, as it moderates adoption intentions in integrity-sensitive domains, prompting calls for empirical tests in future studies to validate this extension across cultural contexts.

## Length-Dependent Evasion Dynamics: Variability and Theoretical Validation

Results from varying generation lengths reveal clear divergences in LLM evasion capabilities, with short texts achieving stronger concealment than longer ones, directly answering RQ2. As detailed in Theme 2, short-paragraph tests exhibited robust evasion through concise mimicry, while full-paper outputs showed heightened AIGC suspicion due to emerging formulaic patterns and logical gaps. This variability corroborates literature on LLMs’ limitations in sustaining depth over extended content (Morocco-Clarke et al., 2024; Wang & Chen, 2024), where initial fluency degrades, exposing machinelike traits that human writing avoids. Through the extended TAM lens, this demonstrates how perceived ease of use excels in brief tasks, fostering low ethical risk, but declines in complex, lengthy outputs, increasing detectability and underscoring the need for context-aware safeguards. Critically, this finding critiques the literature’s overemphasis on LLMs’ general utility (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Sengar et al., 2024), highlighting instead a length threshold where usefulness wanes, potentially deterring misuse if institutions leverage it for targeted detection.

Interview themes add critical depth, revealing that reviewers perceive short texts as seamlessly human-like but flag longer ones for repetitive degradation, aligning with the review’s discussion of enforcement inconsistencies (Balalle & Pannilage, 2025; Yan et al., 2024). This supports extending TAM with ethical risk, as length exposes unoriginality, elevating perceived consequences and justifying theoretical refinement for AI in education. However, a critique arises: if models evolve to overcome length limitations, as hinted in developmental perspectives (Al-Kfairy et al., 2024), current divergences may vanish, rendering TAM insufficient without dynamic risk factors. Future research should test this critically, perhaps through longitudinal studies, to validate whether length-dependent evasion persists amid advancements, ensuring the framework’s robustness in predicting user behavior and guiding policy.

## Institutional Responses to Evolving Evasion: Forward-Looking Strategies and Ethical Imperatives

Integrated findings propose multifaceted responses to potential full evasion by future LLMs, addressing RQ3 from a developmental viewpoint. As outlined in Theme 3, blockchain tracking and ethicsintegrated assessments emerge as key strategies, framed by TAM to elevate perceived ethical risk and counter misuse driven by perceived usefulness. This echoes literature calls for proactive governance amid AI’s rapid evolution (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2024), where reactive tools like PaperPass fall short, necessitating shifts to process-oriented evaluations that mitigate undetected imitation. Critically, this justifies the extended TAM: by incorporating ethical risk, it explains why users overlook long-term consequences, advocating for policies that rebalance adoption intentions toward responsibility, thus bridging gaps in the review’s fragmented regulatory landscape (Balalle & Pannilage, 2025; Morocco-Clarke et al., 2024).

Stakeholder insights deepen this critique, stressing education to heighten risk awareness, aligning with TAM extensions for ethical domains (Al-Kfairy et al., 2024; Gupta, 2024). However, a critical tension arises: while usefulness drives innovation, unchecked evasion could democratize access yet exacerbate inequality, favoring tech-savvy users over others – a point underexplored in prior literature (Dehouche, 2021; Wang & Chen, 2024). This demands theoretical refinement, testing TAM in global contexts to critique Western-centric assumptions and ensure equitable governance. Ultimately, these responses position academia to harness LLMs ethically, transforming risks into opportunities for enhanced integrity.

## CONTRIBUTION AND NOVELTY

This study makes several interrelated theoretical and empirical contributions to emerging debates on generative AI and academic integrity. Theoretically, it extends the Technology Acceptance Model by foregrounding perceived ethical risk as a core construct when tools designed for productivity are repurposed to circumvent integrity norms. By demonstrating how high perceived usefulness and ease of use interact with low perceived ethical risk to normalize AI-assisted pseudo-originality, the study shows that classical TAM is incomplete in integrity-sensitive domains. Empirically, it provides rare, systematically derived evidence that LLMs can generate surface-original yet fundamentally derivative academic texts in Chinese, across different lengths and generation strategies, while still evading widely used plagiarism and AIGC detection tools. The three research questions are answered in a tightly integrated manner, linking confusion experiments, full-paper generation tests, and thematic interview findings to a coherent account of how pseudo-originality is produced, perceived, and contested in practice.

Building on these insights, the article introduces the original concept of “surface-originality evasion ecology” to capture a generalizable socio-technical pattern that has not been theorized in prior work. This concept describes the dynamic configuration in which LLM-generated texts routinely achieve apparent originality at the surface level, with evasion capacity varying by length and generation strategy, while institutional safeguards react belatedly and unevenly. The three themes identified in this study constitute the core dimensions of this ecology: surface originality masking derivative content, length-dependent evasion dynamics, and institutional responses to evolving evasion. By naming and theorizing this surface-originality evasion ecology, the study not only addresses a key blind spot in TAM – its neglect of integrity-sensitive contexts and institutional feedback loops – but also offers a portable analytical lens for future research on AI-enabled academic risks across different languages, sectors, and regulatory environments.

## CONCLUSION

This study has examined how generative large language models reshape academic integrity by enabling texts that are highly original in appearance yet fundamentally derivative, revealing systematic gaps in existing detection and governance. Through cross-analysis of Chinese journal articles, controlled generation experiments with four mainstream LLMs, and multi-case interviews with key stakeholders, the findings show that current safeguards struggle to address three intertwined phenomena: surface originality masking derivative content, where LLMs produce fluent but pseudo-original prose that evades string-matching tools; length-dependent evasion dynamics, whereby short segments conceal machine traits more effectively than extended manuscripts; and institutional responses to evolving evasion, in which governance, policy, and pedagogy lag behind rapidly advancing models. Interpreted through an extended Technology Acceptance Model that incorporates perceived ethical risk, the study demonstrates that high perceived usefulness and ease of use, combined with low perceived risk, normalize AI-assisted misconduct and erode the value of scholarly credentials. On this basis, the paper advances the new concept of “surface-originality evasion ecology” to describe the emergent socio-technical configuration in which pseudo-originality, length-calibrated evasion strategies, and uneven institutional counter-measures interact as a single system. This concept offers a portable lens for future comparative work on AI-enabled academic risks and highlights the need for proactive reforms – ranging from process-based assessment and ethics education to stronger disclosure norms and technical tracking tools – if academic communities are to harness generative AI responsibly while preserving the integrity of research and education.
