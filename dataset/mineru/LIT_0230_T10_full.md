## ABSTRACT

Background: This study explored the factors that influence the balance between academic integrity and the effective use of GenAI tools in higher education. It focused on the role of institutional guidelines in enhancing the responsible use of GenAI technologies to enhance academic integrity.

Objectives: The study was theoretically grounded in the Technology Acceptance Model and the Theory of Planned Behaviour to investigate the factors that promote academic integrity in using GenAI tools (RQ1), their impact and institutional strategies to effectively mitigate ethical risks (RQ2) and the model practices to support the ethical and effective use in higher education (RQ3). Methods: The PRISMA framework was used to systematically review and thematically synthesise the results of 213 peerreviewed articles published between January 2021 and May 2025.

Results: Finding indicates that academic support, defined by structured training, technical scaffolding, and perceived usefulness, is critical to enabling ethical GenAI use. Additionally, student self-regulation, as influenced by behavioural control and goal setting, was associated with greater academic integrity in GenAI-mediated learning. Whereas institutional policies varied widely, those with transparent, adaptive and discipline-responsive governance frameworks more effectively mitigated academic misconduct. Indeed, the model practices included GenAI ethics committees, interactive GenAI literacy modules, and the developer-educator collaborations to promote algorithmic transparency.

Conclusions: A comprehensive systems-based approach that encompasses academic support, self-regulation and ethical guidelines is critical for the responsible use of GenAI tools in education. Hence, to preserve academic integrity while nurturing innovation, institutions should integrate GenAI ethics into curricular design, faculty development and cross-sectoral policy frameworks. Future research may expand into multilingual and longitudinal analyses to support equitable and sustainable GenAI integration across diverse educational settings.

As higher education evolves in response to GenAI advancements, ethical considerations must remain at the forefront, shaping policies and pedagogical practices that preserve integrity in leveraging AI's educational potential.

## 1.1 | GenAI Use Conceptual Framework

This study integrates the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) to systematically explore the ethical and effective use of GenAI tools in higher education. These two were complemented by the Self-Regulated Learning Theory (Zimmerman  2002), which provides a structured foundation for evaluating the factors, impact and model pedagogical practices in the use of GenAI tools to preserve academic integrity in higher education, as illustrated in Figure 1.

The TAM by Davis (1989, cited in Silva 2015) serves as the foundational theory for understanding the integration of GenAI tools in higher education. According to TAM, technology acceptance is influenced by Perceived Usefulness (PU), which is the extent to which GenAI tools are accepted to enhance the learning outcomes. The Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU) refers to the accessibility and user-friendliness of GenAI tools for faculty and students. These TAM elements indicate a direct influence on the users' Attitude Toward Use (ATU), which is further shaped by institutional support, GenAI literacy initiatives and policy frameworks (Ajzen  1991). Hence, TAM predicts Behavioural Intention (BI) to adopt GenAI tools, which determines their actual usage patterns in educational settings. Thus, this framework was critical to investigate not only the technical adoption but also the proactive strategies that are embedded within academic curricula as the factors that promote the ethical use of GenAI tools.

## 2 | Literature Review

The integration of GenAI tools into higher education has generated extensive discourse regarding their impact on academic integrity. Indeed, as AI-driven platforms offer significant benefits in personalised learning, assessment automation and knowledge generation, they also introduce ethical concerns related to plagiarism, accountability and institutional monitoring. This review examines the literature reviews on the factors, impact, and model practices in the integration of AI tools in higher education.

## 2.1 | Factors Promoting Ethical Integration of GenAI Tools in Higher Education

The empirical studies have emphasised the complexities of the ethical integration of GenAI tools in education by pointing to various digital literacy issues, institutional policy enforcement and the students' autonomy. According to Gavira Durón and Jiménez-Preciado (2025), significant AI literacy challenges are attributed to the inconsistent guidelines for ethical implementation across institutions. Additionally, in spite of educators and administrators acknowledging the expanding roles of GenAI tools in education, their response to addressing academic integrity issues remains fragmented, and they lack cohesive frameworks for integrating GenAI tools while upholding ethical standards.

Therefore, Bittle and El-Gayar (2025) examined the institutional strategies for mitigating academic dishonesty but predominantly focused on reactive measures, such as plagiarism detection tools, rather than proactive interventions like curriculum redesign or pedagogical adaptation. A more comprehensive approach would involve embedding GenAI ethics into faculty training and course design, ensuring that students engage critically with AI-generated content. Batista et  al.  (2024) synthesised the results of 37 studies published between 2023 and 2024 on AI-related assessment integrity and pedagogical strategies. However, their study did not include proposing concrete frameworks to mitigate emerging academic integrity risks. Similarly, Cai et al. (2024) highlighted concerns about educator preparedness and the absence of ethical safeguards in AI-mediated learning based on their synthesis of 158 studies published from 2021 to 2024. Indeed, their systematic literature review did not assess whether higher education institutions possess the requisite infrastructure to establish equitable GenAI governance mechanisms that could ensure responsible GenAI deployment in learning.

Moreover, Cai et  al.  (2024) introduced Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development as a theoretical framework for AIassisted learning, suggesting that AI tools can scaffold students intellectual growth, and highlighted concerns about educator preparedness and the absence of ethical precautionary measures. However, they did not sufficiently explore how faculty should regulate GenAI-driven assessments in higher education, which prompts the need for further discussion on ethical supervision in GenAI-mediated education, just as Rodrigues et  al.  (2025) argued that AI should serve as a complementary tool rather than a substitute for human instruction. Despite this, Rodrigues et al. (2025) also pointed to students' cognitive and behavioural challenges in distinguishing GenAI-generated content from original human work. Given the ability of GenAI tools to autonomously generate complex academic content, students' literacy in verifying GenAI outputs should, therefore, be more rigorously developed to ensure the integrity of adopting these tools.

Furthermore, Dimeli and Kostas  (2025) emphasised the limited investigations into AI ethics within primary and secondary education in their synthesis review of 50 articles published between 2023 and 2024. Although this highlights the need for interdisciplinary research on AI adoption across educational levels, it does not provide actionable recommendations for higher education institutions to develop institutionwide GenAI integration frameworks. Consistently, Pinto et al. (2023), based on their synthesis of 171 studies from 2018 to 2023, indicated the importance of institutional monitoring in machine learning adoption. However, they did not focus on the practical strategies for higher learning institutions to regulate GenAI-mediated education without compromising academic rigour.

## 2.2 | GenAI Adoption on Academic Integrity and Institutional Strategies for Mitigation

The integration of GenAI tools in higher education presents other challenges of originality, authorship, and integrity of academic practices, which necessitate structured institutional policies to mitigate academic quality risks. Naznin et  al.  (2025) explored AI writing tools, observing increased efficiency and concerns about the students' overdependence on these tools. Their overdependency may diminish their independent critical thinking and analytical reasoning skills. This issue reflects a wider challenge regarding GenAI's role in promoting cognitive engagement compared to enabling superficial automation of academic tasks. Newton and Jones (2024) evaluated traditional assessment methods, arguing that multiple-choice exams and essays increasingly become vulnerable to AI-generated responses that exceed human output. Their study advocates for critical thinking-based assessments on the one hand. However, their study does not propose systematic alternatives for ensuring authenticity in AI-assisted learning environments. Indeed, as GenAI output content continues to challenge traditional academic conventions, higher education institutions should reconsider whether existing assessment models can uphold academic integrity or call for curriculum reforms.

In addition, institutional responses to GenAI threats to academic integrity vary widely and are often reactionary rather than strategic. Sozon et  al.  (2024) outlined several intervention strategies, including implementing faculty training programmes, AI detection tools and educational policy updates. Nevertheless, their systematic review did not assess the long-term effectiveness of these measures. Similarly, Molina-Carmona and García-Peñalvo  (2025) emphasised the importance of complementing AI's benefits with ethical governance, focusing primarily on institutional compliance rather than the student-centred approaches to responsible GenAI use. Moreover, Bittle and El-Gayar (2025) identified the academic challenges associated with GenAI-induced dishonesty and bias in their review of 41 studies published between 2021 and 2024. Still, they did not sufficiently explore institutional interventions designed to uphold academic integrity as they facilitate GenAI adoption in higher educational settings. Perkins and Roe (2024a, 2024b) examined 142 academic integrity policies from HEIs between 2022 and 2023, and they concluded that most institutions have yet to formulate explicit AI use guidelines in learning. Indeed, their study provides valuable empirical insights into institutional unpreparedness but does not propose a structured model to address AI-mediated academic misconduct.

Furthermore, Imran and Almusharraf  (2023) synthesised 30 studies from 2022 to 2023 to discuss challenges associated with AI-generated plagiarism. However, they did not provide a comprehensive evaluation of strategies that institutions can adopt to regulate AI-assisted writing. Equally, Singh and Ngai (2024), in their synthesis of 151 university responses from 2022 to 2023, highlighted varying institutional attitudes toward GenAI, in which they found that some perceive GenAI as a threat to academic integrity, while others advocated for its structured integration. Hence, the absence of consensus among HEIs underscores the need for the possibility of standardising integrity frameworks based on the synthesis of available empirical evidence as a solution that reconciles institutional strategy with AI innovation.

A recurring challenge in the literature is the assumption that AI-generated misconduct can be easily regulated through detection tools alone, overlooking the students' deeper cognitive and behavioural shifts associated with AI adoption in educational settings. Hence, effective mitigation strategies should extend beyond technical regulation and incorporate curriculum design adjustments that encourage students to engage critically with AI-generated content, promoting integrity, critical reasoning and accountability.

## 2.3    |    Ethical Practices in GenAI Use in Teaching and Learning

The ethical and effective practices of GenAI integration in the teaching and learning process have been investigated by numerous empirical studies that advocate for responsible GenAI integration in education, but few offer concrete, evidence-based strategies beyond generalised ethical directives. For example, Chugh et al. (2025) discussed the role of GenAI tools in student learning engagement and proposed adaptive learning platforms that are designed to personalise learning pathways. However, they did not evaluate whether such platforms unintentionally promote passive learning rather than fostering intellectual rigour. Hence, GenAI tools' ability to automate content generation presents concerns about active student learning engagement when using GenAI tools to generate responses, warranting further investigation into strategies that should promote active learning and critical analysis.

Indeed, Allam et  al.  (2025) proposed the concept of living intelligence, which was explored to frame AI as a collaborative cognitive entity rather than a purely computational tool. This living intelligence concept aligns AI with the constructivist and sociocultural learning theories by Vygotsky  (1978), advocating for the AI-human co-learning models. However, systematic review studies on its practical implementation remain scarce. Thus, further systematic empirical research is required to understand how the GenAI tools can facilitate meaningful student-instructor interactions while preserving pedagogical integrity. Additionally, Xia et al. (2024) proposed restructuring assessments to design them to cultivate self-regulated learning practices in GenAI tool-integrated learning environments. However, their effectiveness in preserving academic integrity remains largely unexplored. Given AI's increasing roles in shaping educational experiences, HEIs must implement pilot studies to assess whether AI-driven assessments successfully enhance independent learning while safeguarding intellectual authenticity.

Moreover, Xia et al. (2024), based on a review of 32 studies, argued that HEIs lack transformative assessment models that promote integrity, self-regulated learning, and responsible AI use. Despite this, their study did not explain how GenAI-powered learning could complement traditional educational methodologies. Additionally, Kangwa et al. (2025) synthesised 148 studies from 2021 to 2024 and stressed the need for well-structured AI learning strategies. On the contrary, they did not examine the instructional models that effectively balance GenAI-enhanced learning with conventional pedagogy.

Furthermore, Melisa et al. (2025) reviewed 19 studies from 2023 to 2024, highlighting a need to understand GenAI's long-term impact on academic integrity regarding student motivation and critical reasoning skills. Though very insightful, the evaluation did not include how GenAI pedagogical and integration frameworks could mitigate GenAI's potential negative effects on students' independent and analytical thought. In addition, Ofusori and Hendradi  (2025) synthesised 35 studies and emphasised the importance of institutional supervision in AI-assisted assessment without focusing on developing a scalable model for integrating AI tools into structured evaluation mechanisms. Therefore, it is still very important to:

• Identify the factors that promote integrity in integrating GenAI tools in higher education, focusing on institutional policies, educator preparedness and equitable AI governance models.

• Evaluate how the integration of GenAI tools affects academic integrity and examine institutional responses to mitigate ethical risks and preserve authenticity in student learning outcomes.

• Develop evidence-based model practices for GenAIenhanced pedagogy that ensure that GenAI is responsibly integrated for better assessments, student learning engagement and knowledge retention.

Hence, adhering to the PRISMA framework, this study rigorously synthesised empirical findings to articulate the ethical and pedagogical strategies that provide higher education institutions with actionable recommendations for GenAI governance. The research questions that guided this systematic literature review included:

1. What factors influence the integrity of integrating GenAI tools in higher education based on empirical findings from 2021 to 2025?

2. How has the integration of GenAI tools impacted academic integrity, and what institutional strategies effectively mitigate ethical risks?

3. What evidence-based model practices support the ethical and effective use of GenAI tools in teaching and learning?

The study employed the systematic review methodology guided by the PRISMA (Page et  al.  2021a) to explore three research questions. The research process involved searching, identifying and selecting articles following the PRISMA principles of extracting and managing secondary data (Lockwood et al. 2015). This systematic approach safeguards an unbiased and comprehensive data synthesis, aggregated and presented through synthesis tables and charts (Kangwa et  al.  2024). Furthermore, the qualitative deductive and inductive coding methods were applied to analyse existing data and generate new themes (Aromataris and Munn 2024).

## 3.1    |    Search Strategy

The designed search ensured the inclusion of high-quality and peer-reviewed studies to enhance the reliability and validity of the synthesised findings for this study (Page et al. 2021b). Hence, this systematic review focused exclusively on scholarly articles that were rigorously peer-reviewed and that the selected studies would contribute substantively to the discourse on GenAI integration in higher education. Additionally, the search criteria were restricted to the research articles published between January 1st, 2021, and May 31st, 2025. This publication period was selected because of the rapid evolution of GenAI technologies, necessitating an up-to-date review of the latest advancements. Additionally, the increasing integration of AI tools in higher education environments requires an examination of recent empirical and theoretical contributions to the integration processes. Thus, the temporal boundary targeted the most relevant findings and discussions that reflect technological developments and contemporary perspectives.

## 3.2 | Search Strings and Terms

The search strings and terms were systematically designed with a multi-layered approach to query formulation to enable an inclusive sample of relevant empirical studies. Article searches were conducted on PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, ERIC and PsycINFO, as these electronic databases indexed rigorously peer-reviewed published research. The study followed the PRISMA principles to strengthen transparency, reproducibility and rigour in literature sampling (Page et  al.  2021a). Indeed, snowball sampling techniques were additionally used to identify other publications, ensuring the inclusion of results that might not have emerged from the direct searches alone (Lockwood et al. 2015). Search queries were also thematically segmented to address database-specific constraints, enabling tailored strings across electronic databases with varying string limitations (Yusuf et al. 2024). For instance, Web of Science has a maximum of 50 search strings, which made the researchers to use a technique of structured decomposition of queries into distinct thematic categories based on the research question, such as ‘AI ethics and integrity’, ‘institutional mitigation strategies’ and ‘evidence-based GenAI applications in teaching and learning’, to optimise the retrieval efficiency.

Moreover, the search terms formulation integrated Boolean logic, using controlled term indexing and adaptive keyword expansion to optimise precision and retrieval (Yusuf et al. 2024). The search strings were designed to accommodate generalised and tool-specific references to GenAI (Cai et al. 2024), thus capturing all the relevant studies. In addition to the core AI terminology, the search strings included prominent GenAI tools, such as ChatGPT and Gemini (Yusuf et al. 2024), to ensure the wider retrieval of empirical studies that explicitly discuss their adoption in academic integrity and pedagogical innovation. The search based on these search strategies yielded 4862 publications, and all of these underwent rigorous eligibility assessments to filter the results that are well aligned with this study's objectives (Page et al. 2021b). ## 3.3 | Screening and Eligibility Criteria

The initial search result of 4782 articles across all the sampled electronic databases was subjected to the screening criteria that included publication dates from 2021 to 2025, peer-reviewed status, and removal of duplicates, and is presented in Table 2. The three researchers independently reviewed the titles and abstracts to determine eligibility, a process that resulted in a 100% interrater reliability, and a selection of 213 studies for further analysis. The initial discrepancies between the independent researchers were resolved through discussion and by adhering to the established eligibility criteria. The second stage in the screening process, after the screening on each electronic database, was the two researchers' manual screening of the articles by matching each record against the predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inter-rater reliability, calculated by percentage agreement, achieved a 95% agreement rate (Lewis et  al.  2018). Any disagreements were again discussed until a 100% consensus was reached, and the articles that failed to meet quality standards were excluded, resulting in a final selection of studies that met the criteria for the systematic review, as in Table 2.

## 3.4 | Sample Extraction

The search results from electronic databases were retrieved through systematic file extraction to facilitate data organisation and citation management. Then the identified research articles were exported in multiple formats, such as CSV and RIS files, to optimise subsequent analysis and efficient bibliographic management through citation tools such as Zotero and Mendeley (Yusuf et al. 2024). Indeed, this approach streamlined the process of organising the sampled studies, sources and proper referencing for this study. The files were exported in CSV format from the electronic databases and merged into one Excel sheet. Then, data cleansing was done, and duplicates were removed. After removing duplicates, the eligibility of the remaining records was verified by looking through their titles and abstracts, which was performed independently by the researchers to prepare the eligible records for a possible full-text screening (Page et  al.  2021a, 2021b). The differences in the independently selected studies were discussed and evaluated to reach a consensus among the researchers in line with the established eligibility criteria. This process ensured a 100% interrater reliability of the inclusion and exclusion procedure.

## 3.5 | Data Extraction

The data extraction process was adapted from Yusuf et  al.  (2024) and the PRISMA guidelines (Page et  al.  2021a, 2021b). The two researchers independently and manually conducted the data extraction using a predefined coding scheme in Microsoft Excel, and then the third researcher validated and refined the datasets. The sampled studies were systematically classified into distinct categories for in-depth thematic analysis. This data extraction process proceeded in three iterative phases, beginning with the initial coding, where titles and abstracts were reviewed for relevance; followed by the full-text analysis, which ensured comprehensive data representation beyond abstract summaries; and was finalised by a cross-validation, to resolve the discrepancies through consensus-based review to strengthen the inter-coder reliability (Aromataris and Munn  2024; Lockwood et  al.  2015). Indeed, the final dataset was consolidated into an Excel-based repository to enable qualitative thematic synthesis and quantitative trend analysis. ## 3.6    |    Quality Appraisal

The quality of the eligible studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for Systematic Reviews and Research Syntheses (Page et  al.  2021a, 2021b), which evaluates the trustworthiness, relevance, and results of published papers through 11 questions that assess various aspects of study design, conduct, and analysis (Moher et al. 2015). The main elements evaluated by the JBI checklist include proper randomisation of participants, allocation concealment, baseline similarity of treatment groups, blinding of participants and outcome assessors, and reliability of outcome measurements (Aromataris and Munn  2024; Lockwood et al. 2015). Studies were assigned a quality score based on how many JBI checklist items they met and were categorised into high, medium or low quality. Initial quality appraisal was independently conducted by two researchers who evaluated the abstracts for suitability based on inclusion and exclusion criteria and the JBI checklist, with disputes resolved through discussion with a third researcher until consensus was reached, achieving 100% inter-rater reliability. The appraisal process resulted in highquality (n=183) and medium-quality (n=30) with no low-quality articles being included for analysis (Kangwa et al. 2024).

## 3.7 | Data Synthesis

The study adopted a thorough data synthesis approach, employing thematic and narrative synthesis methods in a meticulously structured two-stage process (Aromataris and Munn  2024). 4.3.1.3 | Student Self-Regulation and Ethical Awareness. The other thematic results of the included studies (n = 25) were categorised on the students' self-regulation and ethical awareness. The results indicated that ethical literacy frameworks should guide students' use of GenAI tools to minimise the risks of unintentional plagiarism and overreliance on GenAI-generated content. Findings by Abdi et al. (2025), Bayraktar Balkir and Zehir Topkaya  (2025), and Michel-Villarreal et  al.  (2023) show that students struggled with GenAI transparency and unclear understanding of how GenAI-generated responses impact academic integrity. Thus, institutions may design GenAI literacy programs that are focused on promoting transparency, originality and selfregulation to improve ethical GenAI use. Nikolic et  al.  (2025) and Perkins, Furze, et al. (2024) stated that the institutions that are employing GenAI-powered plagiarism detection models are often struggling to distinguish between human-authored submissions and GenAI-assisted writing, which results in unfairness, especially in grading discrepancies.

Hence, Heugh et al. (2022) and Matthews and Volpe (2023) suggested that to improve GenAI-assisted assessments, universities should invest in adaptive GenAI detection frameworks, ensuring that GenAI-generated plagiarism checks align with evolving GenAI advancements. GenAI detection models should integrate multi-layered verification mechanisms, combining GenAIgenerated response recognition with faculty monitoring in evaluations to improve assessment accuracy (Streletska et al. 2024; Sun, Zhao, and Li  2025). Moreover, GenAI-assisted learning tools must prioritise cognitive augmentation rather than knowledge replacement, ensuring GenAI serves as an aid to critical reasoning instead of an alternative to intellectual engagement. ## 4.4.1 | Academic Integrity Risks and AI-Assisted Misconduct

The use of Generative AI tools in higher education has introduced complex ethical dilemmas concerning academic integrity. Among the sampled studies (n = 39), the increasing prevalence of GenAI-generated content is highlighted, which is undetectable by traditional plagiarism detection methods. This raises concerns about authorship accountability and transparency (Chan  2025a, 2025b; Ekaterina et  al.  2025; Kofinas et al. 2025). Indeed, Abdi et al. (2025), Buniel et al. (2025) and Qu and Wang  (2025) indicated that students are struggling to distinguish GenAI-generated materials from their work, and this increases the risk of unintentional academic misconduct. Additionally, Nikolic et  al.  (2023) and Williams and Fadda  (2023) emphasised that without structured GenAI literacy programmes, universities reported higher incidences of student overreliance on GenAI tools for assignments, which potentially undermines their independent reasoning and development of critical thinking skills.

Moreover, Crawford et  al.  (2023), Hargreaves  (2023) and Plata et  al.  (2023) identified that problems arise in subjects that rely heavily on written composition and analytical reasoning for assessments, where GenAI-generated paraphrasing and fabricated citations make plagiarism detection more challenging. Hence, traditional plagiarism detection software proves largely ineffective against GenAI-assisted modifications and creates gaps in academic integrity enforcement (B. W. Stone  2024). Indeed, fabricated references and citations generated by GenAI tools increase the risks of misinformation and may lead students to unknowingly submit falsified sources (Currie et  al.  2023; Naidu and Sevnarayan  2023). Hence, Striepe et  al.  (2023) suggested that, to mitigate these ethical risks, institutions should revise their academic integrity policies to explicitly address GenAI-assisted research practices by incorporating GenAI literacy training into student orientations and coursework. Additionally, Malik and Terzidis (2025)

proposed adopting adaptive assessment models that prioritise discussion-based evaluations and oral reasoning exercises to reduce GenAI-induced academic dishonesty.

4.4.1.1 | Institutional Safeguards for Academic Integrity. The other set of the sampled studies (n = 19) thematically indicated that the institutional mitigation strategies are fundamental in addressing the academic integrity challenges associated with the use of GenAI tools in education. Murch et  al.  (2025), Symeou et  al.  (2025), and Zlotnikova et  al.  (2025) emphasised the importance of GenAI ethics training programs to enhance the faculty's preparedness; however, the inconsistencies in the training accessibility reduced the efficacy of these interventions. Hence, Evangelista  (2025) and Jin et  al.  (2025) found that the standardised GenAI literacy programs were underdeveloped in many institutions and led to disparities in faculty competency when addressing GenAI-assisted academic misconduct. Although GenAI-inclusive assessment designs are being introduced as a mitigation strategy, Almassaad et al. (2024), Plata et al. (2023) and Wilson  (2025) indicated that faculty members require continuous professional development to enable them to seamlessly integrate GenAI into coursework and preserve academic integrity.

Moreover, to strengthen institutional governance in AI adoption, Katsamakas et  al.  (2024) and Teremetskyi et  al.  (2024) suggested that universities should embed GenAI ethics training into professional development frameworks, so that faculty are equipped with standardised strategies for detecting GenAI misuse and adapting assessment formats accordingly. Bikanga Ada  (2024) and Driessens and Pischetola  (2024) highlighted the necessity of establishing cross-institutional GenAI governance frameworks to facilitate collaborative policy implementation and academic integrity monitoring. Despite these efforts, Currie et al. (2023) and Jiang and Huang (2022) pointed to the persisting gaps in longitudinal studies to track the institutional adaptation to curb the GenAI-driven integrity risks and limited empirical validation of the long-term effectiveness of current mitigation strategies. Hence, they suggested that universities should address these gaps by forming interdisciplinary GenAI ethics committees, conducting longitudinal studies on GenAI adaptation and refining their governance models so as to align the evolving GenAI technologies with academic integrity standards.

## 4.5.3 | AI-Inclusive Assessment Redesign

The use of GenAI tools in higher education has led to transformations in assessment design; however, the synthesised results (n = 37) revealed that institutions were struggling to balance GenAI innovation with academic integrity. Studies such as Almaghaslah  (2025), Evangelista  (2025) and Kofinas et al. (2025) indicated that GenAI-integrated assessments have been shown to reduce academic misconduct risks, but challenges persist in distinguishing GenAI-generated responses from student-authored work. Hence, Chan et  al.  (2025), Creighton et al. (2025), T. Liu (2023) and Liu et al. (2025) highlighted that educators are facing difficulties evaluating GenAIgenerated submissions due to inconsistencies in plagiarism detection algorithms, which produce false positives and negatives. Additionally, Fuad et  al.  (2024), Kodali et  al.  (2023), Leong and Zhang (2025) and Pudasaini et al. (2024) underscore the need for multi-layered verification models that account for GenAI-generated paraphrasing and automated responses.

Similarly, Donnelly and Kennelly (2024) and Khlaif et al. (2025) encouraged the institutions to refine GenAI-integrated assessment frameworks to ensure that GenAI plagiarism detection tools conform to grading mechanisms while preserving academic originality standards. Nevertheless, Al Harrasi and El Din (2024), Kortemeyer and Nöhl (2025) and Suhail et al. (2024) indicate insufficient research on faculty-student dynamics in GenAI-assisted grading, with limited empirical validation of GenAI-inclusive assessments. Furthermore, Prajapati et al. (2025) and Pudasaini et al. (2024) emphasised the need for improved GenAI detection models that can reliably differentiate AI-generated and human-authored responses.

## 5 | Discussion

This section discusses the balance between innovation and ethics in using GenAI tools in higher education, with a particular focus on the role of institutional guidelines in enhancing academic integrity. It discusses the synthesised findings from 213 studies published in English between 1 January 2021 and 31 May 2025 on the impact of educational support, selfregulation and institutional regulations on the ethical use of GenAI tools. Results indicated that academic support, including resource availability, training, and technical guidance, is central to effectively integrating GenAI tools in higher education. Additionally, the findings highlighted the importance of self-regulation in maintaining academic integrity, with adaptive learning and personalised feedback playing significant roles. Similarly, institutional regulations, including data privacy, bias, and fairness guidelines, are vital in promoting responsible use and preventing academic malpractice. Thus, this discussion is organised according to the order and scope of the three research questions.

## 5.1 | Factors Influencing the Integrity of GenAI Integration in Higher Education

The empirical evidence from 2021 to 2025 revealed that four main interdependent factors critically shape the integrity of integrating GenAI tools in higher education. These factors include technological infrastructure, institutional governance, faculty preparedness and student self-regulation. The findings emphasise that academic integrity is not merely contingent upon individual behaviour but emerges from systemic design and implementation. Therefore, institutional guidelines are fundamental in promoting academic integrity. It implies that institutions with clear, enforceable GenAI policies, well-articulated through cross-disciplinary committees and ethics review boards, demonstrated improved transparency and coherence in implementing GenAI tools (Alqahtani and Wafula 2025; Johnson and Zhang 2024). Conversely, universities without strong regulatory frameworks reported more interpretive inconsistencies, mainly in assessing student submissions generated with GenAI (Currie et  al.  2023; Henry and Oliver  2022). Indeed, the variations in policy maturity highlight the urgency for unified governance models that align with academic integrity norms while remaining agile to GenAI developments (Yang et al. 2024).

Additionally, faculty preparedness is another critical factor affecting the pedagogical legitimacy of using GenAI tools. The structured training in GenAI ethics and application increases educator confidence (Malik and Terzidis 2025), but disciplinary inequities continue. For instance, Al-Jaghoub et  al.  (2025), Streletska et al. (2024) and Tlais et al. (2025) found that STEM faculties benefited more from early integration and funding support. In contrast, in the humanities and social sciences, educators reported insufficient access to relevant tools or guidance.

Hence, this asymmetry compromised the equitable GenAI implementation and limited its transformative potential in non-STEM disciplines.

Furthermore, student self-regulation and GenAI literacy were underdeveloped across multiple academic contexts. The synthesised results suggested that students often misinterpret the GenAI outputs as inherently valid, resulting in uncritical reproduction of content and a breach of academic integrity (Nam and Bai 2023; Rasul et al. 2023, 2024). Particularly, institutions that embedded GenAI ethics modules into their curricula, such as through reflective assignments or scenario-based assessments, saw measurable improvements in the students' ethical reasoning and self-monitoring skills (Acosta-Enriquez et  al.  2024; Moorhouse et al. 2023).

Moreover, technological infrastructure was also found to be pivotal in the integration process, but it remains a limiting factor. Similarly, Gupta and Gupta (2024), Leong and Zhang (2025) and Smith and Lester (2024) indicated that although automated GenAI detection tools are increasingly being adopted, they frequently yield false positives or overlook nuanced GenAIgenerated content. Indeed, the learning institutions that used multi-layered verification by merging algorithmic detection and human judgement reported improved reliability in academic misconduct identification (Bellini et  al.  2024; Fuad et  al.  2024; Giray  2024). However, such solutions require sustained investment in adaptive learning analytics and faculty training to interpret algorithmic outputs ethically. Therefore, these findings suggest that a fragmentary approach to GenAI integration in the learning environment undermines academic integrity. Instead, institutions should pursue a well-coordinated GenAI governance framework that embeds GenAI literacy across stakeholders, disciplines and decision-making structures. Hence, a systems-based response to the factors that influence the integrity of integrating GenAI tools in education is critical for sustaining trust, fairness and intellectual rigour in GenAImediated education.

## 5.2 | Impact of GenAI Integration on Academic Integrity and Effective Institutional Mitigation

The integration of GenAI in higher education has presented both transformative opportunities and unprecedented academic integrity risks. According to Chan et  al.  (2025) and Kofinas et al. (2025), GenAI tools are enhancing educational accessibility and efficiency while simultaneously disrupting established norms of assessment reliability, academic authorship, and intellectual autonomy. Thus, Currie et al. (2023) and Katsantonis and Katsantonis (2024) advised that the institutions that adopted a reactive rather than proactive approach to GenAI integration reported higher instances of academic misconduct and confusion around ethical boundaries when using GenAI tools.

GenAI-assisted misconduct, though not always associated with intentional plagiarism, through overreliance on automated GenAI outputs, diminishes the students' engagement with evidence-based reasoning, source evaluation, and argument synthesis (Giray  2024; Leong and Zhang  2025; Zhang et al. 2025). They submit algorithmically generated content as their original work without evaluating its epistemic validity and ethical implications. Similarly, Qu and Wang (2025) observed that institutions that lacked structured GenAI literacy training reported higher incidences of citation errors, shallow paraphrasing, and misrepresentation of sources. These patterns suggest that academic integrity violations are mainly a result of epistemological naivety rather than wilful academic dishonesty.

Moreover, faculty engagement is a critical factor in mitigating breaches of GenAI-related academic integrity. Nevertheless, Evangelista (2025) and Murch et al. (2025) highlighted the inconsistent faculty preparedness, especially across non-STEM disciplines. Hence, in the absence of standardisation of the GenAI-integrated instructional frameworks, many educators revert to traditional assessment models, which are ill-suited to GenAI-mediated learning contexts (Symeou et  al.  2025). Bikanga Ada  (2024) and Jin et  al.  (2025) noted that the introduction of GenAI-inclusive coursework, like scaffolded assignments that require human-GenAI collaboration, has the potential to preserve academic rigour and leverage GenAI's potential to enhance academic skills development. However, disparities in faculty access to GenAI pedagogical development are still a critical bottleneck in higher learning institutions.

Furthermore, the automated GenAI detection tools are flawed and yet are widely deployed as mitigation strategies for academic integrity risks. Indeed, despite institutional enthusiasm, these detection tools are often characterised by poor interpretability and false positives, unduly affecting students (Klyshbekova and Abbott 2024; Zhao, Cox, and Chen 2025). For instance, Laflamme and Bruneault  (2025) revealed that algorithmic detectors misclassified 23% of human-authored essays and unreasonably penalised students who used uncommon rhetorical writing styles. Hence, Xu, Zhan, and Liang (2024) and Xu and Jumaat (2024) suggested that mitigation strategies should move toward hybrid verification models that combine contextual human judgement with algorithmic outputs.

Additionally, Alshamy et al. (2025) and Moorhouse et al. (2023) established that the institutions that adopted anticipatory models, such as including GenAI literacy in academic skills modules and framing GenAI-assisted assignments within ethical discussion, had lower rates of academic misconduct and stronger student learning engagement. Moreover, interdisciplinary faculty development programmes that include scenario-based teaching, GenAI ethics training, and critical evaluation approaches were more effective in supporting faculty confidence and instructional consistency (Driessens and Pischetola  2024; Teremetskyi et al. 2024). Indeed, Elbaz et al. (2024) concluded that the cross-sector collaborations between universities, technologists, and librarians were particularly impactful in creating adaptive governance frameworks that keep pace with evolving GenAI capabilities.

Therefore, the integration of GenAI tools in higher education reshapes academic integrity. Rather than depending exclusively on punitive investigation mechanisms, institutions should reframe the use of GenAI tools as a pedagogical opportunity to nurture epistemic agency, ethical literacy, and critical inquiry. The effective mitigation should be based on multidimensional strategies that include standardised GenAI literacy curricula, faculty professional development, and automatic detection protocols that are sensitive to disciplinary distinctions. Hence, sustaining academic integrity in GenAImediated environments requires moving beyond compliance toward a culture of informed and principled GenAI use in education.

## 5.3 | Educational Evidence-Based Model Practices for Ethical and Effective GenAI Integration

The ethical deployment of GenAI tools in higher education hinges not only on governance structures but also on the pedagogical sophistication of faculty training and the depth of student AI literacy. The evidence supports a systems-based approach. The one that embeds interdisciplinary collaboration, dynamic policy feedback, and tailored pedagogical design to safeguard academic integrity to maximise the effective use of GenAI tools.

## 5.3.1 | Institutional GenAI Governance Models

The synthesised results show that the institutions that implemented interdisciplinary GenAI Ethics Committees, with representation from technology, pedagogy and academic policy, demonstrated higher responsiveness to GenAI-related misconduct and stronger stakeholder confidence in policy legitimacy (Hegazy et al. 2024; Lentz 2024; Zlotnikova et al. 2025). However, without iterative policy evaluation, it proved insufficient. Indeed, static policies rapidly become obsolete as GenAI capabilities evolve fast, exposing the gaps in enforcing academic integrity. The model practices emphasise cyclical policy improvement, in which the feedback from GenAI implementation is used to revise the GenAI guidelines regularly and create adaptable governance structures attuned to empirical shifts.

## 5.3.2 | Faculty Development as an Ethical and Disciplinary Imperative

The most effective faculty training programs should incorporate GenAI tool proficiency and promote source verification, critical engagement with algorithmic bias, and discipline-specific ethics (Alderman et  al.  2025; Jain and Menon  2023; Sullivan et  al.  2023). Remarkably, faculty in STEM fields benefited from workshops on GenAI-augmented assessment accuracy. However, humanities and social sciences faculties required support in evaluating GenAI-generated claims and integrating misinformation screening into coursework (Jaidka et al. 2025; Nam and Bai  2023). Institutions that integrated GenAI ethics into faculty promotion and professional development standards reported more consistent ethical engagement across departments (Elom et  al.  2025; Farrell  2024; Mondal  2025). Indeed, generalised training models failed to account for contextual pedagogical challenges, reducing faculty agency and risking instructional inequity. Indeed, faculty collaborations emerged as model practices to facilitate knowledge exchange and promote disciplinary customisation. These academic and professional networks would help to democratise access to GenAI teaching strategies and help educators align localised teaching needs with global ethical standards.

## 5.3.3 | Student-Centric GenAI Literacy and Active Integrity Frameworks

The GenAI literacy efforts targeting students were most effective when structured as ongoing and inquiry-driven programmes rather than isolated workshops. Hence, the institutions that deployed interactive case-based modules, allowing students to compare GenAI- and human-authored content, fostered improvement in students' ability to identify misinformation and question algorithmic reliability (Elom et al. 2025; Farrell 2024; Mondal 2025). Moreover, introducing the longitudinal tracking mechanisms, such as the semester-based GenAI usage audits and reflective journals, allowed educators to detect changes in student reliance on GenAI content and tailor interventions accordingly. Indeed, Evangelista (2025) and Kofinas et al. (2025) suggested that reframing academic integrity training toward intellectual autonomy rather than surveillance and punishment encourages deeper ethical reasoning.

Additionally, GenAI-inclusive assessments requiring students to refine GenAI outputs or critique algorithmic decisions demonstrated the benefits of promoting critical thinking and minimising passive GenAI dependence (Rasul et al. 2023; Zhao, Cox, and Chen 2025). Integrating GenAI tools in teaching and learning demands more than procedural compliance. It requires institution-wide cultural and pedagogical adaptation. Therefore, the evidence-based best practices include establishing interdisciplinary ethics committees with iterative oversight, tailoring faculty training to technical and disciplinary needs, and designing student literacy programmes that foster epistemic agency. It implies that ethical GenAI use develops not through prohibition but through deliberate educational design that empowers students and educators to navigate GenAI responsibly, critically, and reflectively.

## 5.3.4 | Standardising GenAI Integrity Guidelines Across Educational Systems

The perceived policy fragmentation remains a significant vulnerability in GenAI governance. For example, universities in hightech regions tend to offer comprehensive GenAI ethics modules, while under-resourced institutions struggle to implement basic GenAI literacy instruction (Gabriel  2024; Miltenoff  2024; van Rensburg and van der Westhuizen  2024). Indeed, unreliable plagiarism detection further compounds this gap and unfairly evaluates GenAI-generated outputs across institutions (Leong and Zhang 2025).

Therefore, to address these concerns, this literature review advocates for GenAI integrity frameworks that balance uniform benchmarks with disciplinary flexibility. These frameworks should outline essential competencies in GenAI use, faculty engagement, and academic assessment and accommodate fieldspecific norms, such as interpretive analysis in the humanities against algorithmic verification in STEM (Giray  2024; Wang, Huai, et al. 2024). Similarly, the policy examples from GenAIintensive regions suggest that adaptive governance models driven by empirical review cycles enable institutions to update ethics protocols in response to technological advancements and pedagogical needs (Kangwa et al. 2025). Thus, policy coherence promotes transparency and trust among learners and educators, the essential pillars for responsible GenAI integration.

## 5.3.5 | Ethical Alignment in GenAI Model Design

The ethical and pedagogical relevance of GenAI tools is shaped by their architecture. Hence, the best practices underscore the importance of developer-institution collaboration to ensure that GenAI tools are built with features that align with academic integrity standards (Agarwal and Sivaraman  2024; Corbeil and Corbeil  2025; Valdivieso and González  2025). For example, the models should include built-in bias alerts, citation auditing, and discipline-specific logic flags to support critical engagement in pedagogical settings (X. Liu 2025; Plata et al. 2023). If these features are not integrated, they risk producing GenAI tools that reinforce dominant epistemologies while marginalising alternative ways of knowing, a serious concern for inclusivity in the design (A. Stone 2023). Hence, GenAI developers must transition from generalised usability goals to context-sensitive ethical designs tailored to educational environments through participatory development with stakeholders in teaching, learning, and policy domains.

Therefore, effective GenAI adoption is not reducible to institutional innovation alone. Rather, it requires a multilevel ecosystem of evidence-informed strategies. It entails standardising the ethical frameworks to ensure equitable governance and reengineering GenAI tools, embedding pedagogical integrity at their core. Indeed, without investment in policy and design, the potential of GenAI to advance educational quality will remain unevenly distributed and ethically unstable.

Figure 10 illustrates that GenAI's ethical and effective use in education requires integrating the contributions of multiple stakeholders, including policymakers, institutional leaders, faculty, students and GenAI developers. Integrating GenAI tools in education without cross-sector collaboration risks reinforcing systemic inequities, academic integrity concerns and inadequate pedagogical alignment. Hence, institutions should refine GenAI literacy initiatives, policymakers should standardise GenAI ethics policies and technology developers should prioritise ethical model design to ensure that GenAI tools support responsible learning across educational environments.

## 5.4 | Limitations and Recommendations for Future Research

This systematic review adhered to the PRISMA framework and applied a rigorously defined set of inclusion and exclusion criteria (see Table 2), which included 213 peer-reviewed articles for analysis. Indeed, the study exclusively sampled peer-reviewed, full-text research articles indexed in five major scholarly databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ERIC and PsycINFO). Although this approach ensured the quality and relevance of the included studies, it may have excluded high-quality, non-English publications or grey literature, which could have limited the multilingual diversity of perspectives on GenAI integration in higher education. This potential language and indexing bias may particularly underrepresent insights from low- and middleincome countries, where publication visibility in indexed databases is more constrained.

Additionally, the temporal restriction to articles published between January 1, 2021, and May 31, 2025 ensured focus, although this may have excluded recent studies on rapidly evolving GenAI technologies published post-inclusion period. The rapid GenAI development and deployment in higher educational contexts implies that some insights captured in the review's earlier years may no longer reflect current technological capabilities, ethical concerns or pedagogical practices. Therefore, these limitations underscore the need for future systematic reviews to extend their scope by incorporating multilingual and non-indexed databases, but with a strengthened quality appraisal process. Future research may apply mixed-methods synthesis strategies and adopt adaptive inclusion criteria that account for the changeability of GenAI technologies in education. This may help to triangulate current findings and improve their applicability in diverse global higher education settings.

## 6 | Conclusion

This study critically investigated the intersection of academic integrity and the integration of generative artificial intelligence in higher education. It was a synthesis of research evidence from 213 peer-reviewed results that were published between 2021 and 2025. The systematic literature review was framed by the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB), and Self-regulated learning theory to address the three guiding research questions concerning the factors influencing GenAI integration (RQ1), its impact on academic integrity (RQ2), and evidence-based practices for its ethical and pedagogical use (RQ3).

The findings underline that academic support mechanisms, including structured training and institutional resource availability, play a pivotal role in shaping students' acceptance and ethical use of GenAI tools (Lentz 2024; Nguyen 2025; Tang et al. 2024). In alignment with TAM, the perceived usefulness and ease of use of GenAI were found to be positively associated with students' willingness to employ these tools to develop academic skills such as critical thinking, argumentation and research synthesis (Silva 2015). However, unequal access to support systems across institutions promoted disparities in student engagement and ethical awareness, particularly in under-resourced education settings (Fitzsimons  2024; Naidu and Sevnarayan  2023). Additionally, self-regulation emerged as another critical determinant of ethical GenAI engagement. Students with strong metacognitive skills and a high degree of behavioural control, the main constructs of TPB, were more likely to scrutinise GenAI-generated outputs, maintain academic honesty, and align their behaviours with high academic expectations (Afzaal et al. 2024; Al-Emran et al. 2025). These findings affirmed the need to integrate self-regulation development into GenAI literacy programs to foster intellectual autonomy and ethical resilience among students.

Moreover, institutional governance was found to significantly affect the effective use of the GenAI tools. The results show that institutions with clear, enforceable GenAI ethics guidelines and transparent communication channels were more successful in handling academic misconduct and promoting responsible innovation (Alexander et al. 2023; R. T. Williams 2023b). However, fragmented policy environments and inconsistent assessment protocols were identified with inequitable academic integrity enforcement and increased misuse of GenAI tools (Currie 2023; Kofinas et  al.  2025). Hence, standardising GenAI governance frameworks at national and international levels was recognised as an urgent priority (A. Stone 2023; Tsai 2025; Yang et al. 2024). Furthermore, the review acknowledged several model practices that support responsible GenAI integration in higher education contexts. These included interdisciplinary GenAI ethics committees, discipline-specific faculty professional development initiatives, interactive GenAI literacy modules, and algorithmically transparent GenAI platforms that are co-developed by educators and developers (Perkins, Furze, et al. 2024; Plata et al. 2023). These strategies not only reinforce pedagogical effectiveness but also integrate academic integrity into the teaching and learning processes. Therefore, the ethical and effective use of GenAI in higher education cannot be sustained through isolated interventions. Instead, it requires a coherent ecosystem of academic support, self-regulated student engagement and adaptive institutional governance that is guided by evidence and grounded in inclusive pedagogical values. The proposed integrated TAM-TPB framework provides a useful conceptual scaffold for this endeavour, particularly for institutions in developing regions.
