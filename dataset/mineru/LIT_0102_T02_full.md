# Afective counterpublics under constraint: emotion, platform governance, and AI ethics discourse on Chinese social media

Bei Zhang<sup>1</sup>

Received: 9 April 2025 / Accepted: 13 June 2025 / Published online: 26 June 2025

## Abstract

This study examines how emotional expressions shape public discourse on AI ethics within China’s algorithmically curated and policy-regulated digital environment. Analyzing 20,037 Weibo posts from August 2023 to January 2025, we compare institutional and ordinary users’ engagement during key regulatory shifts and the launch of the domestic model DeepSeek R1. Using LDA topic modeling and sentiment analysis, we find that institutional actors promote compliance-focused narratives, while ordinary users mobilize eight distinct emotions—particularly satire, outrage, and anxiety—identified through an extended NRC lexicon, to contest issues such as misinformation, plagiarism, and creative authorship. Notably, posts invoking existential or justice-oriented concerns receive up to 7.3 times more engagement than policy-aligned content, with Gold V accounts averaging 117.95 likes per post compared to 16.09 for Blue V accounts. The viral #AICheating campaign, which garnered 2.3 billion views, illustrates how afective discourse can pressure policy adjustments, contributing to national academic integrity reforms in 2024. We propose the concept of afective counterpublics under constraint—a theoretical framework that extends Fraser’s notion of subaltern counterpublics by integrating algorithmic resistance—to explain how emotionally driven grassroots mobilization operates within authoritarian platform governance. This reframes emotional expression as a form of embodied public reasoning capable of recalibrating policy attention under algorithmic suppression.

Keywords Generative AI · Policy-driven publicness · Afective counterpublics · Algorithmic governance · Platform visibility · Chinese social media · AI ethics

## 1 Introduction

The global rise of generative AI technologies has produced headline-grabbing controversies—from deepfake videos of public figures such as Lionel Messi to AI-generated ghostwritten essays that triggered academic integrity scandals. These cases have intensified public anxiety over misinformation, manipulation, and the erosion of authorship and trust. While governments around the world have begun implementing regulatory frameworks, these eforts often lag behind the pace of technological innovation.

More critically, public discourse on AI ethics increasingly unfolds in algorithmically curated spaces, where visibility is shaped not only by formal policy but also by platform architecture. This shift raises critical questions about public deliberation and participation: in environments governed by opaque algorithms and selective content moderation, how can ethical concerns voiced by the public gain visibility and influence policy directions?

China presents a compelling case for exploring this question. As both a global leader in AI development and a pioneer of state-mediated platform governance, China has institutionalized a system in which policy directives, content moderation, and algorithmic sorting converge. Among Chinese digital platforms, Weibo occupies a unique position as a semi-open, semi-regulated public sphere—an environment where oficial narratives prevail but are actively negotiated. Unlike closed messaging apps or short-form video platforms, Weibo supports both institutional policy communication and emotionally charged grassroots debates, making it an ideal site to investigate how AI ethics discourse is shaped, constrained, and sometimes resisted under authoritarian conditions.

Existing scholarship has begun to theorize the intersection between state control and algorithmic visibility. Han (2022), for example, proposes the notion of policy-driven publicness to describe how Chinese platforms algorithmically amplify state agendas while filtering dissent. While this framework ofers valuable insight into the role of platform design in sustaining state power, it overlooks two important dynamics. First, it underestimates the role of afective publics—ordinary users who engage with sociopolitical issues by mobilizing fear, satire, outrage, or anxiety (Papacharissi 2015). Second, it lacks empirical grounding in how such emotionally charged counter-narratives operate within algorithmically curated and politically regulated environments. As generative AI technologies become more central to governance and everyday life, understanding both top-down governance and bottom-up resistance—especially in afective, informal, or algorithmically suppressed forms—becomes increasingly urgent.

This study addresses these gaps by analyzing 20,037 Weibo posts from August 2023 to January 2025 using Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic modeling and sentiment analysis. We focus on two actor groups: institutional accounts (Blue V) that promote compliance-oriented narratives (e.g., data security, legal governance), and ordinary users who voice emotional critiques around issues such as deepfakes, plagiarism, and creative autonomy. Our findings reveal that grassroots resistance operates as afective counterpublics under constraint—a concept extending Fraser’s (1990) theory of subaltern counterpublics. Unlike Habermas’s (1989) rational-critical public sphere, which presumes open deliberation, afective counterpublics rely on emotional mobilization to contest algorithmic hierarchies of visibility within curated and censored spaces.

These discursive tactics strategically exploit platform affordances—such as viral hashtags and cross-platform sharing—to circumvent suppression and articulate dissent. Despite limited algorithmic visibility, such tactics can exert bottom-up pressure on institutional agendas, as illustrated by the viral #AICheating campaign, which mobilized widespread criticism and contributed to the 2024 revision of national academic integrity guidelines.

By theorizing afective counterpublics in constrained digital spaces, this study contributes to three scholarly conversations: (1) extending agenda-setting theory to authoritarian contexts, (2) challenging liberal assumptions of algorithmic publicness by revealing state–platform entanglements, and (3) informing global AI governance with empirical insights from China’s contested platform ecosystem.

## 2 Literature review

## 2.1  Algorithmic visibility and the public agenda

Algorithmic publicness refers to the ways in which platforms construct “engineered publicness” through code-based architectures that embed political and commercial logics into content visibility (Ananny and Crawford 2018). In authoritarian contexts, this dynamic evolves into what Han (2022) terms policy-driven publicness, whereby algorithmic infrastructures not only amplify state agendas but also suppress dissenting expressions.

Traditional agenda-setting theory, first articulated by McCombs and Shaw (1972), posits that media shape public discourse by determining which issues are prioritized. In the digital age, however, this function is increasingly mediated by algorithms that sort, rank, and promote content. Unlike legacy mass media, social media platforms do not merely transmit messages—they actively curate visibility through personalized recommendation systems (Gillespie 2018; Napoli 2019).

This logic of algorithmic visibility, which structures what users see and engage with, has led scholars to reconceptualize publicness as a designed and regulated phenomenon. Ananny and Crawford (2018) argue that visibility is neither neutral nor spontaneous, but the outcome of deliberate “design choices” that encode political, commercial, and ideological priorities into algorithmic systems. This perspective is especially salient when analyzing algorithmic mediation within state-aligned digital infrastructures, where visibility functions simultaneously as a technical mechanism and a tool of governance.

In authoritarian settings such as China, algorithmic amplification is not merely a product of platform logic—it is deeply entangled with state power. As Trevisan et al. (2020) observe, algorithmic visibility constitutes a “new digital divide”, one that systematically privileges institutional narratives while marginalizing non-institutional voices. While much scholarly attention has been paid to how algorithms shape public discourse, less is known about how emotional or afective content interacts with these systems, particularly under conditions of state intervention and political constraint.

## 2.2 Policy‑driven publicness under digital authoritarianism

Han (2022) introduced the concept of policy-driven publicness to describe a governance model in which state institutions and digital platforms co-produce the boundaries of online discourse. In this configuration, platforms like Weibo selectively amplify state-aligned narratives—such as “data security” or “technological innovation”—while algorithmically suppressing alternative perspectives that may challenge regulatory legitimacy. Han’s model ofers a critical corrective to Western assumptions of algorithmic publicness as a space of pluralism, instead highlighting the institutional embeddedness of visibility in authoritarian regimes.

However, despite its conceptual value, the theory of policy-driven publicness remains underdeveloped in several areas. First, it conceptualizes the public as largely passive, focusing on top-down control without suficiently engaging with user agency, particularly forms of grassroots resistance. Second, it does not account for the role of emotion—a key driver of visibility on social media—in shaping or disrupting platform dynamics. In practice, emotionally charged content such as satire, outrage, or anxiety often finds ways to circulate despite algorithmic and political constraints.

This study seeks to extend Han’s model by introducing the concept of afective counterpublics under constraint. These publics emerge not through rational deliberation but through emotional mobilization, and their visibility is fragile, often relying on afective triggers and networked amplification. In doing so, we emphasize that algorithmic publicness in China is not a static space of control, but a negotiated arena, where state-aligned agendas and emotionally mediated resistance co-exist in dynamic tension.

## 2.3 Afective counterpublics and platform resistance

Building on Papacharissi’s (2015) notion of afective publics, we argue that emotional discourse plays a crucial role in the negotiation of AI ethics on platforms like Weibo. Afective publics do not rely on institutional legitimacy or technical expertise but operate through emotional resonance, using fear, humor, and moral outrage to construct visibility and solidarity. In China’s digital context, emotionally driven campaigns—such as hashtag activism around deepfakes or academic ghostwriting—often create temporal moments of counterpower that disrupt platform hierarchies.

Jiang and Fu (2020) have documented how Chinese users engage in hashtag resistance, using emotionally laden discourse to challenge platform censorship and state narratives. While their study focuses on political activism, similar mechanisms operate in the AI ethics domain, where expressions of anxiety over generative content, creative autonomy, or algorithmic injustice generate afective engagement. Our research builds on this foundation by showing how these affective narratives—despite being algorithmically deranked or filtered—still exert bottom-up pressure on institutional agenda-setting, often prompting reactive policy adjustments.

These afective dynamics often trigger reactive policy adjustments by both state and platform actors. Such adjustments may include the issuance of new administrative guidelines, temporary shifts in moderation thresholds, or recalibration of visibility algorithms in response to viral public sentiment. For instance, emotionally charged discussions around AI-generated plagiarism have led to institutional responses such as the 2024 revision of academic integrity guidelines. Platforms, in turn, may selectively amplify or downrank certain topics depending on their alignment with dominant narratives. These adjustments represent tactical responses that balance afective feedback with regime stability.

While afective counterpublics often challenge dominant narratives through emotional mobilization, it is important to recognize that not all emotional expressions function as resistance. In certain contexts, emotions such as outrage or anxiety may reinforce nationalist discourses or support state-led regulation. For example, some users express “patriotic anxiety” in favor of tighter AI governance, not as a form of dissent but as an appeal for stronger state intervention. This duality highlights the ambivalent role of afect in digitally mediated publicness under authoritarian governance.

## 2.4 Comparative perspectives on platform governance

While this study is situated within China’s digital authoritarian context, it speaks to broader debates on platform governance. Poell et al. (2021)propose a platform governance trinity—comprising platform architecture, institutional rules, and data flows—that structure user behavior and policy responses. In democratic societies, this trinity often reflects tensions between commercial interests and civic participation; in China, the balance shifts toward state control, with platforms aligning closely with regulatory agendas.

By comparing China’s governance model with those of the U.S. and EU, we highlight the structural specificity of Chinese platforms: here, algorithmic systems not only promote user engagement but also enforce political compliance. This creates a unique discursive environment where policy enforcement and afective resistance unfold simultaneously. Our findings contribute to this comparative literature by demonstrating how emotional publics function under conditions of constrained visibility—thereby ofering new insight into how public discourse on AI ethics is negotiated across governance regimes.

## 3  Methodology

This study employs a computational social science approach (Lazer et al. 2009) to analyze AI ethics discussions on Weibo. By combining LDA topic modeling and sentiment analysis, we investigate how institutional users (Blue V accounts) and ordinary users frame generative AI ethics.

## 3.1 Data collection

Weibo was selected as the case study platform due to its central role in policy communication and public debate in China (Zhao 2020). Using a Python-based web scraper, we collected 20,037 posts containing keywords such as “generative AI” (生成式人工智能), “ChatGPT”, “Sora”, and “DeepSeek”.

The dataset covers the period from August 15, 2023, to January 30, 2025, capturing two key moments: (1) the implementation of the Interim Measures for the Administration of Generative AI Services (efective August 15, 2023), which prompted a surge in institutional messaging; and (2) the release of DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025), a domestic AI model that sparked intensified public discussions on intellectual property and creative autonomy.

Keywords were selected based on their relevance to the research topic and validated through preliminary searches on Weibo. During the data collection period, terms such as “ChatGPT” and “generative AI” were widely used by both institutional and ordinary users and were not subject to censorship. We also tested alternative or euphemistic expressions (e.g., “AI assistant”, “writing tool”), but found they appeared infrequently and did not improve data coverage. Therefore, we adopted a keyword strategy focused on direct and commonly used terms to ensure both relevance and comprehensiveness.

The dataset was categorized by user type based on Weibo’s verification system:

(1) Institutional users (Blue V): Government agencies, state-owned enterprises, and oficial media accounts verified by Weibo to disseminate policy-aligned content.

(2) Ordinary users:

Yellow V: Individual influencers (e.g., academics, journalists) with platform-verified expertise.

Red V: Celebrities and public figures.

Gold V: Commercial entities or industry experts.

Non-V users: Unverified accounts representing general public participation.

This classification aligns with Weibo’s oficial certification rules and allows analysis of how user身份 (institutional vs. grassroots) shapes AI ethics discourse.

Eight emotions were identified across three valence types: negative (satire, outrage, fear, anxiety), neutral (confusion), and positive (hope, empathy, optimism). ## 4.2.1 Original theme extraction and validation

To explore how institutional actors and ordinary users construct ethical discourse around generative AI, we conducted separate Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic modeling for the two groups. The number of topics was determined based on the highest topic coherence scores, yielding K = 8 for institutional users (U Mass = 0.62) and K = 10 for ordinary users (U Mass = 0.58), from which 9 topics were retained after consolidating semantically overlapping themes. The validation process followed a three-step protocol: (1) removing semantic redundancy using a cosine similarity threshold of 0.7; (2) manually labeling 500 sampled posts per group by two coders following Papacharissi’s (2015) afective discourse framework (inter-coder reliability κ = 0.81); and (3) applying a minimum theme prevalence threshold of 5%, with exceptions made for distinct ethical dimensions (e.g., Resurrection Ethics).

Several key observations emerge. First, institutional discourse overwhelmingly centers on policy compliance, with 83% of content thematically aligned to regulatory and promotional objectives, compared to just 12% of such content in ordinary user discourse. Second, ordinary users foreground rights protection, with 76% of themes addressing authorship, authenticity, and algorithmic risk—often articulated through strong emotional expressions such as outrage, anxiety, and satire. Third, this asymmetry reflects Han’s (2022) notion of policy-driven publicness, where institutional actors adopt emotionally neutral “[policy narrative]” frames, while grassroots users strategically mobilize afect to challenge algorithmic visibility and assert discursive agency.

## 4.2.2 Higher‑order theme synthesis

Building on the thematic landscape revealed in Sect. 4.2.1, we synthesized the original themes into conceptually coherent higher-order categories to reveal the deeper narrative logic and oppositional discursive structures between institutional and ordinary users.

Thematic consolidation followed three criteria: (1) semantic cohesion, measured by Jaccard similarity scores above 0.6 across keyword distributions (e.g., Data Security and Cyber Governance = 0.73); (2) afective consistency, whereby themes exhibited similar emotional valence (e.g., anxiety, vigilance); and (3) discursive function alignment, indicating convergence in either reinforcing or resisting policy-driven publicness (Han 2022).

Based on these criteria, we identified three composite clusters. For institutional users, the Policy Compliance cluster combines Industrial Policy, Data Security, and Cyber Governance—all advancing techno-legal control and aligning with state-endorsed narratives. These themes share a neutral afective tone and uniformly employ regulatory discourse. For ordinary users, the Cultural Authorship cluster merges Creative Ownership and Artistic Creation, reflecting public resistance to AI’s encroachment on authorship and cultural authenticity. The Risk & Accountability cluster includes Info Authenticity, Tech Responsibility, and Misinformation Bypass, which collectively articulate grassroots anxiety over algorithmic deception, data misuse, and censorship.

Two themes were retained as unclustered due to their distinct discursive orientations. Academic Integrity critiques AI’s impact on education systems and institutional ethics, while Cultural Satire employs parody to mock governance absurdities and circumvent censorship. Both lack strong semantic or emotional overlap with other themes, thus were not merged.

Finally, Resurrection Ethics was treated as a cross-cutting theme. It appeared in both institutional and ordinary user discourses, with divergent narrative framings—technocratic caution in institutional posts vs. moral anxiety in grassroots discourse. To preserve thematic clarity, it was assigned a unified definition and coded across both groups.

This refined framework ensures that all higher-order themes meet the structural expectation of comprising multiple original themes. It also increases analytical coherence by reducing redundancy and distinguishing themes with unique rhetorical or afective profiles. Table 5 presents the revised thematic structure.

## 4.2.3  Thematic divergence analysis

Building on the structural themes outlined above, we identify three core mechanisms that drive the divergence between institutional and ordinary user discourses on generative AI.

First, their discursive goals diverge fundamentally: institutional users predominantly pursue legitimation, as 83% of their themes focus on compliance, policy advocacy, or legal alignment (e.g., “Platforms must register AI tools”). In contrast, ordinary users engage in resistance, with 76% of their themes centered on cultural threat, authorship loss, or platform irresponsibility. For example, 89% of Creative Ownership posts express anger or satire (e.g., “AI stole my novel!”), while the institutional parallel IP Protection is devoid of emotional markers.

Second, the algorithmic-visibility feedback loop reinforces this divide. Institutional content enjoys disproportionate exposure—averaging a visibility score of 8.7 and a 92% hashtag survival rate. In contrast, ordinary users face frequent content suppression: the hashtag #AICheating accumulated 240 M views but was blocked from trending within four hours; #VoiceTheftScandal was downranked after 12 K reposts due to “sensitive content” tags.

## 4.4.2 Factors underpinning topic fragmentation

Despite limited intersections, some thematic convergence can be observed in areas like Education & Integrity, where public pressure—such as viral hashtags related to AI ghostwriting—prompted institutional responses. However, the overall fragmentation in discourse can be attributed to three structural factors:

First, the scope and focus of ethical concerns difer substantially. Institutional users prioritize macro-level issues such as governance frameworks and compliance, while ordinary users emphasize micro-level concerns related to personal rights, authorship, and social justice. This divide is reinforced by their respective communication goals—stateled consensus versus grassroots critique.

Second, discursive styles and emotional registers difer. Institutional users tend to adopt formal, policy-oriented language (“ethical compliance”, “cybersecurity enforcement”), whereas ordinary users engage through experiential, afective rhetoric (“AI is stealing my creativity”). These contrasting expressions result in parallel but rarely intersecting conversations, even when addressing similar issues such as intellectual property.

Third, platform governance and algorithmic visibility mediate exposure and amplification. During the policy rollout phase, institutional content on data security and intellectual property benefited from platform promotion, while emotionally charged grassroots critiques were often downranked. In contrast, the release of DeepSeek-R1 catalyzed a viral surge in public engagement with sci-fi creation, prompting institutions to respond rhetorically but avoiding deeper structural questions around AI authorship.

In sum, the evolution of generative AI ethics discourse reflects a bifurcated dynamic: while institutional users remain anchored in state-sanctioned agendas, ordinary users continue to drive emotional and participatory discourse. The Sankey diagram illustrates how this divergence manifests thematically and temporally, revealing the interplay between algorithmic governance and afective resistance in shaping China’s digital public sphere.

## 5 Discussion

## 5.1 Theoretical implications: algorithmic publicness and afective counterpower in authoritarian contexts

## 5.1.1 From top‑down visibility to emotionally diferentiated counterpublics

Conventional agenda-setting models posit that institutional actors dominate public discourse through top-down control of issue salience. In authoritarian digital environments, this logic is extended through algorithmic infrastructures that amplify policy-aligned narratives while deprioritizing dissent. However, our sentiment analysis complicates this view by revealing that emotionally diferentiated publics—notably negative afect around issues such as creative ownership (89%) and information authenticity (76%)—emerge as strategic counterforces to algorithmic silence.

Rather than being uniformly emotional or irrational, ordinary users exhibit selective negativity, often targeting institutional blind spots where accountability is lowest. Hashtags like #AIStoleMyIdeas and #AICheating represent more than reactive outbursts; they exemplify afective counterpublics—user-driven formations that mobilize emotion as a mode of civic intervention. These publics may not overturn dominant discourse, but they succeed in disrupting visibility hierarchies, forcing institutional responses such as academic integrity reforms, even if symbolically.

This reframes emotional expression not as irrational noise but as embodied public reasoning (Papacharissi 2015), functioning under constraint to recalibrate the boundaries of sayable discourse.

## 5.1.2 Negotiated publicness and policy responsiveness under algorithmic constraint

Our findings support a shift from binary models of “control versus resistance” toward a more nuanced conceptualization of negotiated governance in platformed authoritarianism. Emotional content—particularly negatively valenced yet semantically coherent posts—operates within a logic of algorithmic constraint: visible enough to circulate within counterpublic spaces, but rarely algorithmically amplified to trend.

This condition produces what we term a controllable controversy: topics like AI plagiarism or misinformation are allowed limited discursive space, providing symbolic responsiveness while maintaining structural suppression. For instance, while Article 12 of the Interim Measures mandated AI labeling, it failed to address deeper platform-level opacity, reinforcing public skepticism and sustaining negative sentiment over time.

Our findings extend Han’s (2022) notion of policy-driven publicness by demonstrating that emotion functions as both a bottom-up pressure mechanism and an institutional risk signal. Specifically, while positive sentiment is clustered around speculative domains (e.g., artistic creation), negative sentiment is concentrated in domains of normative contention—revealing where symbolic policy fails to address structural asymmetries.

In sum, afect under constraint does not merely reflect public mood; it performs governance signaling. It identifies algorithmic and institutional blind spots, mobilizes symbolic critique, and occasionally catalyzes policy correction—however, partial. Thus, we position afective publics as algorithmically legible yet structurally constrained political actors within an evolving ecosystem of negotiated publicness.

## 5.2 Policy implications: designing emotion‑aware AI ethics communication

## 5.2.1 From policy promotion to emotion‑anchored narratives

This study reveals clear afective variations across diferent AI ethics themes (see Fig. 1). Negative emotions—such as outrage (observed in 89% of posts on creative ownership) and anxiety (76% on information authenticity)—are concentrated in topics involving personal rights and social trust. Conversely, positive sentiment, particularly hope and curiosity, clusters around artistic and speculative themes, such as AI-assisted creation (68%).

These findings suggest that institutions should align their communication strategies with these emotional contours. For high-conflict topics, adopting victim-centered narratives— for example, “Digital artists speak out: How AI plagiarized my style”—can activate empathy and reinforce legitimacy. For more positive topics, forward-looking storytelling, such as “AI helped me overcome a creative block,” can foster optimism and participatory imagination. Such afective anchoring transforms policy messaging into emotionally resonant public discourse.

## 5.2.2 Emotion translators: mobilizing hybrid‑afect influencers

Verified influencers (e.g., Gold V accounts) play a pivotal role in transmediating policy into emotionally engaging formats. For instance, @PopScienceStar's viral post blending satire with existential anxiety gained 12,304 likes (as documented in Sect. 4.1, Table 1)., demonstrating the power of hybrid afect in public engagement.

To enhance communication eficacy, authorities should co-produce content with such actors. Science communicators, for instance, can explain regulatory clauses through short-form humor videos—e.g., “Can you spot the deepfake? When Messi sells you crypto.” Additionally, themes like AI’s impact on labor can be framed through an anxiety–hope duality: “Is your job at risk? Here’s how to pivot.” These emotion-driven strategies not only amplify reach but also build relational trust in otherwise opaque governance narratives.

## 5.2.3 Feedback channels for emotion migration and controversy management

Emotional discourse often spills over into alternative platforms when suppressed on mainstream ones (see Table 6). For example, after the hashtag #AICheating was suppressed on Weibo, users migrated to Bilibili and Zhihu, where related content gained millions of views and mobilized collective petitions. This cross-platform migration reveals the need for institutional feedback mechanisms that are both afect-aware and structurally responsive.

We recommend the implementation of dual-mode feedback channels. On domestic platforms like Weibo, policy response portals should allow users to submit emotiontagged inputs—selecting from predefined emotions (see Table 2) alongside anonymized textual claims (e.g., “ AI copied my design, no platform accountability!”). These submissions can be algorithmically clustered by emotion–theme patterns to inform policy prioritization.

Simultaneously, regulators should monitor emotionally charged spillovers on platforms such as Bilibili, Zhihu, and Douyin. Proactively addressing these alternative discursive arenas can help manage “controllable controversies” and preempt the escalation of unmanaged public grievances.

## 5.3 Limitations and future research

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, the sentiment classification was based on SnowNLP, a lexicon-based model calibrated through manual validation. While we ensured inter-coder reliability and provided clear thresholds, the model may still underperform in detecting nuanced or sarcastic expressions in Chinese social media, particularly under evolving platform norms. Future work could apply fine-tuned large language models (LLMs) or context-aware deep learning classifiers for enhanced accuracy.

Second, our theme extraction relies on unsupervised topic modeling (LDA) and post hoc manual refinement. Although coherence scores guided model selection and semantic overlap was minimized, LDA's bag-of-words limitation may affect topic distinctiveness. Structural Topic Modeling (STM) or transformer-based clustering could provide greater interpretability.

Third, while the study integrates afective patterns with governance dynamics, it is limited to textual data from Weibo. Excluding multimodal content (e.g., images, videos) and other platforms (e.g., Douyin, Zhihu) constrains the generalizability of afective publics. Cross-platform and multimodal analyses are needed to capture the full scope of emotion-driven discourse.

Finally, this study focuses on ethically themed discussions about generative AI. Broader applications of afective counterpublics—such as in environmental activism or public health crises—could further refine the theoretical model under diverse authoritarian settings.

## 6  Conclusion

This study demonstrates how policy-driven publicness and algorithmic curation jointly shape the discourse of AI ethics on Chinese social media. Drawing on over 20,000 Weibo posts across two regulatory milestones, we show that institutional actors largely reinforce complianceoriented narratives, while ordinary users challenge these framings through emotionally saturated discourse around misinformation, authorship, and human well-being. Emotion—especially negative emotion—emerges not merely as a rhetorical device but as a grassroots mode of contestation within algorithmically managed publics.

## 6.1  Emotional agency in evolving AI discourse

Even under constrained digital governance, emotional agency persists and evolves in patterned ways. As shown in Fig. 1, negative afect—particularly outrage, anxiety, and satire—clusters around themes with immediate ethical implications such as creative ownership (89% negative sentiment) and information authenticity (76%). In contrast, hope and optimism are concentrated in more speculative domains such as artistic creation. These emotion-theme pairings reveal that user emotions are not arbitrary but issue-specific and temporally dynamic. Emotional discourse thus becomes a central modality through which publics sense, interpret, and act upon emerging AI technologies.

## 6.2 Theoretical contributions

This research contributes to ongoing debates in AI ethics and digital governance in three ways:

It extends agenda-setting theory to authoritarian contexts by illustrating how afectively mobilized counterpublics shape visibility hierarchies, despite algorithmic demotion.

It expands the concept of policy-driven publicness by showing that emotional resistance—particularly satire and anxiety—persists under platform constraints and can influence institutional discourse indirectly.

Methodologically, it introduces a structured approach to emotion-theme-user mapping using lexicon-based sentiment classification and visual analytics (e.g., radar-style emotion mapping), ofering a scalable model for analyzing contentious publics in platform environments.

## 6.3  Toward emotionally responsive governance

Our findings point to practical strategies for making AI ethics governance more emotionally responsive and socially grounded. First, institutions should tailor narratives to specific emotional patterns: victim-centered storytelling can address outrage over plagiarism, while future-oriented framing can build hope around creative AI. Second, verified influencers—especially those blending satire with expert insight—can function as afect translators to bridge policy and public sentiment. Finally, feedback systems should allow emotion-tagged input and monitor cross-platform migration of emotional discourse. Rather than suppressing dissent, such measures can help institutions detect blind spots, preempt backlash, and craft more legitimate responses to the afective realities of AI governance.
