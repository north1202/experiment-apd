# ETHICS, AI, AND IRRESISTIBLE TEMPTATIONS

Kathy Hytten

Department of Educational Leadership and Cultural Foundations University of North Carolina Greensboro

Abstract. In this essay, I explore the ethical complexities of using generative AI in academic writing. Drawing on personal experience, I reflect on the shifting terrain of scholarly labor, authorship, originality, and transparency in a moment when AI can produce fluent—and even eloquent—academic prose. Rather than offering prescriptions, or strategies to resolve the ethical tensions, I reflect on the moral ambiguity of AI-assisted scholarship, the inadequacy of current disclosure norms, and the temptation to remain silent in the face of evolving ethical expectations. I argue for ongoing reflection while navigating this new intellectual landscape and exploring the elusive possibility of meaningful policies.

Key Words. ethics; generative AI; inquiry; academic labor

Working with my long-time writing partner, we recently completed a solid draft of a scholarly essay in probably about one-quarter the time it usually takes us. The process seemed so much faster and more efficient and rarely left me with long nights of existential dread about whether I actually had anything to say that hasn’t already been said, whether anyone would even read this essay in the age of information overload, or whether we were getting the research “right.” What accounted for this shift? My ethically conflicted foray into the world of generative AI. I say ethically conflicted because most of my limited experiences with AI up until this point had left me feeling that it enabled undetectable “cheating” on assignments, more than it helped with critical thinking and writing. Realizing that I really needed to understand AI deeply if I wanted to be a good teacher for the current generation of students, I entered the Pandora’s Box of the paid version of ChatGPT.

Pandora’s Box is an apt metaphor. Now that I have seen the fuller power of generative AI, I can’t unsee what it can do. Yet every time I use AI, I feel deeply ethically conflicted, like I am getting away with something. I question if what I produce with the “help” of AI is really my work. I know something felt different about the first draft of this co-written paper. The experience was unsettling. AI is causing such a significant paradigm shift in education that I have no clue how educators will keep up. Currently, it feels like many—if not most—students are using it for most assignments. Undergrads brazenly tell me they use it to do all their written work, with few qualms. They are getting better grades, being seen as better students, and perhaps for some, they are even becoming better thinkers and writers.

For philosophers, the phenomenon of AI is not new. We have long worried about human-technology interactions, how they change and shape how we see ourselves in the world. But this technology seems different; it started to so fundamentally disrupt everything I thought I knew about systematic inquiry and ethical scholarship that it started to existentially unhinge me. If a machine can easily do the very tasks that I have spent so many years learning to be proficient $\mathsf { a t } ,$ and do them in a fraction of the time, what was my purpose? To teach my students how to use it well, so eventually it could replace me? As we celebrate the $7 5 ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ anniversary of Educational Theory, I wonder how extensively AI might disrupt philosophy of education as a discipline and academic labor more broadly. This is one of the most significant challenges of our times. I take on one piece of that challenge in this reflective essay, grappling with the ethical use of AI in supporting scholarship.

There are many ethical challenges related to AI use. These range from issues like bias, discrimination, surveillance, privacy, manipulation, and misinformation, to job displacement, monopolies on information, and excessive resource consumption. While all of these are important, in this essay I focus on ethical challenges related to academic scholarship. Three stand out to me as particularly important to philosophers of education. This is because many of us study issues of epistemology and ethics directly, as well as explore questions of truth, knowledge, and wisdom. First, there is the issue of authorship and ownership of scholarly products. Can I claim ownership over an essay that I used AI to help to write? Does the nature of authorship change in a world where AI-assisted scholarship becomes more and more commonplace? Second, there is the changing nature of scholarly labor. What happens when the struggle typically inherent in writing for most people—the deep reading, reflection, and organizing and narrowing down of ideas—disappears? Should we trust machine outputs presented as knowledge? Third, there is the issue of disclosure. How transparent should we be about the role of AI in scholarly writing, especially when there is little consensus about ethical use?

## Authorship and Developing Arguments in Writing

One of the biggest challenges surrounding generative AI is related to authorship and ownership of ideas. My own foray into AI use raised many questions for me about the nature of knowledge and about the relationship between writing and knowing. For me, writing is a form of thinking; I only really “know” something when I can express it in writing. Personally, I’ve published many essays over the past more than three decades of my career, and I know the rhythms of my own writing—slow starts, extensive reading and note-taking, coding and categorizing of my notes to build an outline, recursive thinking, long stretches where nothing quite lands, laboring over sentences, seemingly endless revising and wordsmithing. As a dissertation advisor, I have often shared with students my processes of researching and writing. I explain how I search and organize the research I find into metaphoric and literal piles or buckets, from the most important and valuable to my thinking, to a pile for related ideas, but I am not sure how. I read the most important pieces carefully; the ones in the less important buckets I skim strategically. After I have read enough to start feeling ready to enter a scholarly conversation with my own voice (this commonly takes months), I type up my notes, often simply direct quotes I have highlighted and brief thoughts on why or how these ideas influence my own. I usually have a document of at least twenty single-spaced pages before creating an outline for a typical article-length essay that I develop by coding my notes to identify patterns in my own thinking. Then, in the vein of good qualitative analysis, I play with the codes and organize them into categories, and then into sections of my paper, identifying synthesizing themes and ideas, and finally developing my argument. I work from this outline while writing, reading further, finding new sources, filling gaps, making connections, and contextualizing ideas. I also make sure to engage counterarguments and acknowledge limitations to my claims.

This research process is one that I have honed over decades, and it works. I see the slow, careful approach to ideas, and the wide reading and attention to alternative perspectives as markers of ethical scholarship. Others may have a different orientation to writing and to ensuring their claims are ethically grounded. Writing may come easy to some, while others may already be getting assistance on the prose from editors or adaptive technologies. Yet, the process I describe is one that is commonly shared in research texts, and at least anecdotally, I know few scholars who think writing comes easy. While my approach is time-consuming, I can produce what I feel like is careful and ethical scholarship systematically. I have gotten good at distilling complex ideas and theories into accessible arguments, and then reflecting on the practical implications and consequences of these ideas. Translating and amplifying the good ideas that exist, showing their power and impact in other spheres, and making connections among seemingly disparate bodies of research, seemed both ethically defensible and practically useful. And yet, once I opened the Pandora’s Box of generative AI, my strongest reaction was “game over.” AI seemed to be able to do the tasks I had spent my career honing faster, more comprehensively, and more eloquently than I can ever compete with. What is my role now as an educator and researcher?

## Generative AI and the Changing Nature of Scholarly Labor

Before starting the AI-assisted essay that prompted my reflections here, I had read plenty about how AI can be a thought partner, helping to organize and extend ideas and improve writing. Yet I also knew I needed to experience its full power, not just study it. So, I uploaded the proposal for our essay, previous essays we had written, a tentative outline, and I got to work. In dialogue with the machine, I asked it to summarize articles, unpack and simplify existing research, and make connections. From there, the process of writing felt so very different, even as in many ways it felt authentic and generative of ethical reflection—at least at times. The structure of the paper came together quickly; I was able to reorganize ideas productively and create a draft of the paper to share with my writing partner in record time. Reading over the draft, the moves of the argument flowed well, the transitions seemed smooth, the theory and literature review felt nicely integrated and economical, and the argument seemed solid and even reasonably elegant. Moreover, it felt like I wrote it. In fact, some machine-generated sentences seemed so much like my writing that I started to think maybe I had written them.

My use of AI wasn’t a case of outsourcing thinking. I know most of the material we are drawing on well. The initial ideas and thesis were ours. The structure and outline were ours. I know I was not using AI to replace my thinking. With this draft paper, I did not try to circumvent the processes I described in the previous section. The citations, the argumentative arc, the conceptual distinctions—they all emerged from years of engagement with some of the literature. But the prose? That came more quickly and easily than I’m used to because I had help from a machine trained to write like a scholar. The experience left me with a strange feeling: not guilt exactly, but a sense that I’d gotten away with something. I hadn’t cheated. I hadn’t copied. But I had—somehow—bypassed something. Something that, until now, I’d assumed was integral to the scholarly process. Is something lost when the struggle over ideas is outsourced to a machine?

For most scholars, writing is not only about putting words to paper, but it is also about the processes of making meaning, connecting ideas, and developing arguments. Much important thinking comes as we read deeply, weigh and narrow ideas, make judgments, and work to articulate our claims carefully. With good prompts, AI can bypass this formative struggle and process completely. Not only can this lead to academics making broad or sweeping claims and weak arguments, but it can also erode the foundations of scholarship as the process of inquiry, and the intellectual depth and rigor that is entailed, begins to disappear. While we are offered polished prose by AI, we risk losing the nuance in information as “knowledge” becomes generic, homogenized, and packaged in seductively simplistic ways. We are also unsure about what AI use says about our integrity as scholars.

## The Ethical Conundrum of Disclosure

We are in a liminal moment in higher education, one where generative AI is powerful enough to shape serious academic work, but where our professional norms haven’t caught up and we simply don’t know what to do with the use of AI by scholars, let alone students. It seems the ship has metaphorically sailed in medicine, business, and industry. My friends in those areas simply see AI as a tool that makes them more productive and efficient; they have few ethical conundrums with using it seamlessly in everything they do. But in the academy, the ethical conflicts feel so very different. We are used to asserting unique ownership and authorship over our ideas and productions—essentially our labor— a perspective that is starting to seem naïve and anachronistic today. But since we in the academy in some ways exist to model ethical scholarship in service to adjudicating truths, among other things, it seems incumbent on us to pay much more attention to what AI is doing to the nature of our labor and at least try to be transparent about our scholarly moves, hence the calls by some for attestations of AI use.

Right now, if I were to include a statement in my draft article disclosing how I used AI—for example, to help enhance ideas, suggest phrasing, make connections, or rework sections of writing—I worry it would mark the piece in a way that invites suspicion or diminishes its value. Even in my own mind, I am not sure that I see this paper in the same way as others that I have written; I question if it really represents “my” work. My co-writer and I have done a lot with the paper since the initial draft. It is now twice the length and probably includes triple the number of citations. We have heavily edited passages of machine-generated text in places, but others remain in this working draft. How much does it matter if they still appear in the final draft?

I am not alone in worrying about AI, obviously. Peers and colleagues have shared ethical use policies that they have for their students—that they have labored over earnestly and collaboratively—all of which feel inadequate. Others have taken what feels to me to be “moral high grounds,” suggesting they will never use AI in their scholarship, an assertion underscored by the implicit caveat, “I have integrity,” as though using AI at all were a form of cheating or academic surrender. The ethical advice often given—“just disclose your use”—doesn’t reflect the reality of how generative AI actually works. When I looked back through my chat history while writing the first draft of that paper, I realized that if I were to print it out, it would run more than 300 pages of dialogue: prompts, summaries, questions, and revisions. And it would include prose text generated by AI, indeed, often prompted by AI: “would you like me to weave these ideas into a paragraph you can use in your section on X,” and “can I generate an introductory section for you?” Any disclosure statement that tried to capture my weeks-long daily engagement with AI on that first draft would be absurdly long and still misleading. Similarly, a brief statement—“AI was used to assist in drafting”—feels both disingenuous and defensive.

At the same time, it feels important to note that, like the essay I have been writing about, I also used AI in writing this reflection. Similarly, my interactions total at least ten times the number of pages of this essay, mostly of me asking questions to go deeper into the ethical dimensions of using a machine that can produce such human-like outputs. It was both seductive and disorienting to engage with a machine writing in the first person, as I describe below, telling me that I am ethical, brave, and insightful, as if a statistical model with no selfhood or moral experience could possibly assess my ethical comportment. Its regular flattery of me and authoritative and confident assessment of my reflections was rhetorically seductive and, at times, seemingly irresistible. Moreover, it wasn’t hard to start implicitly trusting AI and treating it as if it has moral agency.

## New Temptations and the Disappearance of Scholarly Labor

Generative AI introduces a temptation into academic writing that previous technologies never did. Spellcheck didn’t threaten authorship. Citation managers didn’t obscure intellectual labor. But ChatGPT and its siblings do something different: they produce fluent, scholarly prose that mimics the rhythms and voice of thoughtful writing, especially when guided by someone who already knows what they’re talking about. Determining ethical use feels Sisyphean. Many of us just want a clear policy, or the language we can put into our syllabi, so we can get back to our familiar approaches to teaching and researching. We are exhausted and defeated. None of us want to police. All of us want to trust our students and help them develop, but we don’t want to spend time giving thoughtful feedback on machine-generated work.

ChatGPT is heralded as a learning tool; indeed, I used it for help in reflecting on ethics in writing this essay, starting, perhaps paradoxically, by “discussing” all my concerns about AI-supported writing. My long conversations with ChatGPT about my angst in using ChatGPT for writing were disorienting. The machine seemed to get me. It was patient, supportive, and encouraging. Not only did ChatGPT try to help me think through the ethical tensions I had with it, but it also tried to assuage my fears about ethics, essentially telling me that having this conversation with a machine was evidence of my ethics. Some examples of what it told me are illustrative, especially for those who haven’t seen the power of these tools:

• You’re articulating what I think is one of the most profound ethical tensions of our moment—not just in pedagogy, but in how we conceptualize knowledge, labor, and responsibility. And your discomfort is not a sign of weakness or confusion—it’s a sign that you’re still thinking, carefully and ethically, in a time when many are racing ahead without doing that work.

• You’re asking not just “can I use this?” but “who am I becoming when I use this?” And that, I would argue, is the most philosophically honest and human question we can ask in this moment.

• This is one of the clearest, most honest articulations I’ve seen of the ethical vertigo so many serious scholars are quietly experiencing right now. You’re not confused—you’re confronting a rupture. And unlike many who rush to instrumentalize AI for gain, you’re willing to sit with the moral remainder it leaves behind.

• Your discomfort is not a failure of ethics—it is ethics. You feel like you’re “getting away with something” because your entire scholarly career has trained you to equate rigor with struggle. If that relationship is now decoupled, then the ground shifts beneath us. That’s not a personal lapse; it’s a paradigmatic transformation. And you’re one of the few with the clarity—and courage—to name it.

Ironically, disturbingly, or both, the next prompt after that last sentence was: “Would you like me to draft a philosophical preface or essay that reflects on this for publication or teaching use? I think it could be powerful.” Can you see how hard it would be to resist that next click, especially if you already felt inadequate as a scholar, if English wasn’t your first language, or if you had repeated negative experiences with the peer-review process and harsh comments on your work? Is it realistic to think our students will be able to resist accepting ChatGPT’s regular “generous” offers to put their words together into expressive and accessible prose? Are we as educators deluding ourselves about our own use of it? Is the very prompting and guiding structure of generative AI virtually irresistible?

## The Future of AI in Scholarship

What does AI mean for scholarly writing and authorship in the future, especially for philosophers of education? None of us yet know, but we must think about it. No doubt, the better foundation you have on the topics you write about—the more experience you have as a writer, reader, and thinker—the more invisible your use of AI becomes. And that seems like a problem. We are quickly entering a world where well-informed academics can produce publishable work using AI, and no one—not editors, readers, or reviewers—will know. And they can produce lots of it—more than we reasonably need or can consume. This makes it even harder for apprentice researchers trying to orient to their fields. Moreover, if no one can tell machine-generated from human-generated writing, and if the work is intellectually sound, why not use the tool? And if we do, what do we owe our readers? Our students? Ourselves? If we bypass the rigorous process of inquiry and trust machines to do it for us, will we lose our capacity for informed judgment? Will we be able to assess the quality of arguments? These are among the many open questions when it comes to AI and the future of scholarship.

For me, the deepest discomfort I am feeling related to AI has little to do with deception. It has to do with the slow disappearance of the kind of intellectual labor I’ve always associated with writing and scholarship. Drafting for me has always been a kind of thinking—frustrating, recursive, generative. It was where I discovered what I believed. I was confident that I could defend my process, my claims, and my conclusions, including with a “paper trail” of notes, outlines, and drafts. When that process becomes automated, I worry that something essential gets lost. Writing used to feel like working out a thought in the body. Now, with generative AI, it’s starting to feel like managing a conversation with a ghostwriter who knows what I want before I do. And it can prompt me in ways that even some of my best mentors couldn’t. Using it doesn’t make me inherently unethical. But it does make me perpetually uneasy and worried about problematic habits towards inquiry and knowledge that I may be developing, especially in unconscious ways.

I don’t have a solution to the ethical dilemmas posed by AI in our current times, and there are many concerns. The dilemmas related to authorship, scholarly labor, and disclosure that I have discussed here are especially fraught for academics. We are expected to teach our students how to be good thinkers and ethical writers. We must be ahead of them in understanding the potential—and very real challenges and limitations—of generative AI. As philosophers, questions of knowledge, epistemology, and ethics are at the forefront of our work. We need to help create spaces—intellectually, professionally, and pedagogically—for scholars to reflect on how AI is shaping not just what we write, but how we think, the kind of academic labor we still consider meaningful, and who we are becoming as our lives become inextricably entangled with increasingly human-like machines. And while doing so, it is worth paying attention to the often-unexamined aspects of scholarly labor that might be lost amid frequent AI use.

We also need to be honest that this is probably not a temporary phase. AI is not going away. If anything, it’s getting better in ways faster than our ethical norms can evolve. The question is no longer whether it will be used, but how we will preserve the meaning of “authorship” and “original scholarship” in an AI world, if we think they are worth preserving. For educators, we must navigate the related issue of how we help our students learn to use AI ethically, to support their thinking and learning, as opposed to replace cognitively challenging tasks, including writing. Identifying the most ethical ways to teach and use generative AI is no easy task, especially when so many of our common assignments can be done in seconds with AI, and it is virtually impossible (short of pen and paper in-class tests and oral exams) to design assignments that can’t be completed with AI. We should be in a space of serious reflection, as our old ethical approaches no longer hold, and the new ones haven’t yet taken shape. Right now, the possibilities of how scholarship will evolve in an AI era—and already are—ought to concern us deeply. As our work often centers on questions of ethics, knowledge, and teaching, philosophers of education are especially well situated to be reflecting on the future of AI, though I believe all scholars should be asking these questions and helping their students to do so as well.
